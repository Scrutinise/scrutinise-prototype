# §4.2A — What would actually be required to reach the case law

§4.2 concluded that neither repeal nor annulment touches a judgment's reasoning. The proposer's
natural next question is: **then what would?** This section answers it, because a scrutiny report
that identifies an obstacle without costing the route round it has done half a job.

**Parliament can reverse a judicial decision by statute. It is ordinary, lawful, and done regularly.**
There are three techniques, escalating in force and in political cost.

### 1. Restate the law prospectively

The mildest. Parliament states a different rule for the future; the judgment stands as a decision on
the old law. Finance Acts do this to tax decisions most years and nobody calls it constitutional.
**Sufficient where the objection is to the rule going forward.** Insufficient where the objection is
that a court has identified a right the programme wants gone, because a common-law right restated
around simply reappears in the next case on slightly different facts.

### 2. Reverse it retrospectively

**War Damage Act 1965** extinguished, retrospectively, the claim the House of Lords had upheld months
earlier in *Burmah Oil Co Ltd v Lord Advocate* [1965] AC 75. It worked. It remains the standard
demonstration that Parliament can undo a specific judgment including its result.

**The lesson is that the obstacle is not legal capacity.** Parliament plainly has the power. The
question is always what the exercise costs.

### 3. Deem the contested question closed

The strongest, and there is a very recent worked example directly on point.

**Safety of Rwanda (Asylum and Immigration) Act 2024**, responding to *R (AAA) v Secretary of State
for the Home Department* [2023] UKSC 42. Mechanically it did four things:

- **A deeming provision:** every decision-maker, courts and tribunals included, must conclusively
  treat Rwanda as a safe country — irrespective of contrary evidence.
- **Partial disapplication of the Human Rights Act**, barring courts from considering the safety
  question, refoulement risk, or compliance with international obligations.
- **A direction to courts** not to have regard to Strasbourg interim measures, with ministerial
  discretion to disregard them.
- **A narrow exception** for compelling evidence about a person's own particular circumstances,
  expressly excluding the general safety argument.

⚠ **Note what it did not do: it did not annul the judgment.** It left *AAA* standing and legislated
around its effect. That is the technique that is actually available.

## What this means for the programme, concretely

To displace a common-law holding — *UNISON* being the clearest — a Bill would have to do four things,
and each carries a cost the report should state plainly.

| Requirement | Why | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Name the proposition being displaced** | The principle of legality in *ex p Simms* means general or ambiguous words cannot override a fundamental right. Only express words reach it. | The clause must say what it is doing, in terms |
| **State the replacement rule** | A displaced right leaves a vacuum a court will fill | Somebody must decide what replaces it |
| **Address retrospectivity expressly** | Interpretation Act 1978 s.16 preserves accrued rights unless a contrary intention appears | Retrospective effect must be argued for, not assumed |
| **Accept a declaration of incompatibility remains available** | The Rwanda Act retained it deliberately | The court still gets to say so, publicly |

⚠⚠ **The real price is not drafting difficulty. It is that clarity is unavoidable.**

A common-law constitutional right cannot be displaced quietly. Somebody has to write — and defend at
the despatch box — a clause saying in terms that access to the courts may be restricted
notwithstanding the right the Supreme Court identified in *UNISON*. The drafting is not hard. The
sentence is hard.

**And the Rwanda Act demonstrates the second cost: deeming did not end the litigation, it relocated
it.** Challenges moved to individual circumstances, to interim measures, and to the declaration of
incompatibility route the Act had left open. A programme expecting legislation to close a question
should expect instead to change where it is argued.

## So how much legislation does the programme actually need?

Stated as a ladder, because each rung reaches something the one below does not:

1. **Repeal the Human Rights Act.** Removes the statutory scheme. ⚠ Leaves the common law intact —
   *UNISON*, *Osborn*, *Kennedy* all survive untouched.
2. **Denounce the Convention (Article 58).** Removes the treaty obligation and the Strasbourg route.
   ⚠ Still leaves the common law, and does not by itself alter domestic law at all.
3. **A restatement Act naming and displacing specific common-law propositions.** This is the rung
   that actually reaches what the proposer has identified. ⚠ **It requires the propositions to be
   enumerated — and nobody has enumerated them.**
4. **Consequential amendment of the devolution statutes** — see §4.4, which is not optional.

**Point 3 is the finding.** The programme's central mechanism requires a list that does not exist:
which common-law holdings are to be displaced, in which cases, on what facts. Producing that list is
a discrete, bounded piece of work. It is also precisely the kind of work this platform exists to do,
and we would say so as a statement of what is available rather than as a proposal.

# §4.3 — The historical analogy, tested

The proposer invokes Henry VIII's annulments as the model. Testing his precedent rigorously is the
most respectful thing this document can do, and the test produces a result that is useful to him.

## What the statutes mechanically did

**Ecclesiastical Appeals Act 1532 (24 Hen VIII c.12)**, the Act in Restraint of Appeals, is the
central instrument. Its famous preamble declares the realm of England an empire, sufficient to itself
and free of foreign jurisdiction. Its operative effect was narrower and more precise than the
preamble suggests: **it prohibited appeals to Rome and vested final determination in the domestic
courts.** The Act of Supremacy 1534 completed the transfer of jurisdictional authority.

⚠⚠ **These were jurisdictional statutes, and they operated prospectively.** They stopped appeals
going out; they did not unmake determinations Rome had already made. The machinery redirected a
route of appeal. It did not annul a body of decided law.

The one genuinely annulling instrument was the **First Act of Succession 1534**, declaring the
marriage to Catherine void from the beginning. But that operated on a **status** — a marriage is a
legal state which a statute can deem never to have existed. **A body of judicial reasoning is not a
status.** Precedent cannot be deemed away, because it is a proposition of law rather than a fact
about the world.

## What follows

**The proposer's own precedent supports the jurisdictional route rather than the annulment route.**
What Henry VIII's statutes actually did — remove a foreign appellate jurisdiction and vest finality
domestically — maps closely onto denouncing the Convention and ending the Strasbourg route. It maps
badly onto annulling forty years of domestic case law, which is not what those statutes attempted.

That is a finding in his favour on the mechanism and against his reading of it, and both halves
should be stated in the same voice.

## ⚠ And a false friend the programme must not walk into

In modern usage a **"Henry VIII clause"** means something quite different: a provision letting a
minister amend or repeal primary legislation by statutory instrument. The name comes from the
Statute of Proclamations 1539.

**If the programme reaches for Henry VIII powers to deliver repeal at speed, it will be using
executive power to reduce Parliament's role — the exact opposite of its stated aim of restoring
parliamentary sovereignty.** This is an interlock problem, not a drafting one, and it belongs in
§4.7. A programme whose purpose is to return power to Parliament cannot be delivered by a mechanism
that takes power from Parliament without the contradiction being visible on the face of the Bill.

# §4.4 — The devolution wall

**Convention compliance is written into the devolution statutes themselves, and repealing the Human
Rights Act does not touch them.**

| Statute | Provision | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Scotland Act 1998 | s.29(2)(d) | An Act of the Scottish Parliament is not law so far as incompatible with Convention rights |
| Government of Wales Act 2006 | ⚠ s.108A(2)(e) — **UNVERIFIED, see below** | The same for an Act of Senedd Cymru |
| Northern Ireland Act 1998 | s.6(2)(c) | The same for an Act of the Assembly |

⚠⚠ **The Welsh citation is in doubt and is out for verification.** I wrote s.108A(2)(e) from
knowledge. The Prosperity Institute's roadmap cites **Government of Wales Act 1998 s.94** and
**GoWA 2006 s.80** for the same purpose. One of us is wrong, or they are doing different work. CCW-B12
settles it against the corpus. **Nothing in this table prints until that comes back.** The Scottish
citation is separately corroborated; the Welsh one is not.

⚠⚠ **A definitional gap nobody appears to have flagged.** The devolution statutes do not define
"the Convention rights" independently — Scotland Act 1998 s.126(1) takes the meaning **from the
Human Rights Act**. Repeal the Act without amending the devolution statutes and those provisions
refer to a definition that no longer exists.

**This must be resolved and there is no silent option.** Either the definition is re-enacted inside
each devolution statute — leaving Convention compliance binding on the devolved legislatures after
the Act is gone, which is unlikely to be the intention — or each devolution statute is amended, which
engages the Sewel convention in three legislatures at once.

**On Sewel:** the courts held in *R (Miller) v Secretary of State for Exiting the European Union*
[2017] UKSC 5 that the convention is not justiciable. **So Westminster may legislate without
consent.** The obstacle is political and constitutional, not legal — and the working assumption
should be that consent is refused in Edinburgh and Cardiff, and that the Northern Ireland position
is different again because it rests on a treaty (§4.5).

**The consequence to state plainly:** repealing the Human Rights Act is not a single-statute
operation. It is a repeal plus three consequential amendments, at least two of which will be refused
consent, and one of which touches an international agreement.

# §4.5 — Northern Ireland

⚠ **NOT DRAFTED. Deliberately.**

This section requires the three published positions — Prosperity Institute (Braverman/Dampier),
Policy Exchange (Casey/Ekins/Laws), and the Committee on the Administration of Justice — to be set
out **fairly, from their own texts, with their own authorities**, and not adjudicated between.

I have not read the three papers. Characterising them from memory would produce exactly the failure
this report is built to avoid: a confident, fluent account of somebody's position that they would not
recognise. Given that one of the three is published by an organisation the proposer works with
directly, an inaccurate summary would be noticed immediately and would discredit the sections around
it.

**What the section needs, and it is a morning's work once the papers are in hand:**

1. Each position stated in its own terms, with its authorities.
2. **What each would need to be true** — the factual or legal premise on which it rests.
3. What turns on which is right: specifically, whether Convention rights can be removed in Northern
   Ireland without breaching the Belfast/Good Friday Agreement, and what Windsor Framework Article 2's
   non-diminution obligation adds.
4. No adjudication. State what follows on each view and stop.

**Structural point that can be stated now:** the Northern Ireland gate is separate from the
devolution gate because the obligation is **bilateral treaty**, not domestic settlement. Sewel can be
overridden; a treaty with another sovereign state cannot be, at least not without consequences that
belong in §4.6 and the counterparty-response analysis.
