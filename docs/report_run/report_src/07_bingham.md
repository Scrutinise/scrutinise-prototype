# Part 2.8 · What happened between 1997 and 2010

## 2.8 What actually happened between 1997 and 2010

**The programme is a proposal to undo something. This section sets out what that something was, what the people who built it said they were doing, and whether they intended the result.**

⚠ **A note on sourcing.** This section draws on published books, official papers and parliamentary records. Because it deals with intellectual history rather than the statute book, much of it comes from named published work rather than from our own search of the law. **Every item carries a mark:**

- **✔ checked against the source**
- **● named independently by two or more of our four research passes, not yet checked**
- **○ named once only, not yet checked — treat as a lead, not a citation**

---

### What was built

Six things, over thirteen years, by different hands and for different stated reasons.

| Year | What | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| 1998 | **Human Rights Act** | Made Convention rights enforceable in UK courts, with a deliberately weak remedy — a declaration of incompatibility that changes nothing legally |
| 1998–99 | **Devolution statutes** | Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland — each with Convention rights written into the limits of what the devolved bodies may do |
| 2005 | **Constitutional Reform Act** | Took the Lord Chancellor out of the judiciary, created the Judicial Appointments Commission, and moved the final court out of the House of Lords |
| 2009 | **Coroners and Justice Act** | Created the Sentencing Council as an independent body |
| 2010 | **Constitutional Reform and Governance Act, Part 1** | Put the civil service on a statutory footing — the Civil Service Code, merit recruitment, and the Civil Service Commission |
| 2010 | **Equality Act** | Consolidated earlier discrimination law and added the public sector equality duty |

⚠ **They were not one project.** Different departments, different ministers, different arguments, spread over more than a decade. **Treating them as a single design is the first thing a critic will attack**, and the programme's own case is stronger if it argues that the *effect* was cumulative rather than that the *intent* was coordinated.

---

### What the architects said they were doing

**This is where the record is clearest, and it does not say what the programme's stronger version assumes.**

**On rights.** The 1997 white paper was called *Rights Brought Home* ●. Its stated purpose was to let people enforce Convention rights in British courts instead of spending years and large sums going to Strasbourg. The Convention was presented as a British-drafted instrument the UK had helped write.

**⚠⚠ And the sovereignty question was not overlooked — it was the central design problem.** Jack Straw's Second Reading speech on 16 February 1998 ● is the primary source. He said the most fundamental question was how to incorporate the Convention while *strengthening* rather than undermining parliamentary sovereignty, expressly rejected giving courts power to set aside Acts of Parliament, and said:

> "The sovereignty of Parliament must be paramount."

⚠ *Named by two independent passes with that quotation. It is Hansard-checkable and should be checked before it is quoted in a printed document — but if it holds, it is decisive for what follows.*

**That is not retrospective justification. It is the contemporaneous ministerial explanation of the architecture** — and the declaration of incompatibility, which deliberately changes nothing, is the mechanism that was chosen precisely to preserve Parliament's last word.

**On the courts.** The 2005 reforms were announced in a June 2003 reshuffle, not after a royal commission ●. **That origin is itself part of the argument** and the programme should use it: a change to the highest court in the land, made without a commission, without a green paper, and announced alongside a ministerial reshuffle.

Lord Bingham argued publicly for it in *The Times* in June 2003 ●, on the ground that judges sitting in the legislature was an anomaly. **What he wanted was explicitly not the American model** — a court that could not strike down Acts of Parliament.

⚠ **And Lord Irvine, the Lord Chancellor removed by the same reshuffle, opposed both the abolition of his office and the creation of the court** ●, and later said the decision was taken without proper advice. **The strongest contemporaneous criticism of the 2005 Act came from inside the government that made it.**

**On the civil service.** The 2010 Act completed a debate running since the 1990s. **Its stated aim was to lock existing Northcote-Trevelyan values into statute so that a future government could not quietly unpick them by Order in Council** ●.

⚠ **Note what that means for the programme's sixth measure.** On this account the 2010 Act **codified** rather than created. Repealing it returns the position to what it was in 2009 — which was not a patronage system.

---

### Bingham himself, and why he is misread by both sides

**Lord Bingham was Master of the Rolls, Lord Chief Justice and Senior Law Lord — the only person to have held all three offices.** *The Rule of Law* (2010) ● is the book the programme is arguing against, and David has written about it directly, in *Bingham's failed revolution*, *The Critic*, March 2025 ✔.

**Bingham's conception is deliberately a "thick" one:** not merely that government follows the rules it enacts, but that fundamental rights are protected, that officials act lawfully, that courts adjudicate fairly — and, in his eighth principle, **that the state complies with its international legal obligations as with its national law.**

⚠⚠ **That eighth principle is the crux. It is the single most concise statement of the position the programme attacks**, and it is where the argument should be joined.

**But there is a qualification both sides routinely lose.** Bingham did not conclude that Parliament had ceased to be sovereign. His book contains an extended treatment of the rule of law and parliamentary sovereignty, and in a 2008 journal article ● he rejected the suggestion — made by some judges in the *Jackson* case — that courts might one day refuse to apply an Act of Parliament.

**His thesis is that a sovereign Parliament *ought* to legislate consistently with deeper principles. It is not that judges acquire power to stop it.**

⚠ **A well-informed critic will use Bingham against the programme's stronger version**, and a proponent who has treated him as the patron saint of judicial supremacy will be caught out. **The accurate criticism of Bingham is not that he claimed too much power for courts. It is that his eighth principle makes international obligation part of the rule of law itself — which is a much more interesting argument, and a harder one.**

---

### ⚠⚠ Was "government by unaccountable experts" intended?

**The documentary answer is: partly yes as to independence, no as to supremacy — and the distinction is what the whole dispute turns on.**

**These were deliberate design objectives**, stated at the time:

- Judges should not owe appointment or promotion to political patronage
- Civil servants should be recruited on merit and serve successive governments
- Rights should constrain public authorities regardless of electoral popularity
- Equality considerations should happen before decisions, not only through later claims

**These were not:** transferring ultimate legislative authority to judges. The refusal to permit strike-down was designed to prevent exactly that, and it is on the record.

**So the weak version of the programme's historical claim is well supported and the strong version is not.**

⚠⚠ **And the serious version of the case is better than either, because it does not depend on intent at all:**

> You need not formally give judges sovereignty for legal constraint to displace political choice. If Parliament legislates through broad concepts — *proportionality*, *necessity*, *reasonable justification*, *due regard* — and courts progressively determine what those words mean, then substantial political decisions migrate into adjudication, **even though Parliament technically retains the power to reverse any of it.**

**That proposition does not require anyone to have intended anything.** It is a claim about what happens when vague standards are put into statute, and it is testable against the record measure by measure.

**It is also the version this document's own findings support.** Part 4.2 shows exactly that mechanism at work: a duty to have "due regard", whose content only a court can settle after the fact.

⚠ **Recommendation: this is the strongest available framing of the programme's historical case, and it is not ours — it is what all four of our independent research passes said the serious literature actually argues.** It is offered as an option, not urged.

---

### What was done about it before, and what happened

**The programme is not starting a new movement. Since about 2010 governments and Parliament have repeatedly tried to restore political discretion, and the results are instructive.**

| Attempt | Outcome |
|---|---|
| **Commission on a Bill of Rights**, 2012 ● | Split. Majority favoured a domestic bill of rights **while staying in the Convention**; two members dissented |
| **Independent Review of Administrative Law** (Lord Faulks), 2021 ● | Commissioned to find judicial overreach. **Found none systemic.** Recommended modest procedural change |
| **Judicial Review and Courts Act 2022** ✔ | Enacted. Modest — but it contains the one court-exclusion clause that worked. See Part 2.2 |
| **Independent Human Rights Act Review** (Sir Peter Gross), 2021 ● | Government-commissioned, retired appeal judge in the chair, a former First Parliamentary Counsel on the panel. **Recommended keeping the Act** |
| **Bill of Rights Bill 2022** ✔ | The only real legislative attempt. Abandoned 2023 without a second reading |
| **Retained EU Law (Revocation and Reform) Act 2023** ● | Removed the supremacy of EU law in domestic interpretation. ⚠ **Its sunset clause — the model the Prosperity Institute's Case Law Review Commission is drawn from — was abandoned before Royal Assent** |
| **Safety of Rwanda (Asylum and Immigration) Act 2024** ● | ⚠⚠ **The closest thing to the programme's method actually enacted.** Disapplied specific sections of the Human Rights Act for a defined class of decision, and directed courts to treat a factual question as settled |
| **Farage ten-minute-rule motion**, Oct 2025 ✔ | Ayes 96, Noes 154 |
| **Wolfson advice and Conservative policy**, 2025 ✔ | Withdrawal adopted as party policy |
| **Wood and Morrissey Bills**, 2026 ✔ | Before the Commons now — see Part 2.6 |

⚠⚠ **The Safety of Rwanda Act is the most important row in this table and the programme should study it closely.** It is the method — disapply named provisions for a named purpose — executed at small scale. **What it cost politically, how the courts handled it, and whether it achieved its object are the best available evidence about the programme's own method**, and nobody appears to have used it that way.

**And the pattern across the table is worth stating plainly: every broad attempt failed, and every narrow one succeeded.** That is the same finding as Part 2.2, arrived at from a completely different direction.

# Part 2.9 · Democracy and international obligation

## 2.9 The real argument: democracy, courts, and international obligation

**All four of our independent research passes reframed the question the same way, without being asked to.** They said the serious literature is not organised around "British law versus European law". It is organised around a different distinction, and getting it right changes who the programme's allies are.

---

### The distinction that actually runs through the literature

**Political constitutionalism versus legal constitutionalism.** It traces to a 1979 lecture by J. A. G. Griffith called *The Political Constitution* ●.

| | **The political constitution** | **The legal constitution** |
|---|---|---|
| Where authority sits | The elected majority in the Commons | Enforceable rights, treaties and statutory duties |
| How power is checked | Elections, ministerial responsibility, public argument | Judicial review, regulators, tribunals, international courts |
| What judges do | Interpret what Parliament meant; develop the common law step by step | Test executive decisions against rights and equality standards |
| What democracy means | **Procedural** — what the majority chooses through elected representatives is legitimate | **Substantive** — a real democracy requires legal constraints protecting rights and minorities |

⚠⚠ **Why this matters more than it looks.** On the "British versus European" framing, the programme is making a nationalist argument, and it is vulnerable to a single book — see below. **On the political-versus-legal framing, it joins a serious argument with serious people on both sides, including people who are not on its political side at all.**

---

### ⚠⚠ The book that breaks the "British versus foreign" framing

**Sir John Laws, *The Common Law Constitution* (2014) ●** — one of the most important British public-law judges of his generation.

**His argument is that the common law itself supplies constitutional principle** and itself polices the boundary between political power and legal constraint. **Strong judicial constraint is not an import. It grew here.**

⚠ **This is the single most awkward item in the literature for the programme's stronger version**, because it destroys the binary:

> **British common law = parliamentary government · European rights law = judicial government**

**If Laws is right, repealing the Human Rights Act and leaving the Convention removes the imported layer and leaves the domestic one standing** — which is precisely what Part 2.1 finds from the case law, arrived at independently.

**The programme needs an answer to this, and the answer cannot be that judicial power is foreign.**

---

### Who is on each side

⚠ *Names and works below are drawn from our research passes. Those marked ● were named independently by two or more; ○ by one only. **None has been read by us in full.** They are a reading list, not authority.*

**Arguing that political choice has been displaced by legal process**

| Who | Work |
|---|---|
| **Lord Sumption** ✔ | *Trials of the State* / 2019 Reith Lectures. **The most formidable statement of the case, made from the bench** |
| **Lord Hoffmann** ● | *The Universality of Human Rights*, 2009 lecture. Objects to Strasbourg's claim to set uniform European standards |
| **Professor Richard Ekins** ● | Policy Exchange's Judicial Power Project, 2015 onwards |
| **Sir Stephen Laws** ● | Former First Parliamentary Counsel. ⚠ **Note: he sat on the 2021 review that recommended keeping the Human Rights Act** |
| **Professor Martin Loughlin** ● | *Against Constitutionalism* (Harvard, 2022). ⚠ **A critique from the left** |
| **Professor Adam Tomkins** ● | *Our Republican Constitution* (2005). Executive should answer politically through Parliament, not legally through courts |
| **Professor Richard Bellamy** ○ | *Defending the Political Constitution* (2026). ⚠ Named once. If real, it is the most directly relevant recent book |
| **Ran Hirschl** ○ | *Towards Juristocracy* (2004). Argues this is a general Western pattern, not a British plot |
| **David Starkey** ✔ | *Bingham's failed revolution*, *The Critic*, March 2025 |

**Arguing that the settlement works and is not anti-democratic**

| Who | Work |
|---|---|
| **Lord Bingham** ● | *The Rule of Law* (2010). The eighth principle is the crux |
| **Professor Aileen Kavanagh** ● | *The Collaborative Constitution* (2023). **Named by all four passes.** Argues courts, Parliament and the executive work in partnership rather than competing for supremacy |
| **Professor Sir Vernon Bogdanor** ● | *The New British Constitution* (2009). The standard account that a constitutional revolution happened without being announced |
| **Professor Conor Gearty** ● | *On Fantasy Island* (2016). Argues the "foreign imposition" story is a political construction |
| **Lady Hale, Lord Reed, Lord Neuberger** ● | Extra-judicial lectures denying a US-style political role for the court |
| **Lord Hermer KC** ● | Attorney General. 2024 Bingham Lecture, treating "rights versus democracy" as a false choice |

---

### ⚠⚠ The ally the programme has not noticed

**Griffith, Tomkins, Loughlin and — if it exists — Bellamy make substantially the same diagnosis as the programme, from the left.** Their argument is that political choices have migrated to legal and expert bodies, and that this insulates decisions from electorates.

**They disagree about the remedy, not the diagnosis.**

One named example: **Peter Ramsay, writing in April 2025** ○, accepts the diagnosis and rejects the remedy — arguing that much of this legislation must go for government to be democratically accountable, while treating the Great Repeal as the dead end of the Thatcherite right.

⚠ **That is a more useful opponent than a defender of the status quo**, and it is the strongest available evidence that the programme's diagnosis is not a partisan position. **Proponents rarely read this literature. It is their best argument that the case is not party-political.**

---

### What a well-informed critic will cite that a proponent may not have read

**This is the shelf to have confronted before publishing legislation.** It is not the other side's slogans; it is the material that makes the strongest case that these statutes do something other than the repeal case says.

1. **The full 2021 Human Rights Act review** ●, not the press coverage. Government-commissioned, appeal judge in the chair, a former First Parliamentary Counsel on the panel — and it recommended keeping the Act.
2. **The 2021 administrative law review** ● — a second sceptic-chaired government review that looked for overreach and found the problem smaller than alleged.
3. **Kavanagh, *The Collaborative Constitution*** ● — the current academic answer to "judges versus democracy", which rejects the premise rather than the conclusion.
4. **Sir John Laws, *The Common Law Constitution*** ● — for the reason set out above.
5. **The Belfast/Good Friday Agreement texts themselves**, and the competing Northern Ireland legal opinions. See Part 2.4.
6. **Bingham read properly** ● — chapter on parliamentary sovereignty, with the 2008 article. He is not the patron saint of judicial supremacy and a critic will say so.
7. **The devolution statutes and the Sewel convention** ● — a Westminster-only programme collides with three of them.
8. **The pre-1998 common-law rights cases** ● — *Simms*, *Witham*, *Leech*, *Pierson*. ⚠ **If you repeal the Human Rights Act you do not return to a blank common law. You return to one that senior judges had already said contains fundamental rights.** David has himself made this point; these cases are what make it precise.
9. **Comparative evidence on impartial civil services** ● — the claim that statutory impartiality makes a state ungovernable is an empirical claim, and the comparative work runs the other way.
10. **The actual text of section 149** — "due regard", not "achieve equality of outcome". ⚠ **Much of the repeal case is a case against institutional practice that may not be required by the section**, and a critic will insist on that distinction before arguing that practice can be changed by guidance rather than repeal.

---

### And the development that cuts against the central premise

**A load-bearing premise of the programme is that the Convention system cannot be reformed, so only leaving works.**

**In May 2026 all 46 Council of Europe member states adopted a political declaration at Chișinău on the Convention and migration** ✔ — subsidiarity, the margin of appreciation, Articles 3 and 8 in expulsion cases, and mass arrivals.

⚠ **Both halves of this must print.** It is not binding, does not amend the Convention, and does not bind the Strasbourg court — so whether it changes any outcome is an open empirical question. **But 46 states have just done, by declaration, the thing the premise says cannot be done, on precisely the ground where the pressure is greatest.**

**That is a contested development, not a refutation. It is also the question the programme is most likely to be asked in public and least prepared for.**
