## 4.2.0 — The claim, confirmed by the Shadow Attorney General

⚠ **Verified against the source document, not a summary.** Lord Wolfson KC, *ECHR Review: Advice to
the Leader of the Conservative Party*, 2 October 2025, **paragraph 345, pages 163–164**:

> "It is, however, clear that leaving the ECHR and repealing the HRA would not amount to a 'factory
> reset' of UK law to its state pre-ECHR and pre-HRA; to achieve anything like that would involve a
> complicated legislative programme aimed at rolling back case law and relevant legislative
> provisions."

**That is the proposer's central mechanism, reached independently, by the Shadow Attorney General,
in advice to the Leader of the Opposition.** He is right that repeal alone does not restore the
prior position — and the most authoritative legal analysis of the question agrees with him.

The same paragraph gives the reason: before and after the Act, Convention rights have influenced the
development of the common law, and Wolfson describes that influence as so pervasive that **it would
be impossible to give a comprehensive account** of the parts of domestic law affected.

⚠⚠ **Read that second sentence carefully, because it cuts both ways.** It confirms the diagnosis and
simultaneously sets a limit on the remedy: if no comprehensive account of the affected law can be
given, then no programme can claim to have rolled all of it back. Any enumeration — including one
this platform could produce — is necessarily partial, and must say so. **A declared partial list is
useful. An undeclared one would be the defect this whole document exists to avoid.**

Wolfson also names the instrument, and it is not annulment: *a complicated legislative programme
aimed at rolling back case law and relevant legislative provisions*. §4.2A sets out what such a
programme would mechanically require.

### Two supporting findings from the same passage

**The authorities converge.** At paragraph 344 Wolfson relies on Lord Hoffmann in *ex p Simms* for
the proposition that the common law often mirrors the Convention rights — "or rather the ECHR
reflects the common law". At 346 he quotes Lord Reed in *Osborn* (the Act did not supersede the
protection of human rights under the common law) and Lord Mance in *Kennedy* (the natural starting
point in any dispute is domestic law). Those are the same three authorities this section reached
independently before the report was consulted.

⚠ **And a finding the proposer will not have anticipated: the influence ran one way.** At paragraph
347.1 Wolfson observes that rights derived from the Convention have *expanded* rather than reduced
common-law protection — because where the common law already went further, there was no basis for
narrowing it by reference to the Convention. **Leaving the Convention therefore cannot mechanically
shrink the common law back.** The ratchet has no reverse gear, which is precisely why the roll-back
has to be legislated rather than merely permitted to happen.

## The proposer's claim, stated as a testable proposition

Repeal of the Human Rights Act 1998 is insufficient, because the courts have absorbed Convention
principles into the common law. Repeal would therefore leave the substance in place, and something
stronger — annulment, declaring the provisions never to have had effect — is required instead.

Three questions follow, and they have different answers.

---

## 4.2.1 Is the absorption claim correct?

**Substantially yes — and the evidence is stronger than the proposer has himself argued.** §4.2.0
gives the authoritative confirmation; this section gives the underlying case law, reached
independently of it.

Two lines of authority carry it, and they run in opposite directions from 1998.

**Before the Act, the common law already protected several of the rights in question.** Natural
justice in *Ridge v Baldwin* [1964] AC 40. Access to the courts in *R v Secretary of State for the
Home Department, ex p Leech* [1994] QB 198 and *ex p Witham* [1998] QB 575. Freedom of expression in
*Derbyshire County Council v Times Newspapers Ltd* [1993] AC 534, decided expressly on common-law
grounds. And the principle of legality in *R v Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex p
Simms* [2000] 2 AC 115, where Lord Hoffmann held that fundamental rights cannot be overridden by
general or ambiguous words — a canon of construction owing nothing to the statute.

**Since 2013 the Supreme Court has repeatedly held that the common law, not the Convention, is where
a rights question starts.** *Osborn v Parole Board* [2013] UKSC 61; *Kennedy v Charity Commission*
[2014] UKSC 20; *A v BBC* [2014] UKSC 25; *Pham v Secretary of State for the Home Department*
[2015] UKSC 19.

**The strongest single authority is *R (UNISON) v Lord Chancellor* [2017] UKSC 51.** Employment
tribunal fees were quashed on the **common-law constitutional right of access to justice**. The
Convention analysis was secondary. Nothing in the reasoning depends on the Human Rights Act, and the
decision would stand unaltered if the Act had never been passed.

**So repeal would not return the law to 1997.** It would leave a developed body of common law which
grew partly under the Convention's influence and now rests on its own footing.

⚠ **But the claim is not correct without limit, and the report should say so.** The courts have also
held that the common law is not a mirror of the Convention: in *Moohan v Lord Advocate* [2014] UKSC
67 the Supreme Court declined to find a common-law right to vote. **The absorption is asymmetric** —
robust for access to justice, natural justice and legality; thin or absent elsewhere. A programme
built on the assumption that the common law now replicates the Convention across the board would be
built on a misreading in the proposer's favour.

---

## 4.2.2 Would annulment achieve what he wants?

**No — and this is the finding most likely to be unwelcome.**

Deeming a statute never to have had effect reaches the statute. **It does not unmake the reasoning
of cases decided while it was in force.** A judgment's *ratio* is a proposition of law; where a court
has held that *the common law* protects a right, that holding was never dependent on the statute and
so survives its removal. *UNISON* is the demonstration: annul the Human Rights Act and *UNISON*
still stands, because it was not decided under it.

Repeal alone is already less destructive than is often assumed — Interpretation Act 1978 s.16
preserves things previously done and rights already accrued unless a contrary intention appears — but
that is a point about transactions, not about precedent. **Precedent is not undone by either device.**

**The proposer's diagnosis and his remedy do not meet.** He is right that repeal leaves the judicial
reasoning in place. Annulment leaves it in place too. To reach the case law, Parliament would have to
legislate against the propositions themselves — which is a different, larger and more contentious
exercise than annulling an Act, and would have to name what it is displacing.

---

## 4.2.3 What have similar devices actually achieved?

**Here the record turns back in his favour, and it contains the most useful thing in this section.**

The orthodoxy is that ouster clauses fail. *Anisminic Ltd v Foreign Compensation Commission* [1969]
2 AC 147 read a total ouster down to nothing by treating a decision infected by error of law as a
nullity, and so not a "determination" at all. *R (Privacy International) v Investigatory Powers
Tribunal* [2019] UKSC 22 defeated a modern one on comparable reasoning.

⚠⚠ **But the orthodoxy is now out of date. In 2022 Parliament drafted an ouster that worked.**

Section 2 of the Judicial Review and Courts Act 2022 inserted s.11A into the Tribunals, Courts and
Enforcement Act 2007, removing judicial review of Upper Tribunal permission decisions — the *Cart*
jurisdiction. It was upheld in *R (Oceana) v Upper Tribunal* [2023] EWHC 791 (Admin) and by the Court
of Appeal in *R (LA (Albania)) v Upper Tribunal* [2023] EWCA Civ 1337.

**Why it survived, and this is the transferable part:**

1. **It was partial, not total.** Review was preserved for jurisdictional error in the pre-*Anisminic*
   sense, for fundamental breach of natural justice, for an improperly constituted tribunal and for
   bad faith. A court asked to accept a complete exclusion refuses; a court asked to accept a
   bounded one need not.
2. **It closed the nullity route expressly.** Section 11A(7) brings *purported* decisions within the
   ouster — the precise device *Anisminic* used to get round the clause of 1950.
3. **It restored a standard the courts had themselves preferred**, rather than inventing one.
4. **The body protected was an expert judicial one**, not an executive decision-maker.

Dingemans LJ's reasoning rests on giving effect to Parliament's clear words, on the footing that no
one, a court included, is above the law.

**The lesson for the programme is precise and it is not the one the proposer has drawn.** The
successful device was narrow, surgical, and drafted around the known failure mode. The unsuccessful
ones were broad and declaratory. Annulment, as described in the source material, is a broad
declaratory device.

---

## What this section establishes, in one paragraph

The proposer's central instinct is sound and better supported than his own account of it: repealing
the Human Rights Act would not restore the pre-1998 position, because the courts now protect several
of these rights as common law. His chosen remedy does not follow from it — annulment reaches
statutes, and the problem he has identified lives in case law, which neither repeal nor annulment
touches. But Parliament has, once and recently, restricted judicial review in a way the courts
upheld, and the drafting features that made it survive are identifiable and reusable. **The
programme's difficulty is not that its aim is unachievable. It is that the instrument named for it
is aimed at the wrong target.**

## Open questions for the proposer

1. Is the intention to displace the *statute*, or the *case law* decided under it? These require
   different instruments and only the second reaches the absorption problem you identify.
2. Where the common law protects a right independently — access to justice being the clearest —
   is displacement intended? That is a decision about the common law itself, not about Blair-era
   legislation.
3. *Moohan* shows the absorption is uneven. Which rights does the programme assume are absorbed, and
   has that assumption been tested right by right?
4. Given that the one ouster clause to survive judicial scrutiny was narrow and surgical, is a broad
   annulment device still the preferred route?
