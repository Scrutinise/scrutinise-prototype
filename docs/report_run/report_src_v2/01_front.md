# What this is

## What this is

**This document takes a political programme and works out what it would take to actually do it.**

That is the whole idea behind Scrutinise. Someone says what they want changed. Scrutinise works through what would have to happen — which laws, which bodies, which decisions, which obstacles — and hands it back with the questions only the proposers can answer.

This is the first time Scrutinise has been pointed at a programme this size.

### The programme is twelve measures, not one

Most of the public discussion treats "the Starkey thesis" as a single idea. It is not. It is at least twelve separate changes, each with its own law, its own opposition and its own difficulty. Some are simple. Some have never been attempted by anyone. Two are already before Parliament in narrower form.

**Part 1 sets out all twelve.** Parts 2 and 3 cover what applies across the whole programme. Part 4 takes two of them all the way down, so you can see the depth available and multiply it by twelve. Part 5 covers the rest more briefly.

### What is in it

**Across the whole programme**

- The register — every change, in David's own words, with the law that would have to change, and why
- Whether repeal alone achieves what is intended, tested against the courts and against two published legal analyses
- Four different plans proposed within a year by people on the same side of the argument, and where they contradict each other
- What repeal does to Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland
- The published positions on each measure, including the ones that cut against the programme and the ones that have overtaken it

**For each measure worked in full**

- What the law actually does, and what it does not do
- What else in the law points at it, counted and grouped
- What the courts have said
- What was tried before, and what happened to it
- How hard this will be, and what is most likely to go wrong

## What this is not

**It is not a finished proposal, a recommendation, or a costing. Nothing here argues that this programme should proceed.** It sets out what proceeding would involve.

It is also not complete, and does not pretend to be. It is a first pass whose purpose is to show the shape and depth of what the programme entails. Carrying it through would need planning, strategy and resourcing well beyond anything in this document.

**And it is a pilot.** Scrutinise is new, and this is among the first proposals put through it. Where a section misses, saying so is the most useful correction available. Some items will be a simple deletion; others will open something that has to be worked properly. **

## How to read it, and three limits to know first

Every finding traces to a source and is dated. Where the document reasons rather than cites, it says so. Where the record is silent, that is said too — **a gap that names itself is worth more than one that looks like an absence of evidence.**

Three limits you should know before you read anything else.

**One. The record of court judgments used here begins in 2003.** Not by choice. The free public record starts in 2001 at the earliest, The National Archives has confirmed the pre-2001 paper record will not be digitised, and the main free database does not permit copying. Older judgments are cited from public sources and marked as such. No citator — the tool lawyers use to check whether a judgment has since been doubted or overruled — was available for this pass, so it cannot be confirmed that none of the judgments cited has been doubted or overruled. That check should be done before any of this argument is relied on.

**Two. Quotations from spoken material are machine-transcribed.** Where two independent transcription systems agree, the text is treated as sound. Where only one exists, the passage is either checked by ear or turned into a paraphrase and labelled as one. It is never quoted from a single machine transcript.

**Three. Every number says what it counts and out of what. None is presented as complete.** Where the document says "23 Acts were cited", that means 23 in the material retrieved — not 23 in the world.
