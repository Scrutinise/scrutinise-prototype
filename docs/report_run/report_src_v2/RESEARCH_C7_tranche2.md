## 7 · Belmarsh — the case that shows exactly where the line is

**For: Part 1's framework section, and the register entry for measure 1.**

***A v Secretary of State for the Home Department* [2004] UKHL 56**, December 2004.

**What was challenged.** Section 23 of the Anti-terrorism, Crime and Security Act 2001, which permitted the indefinite detention without trial of suspected international terrorists who were foreign nationals.

**What the House of Lords held.** Section 23 was incompatible with **Article 5** (liberty) and **Article 14** (non-discrimination). The reasoning was not that detention without trial is always impermissible. It was that this regime was not strictly required by the emergency, because it applied only to foreign nationals when British nationals presented a comparable risk, and because a detainee was free to leave the country — so the measure did not do what an emergency measure would have to do.

Lord Hoffmann dissented on whether a public emergency existed at all, in the sentence that is still quoted: *"The real threat to the life of the nation … comes not from terrorism but from laws such as these."*

⚠⚠ **The point the programme should take from this is procedural, not political, and it is the sharpest illustration available of the distinction Part 1 rests on.**

**The House of Lords did two different things in the same judgment:**

1. It **quashed** the Human Rights Act 1998 (Designated Derogation) Order 2001 — a statutory instrument, an executive act.
2. It **declared section 23 incompatible** — and stopped there. Section 23 remained the law of the land the day after the judgment, and every day until Parliament changed it.

**That is the constitutional line, drawn in a single case:** against an executive act the court's power is to void it; against an Act of Parliament the court's power is to say so and wait.

**And Parliament answered.** It repealed the Part 4 detention power and replaced it with control orders in the **Prevention of Terrorism Act 2005**. That Act has itself since been repealed.

**Alternative views on this issue:**

1. **The case proves sovereignty survives.** The court could not touch the Act. It said what it thought and handed the question back, and Parliament decided. That is the political constitution working exactly as designed, and it is an argument against the programme's premise that courts have taken legislative authority.
2. **The case proves the opposite.** Parliament repealed the provision within months and replaced it with something markedly weaker. A declaration that is always followed is a veto that has been given a polite name. The formal position — the Act stands until Parliament acts — describes a power nobody has ever exercised against.
3. **Both, and the difference is the point.** A declaration of incompatibility has no legal force and near-total practical force. The programme's real complaint is with the second, and repeal of the Human Rights Act would remove the first. **Whether it removes the second is a question about political behaviour, not about legislation**, and no Bill can answer it.

⚠ Reading 3 is the one that should be printed as the finding, because it identifies what repeal would and would not achieve — which is what the whole report exists to establish.

*Sources: A v Secretary of State for the Home Department [2004] UKHL 56; Prevention of Terrorism Act 2005 (c.2), legislation.gov.uk, recorded as repealed.*

⚠ **One citation flag, and it is instructive.** The commentary used to assemble this named the responding statute as "the Terrorism Act 2005" and put it "within 17 days" of the judgment. Both are wrong: the Act is the **Prevention of Terrorism Act 2005**, and the interval was months rather than days. The source was a reputable set of chambers. **The exact date of Royal Assent has not been confirmed from a primary source and must be before it goes in the report.** This is the standing rule doing its job on a source nobody would have thought to doubt.

## 8 · *Jackson* — the judges saying out loud what the programme says they think

**For: Part 1's framework section. This is the strongest single piece of evidence for the programme's central claim, and it comes from the judges themselves.**

***R (Jackson) v Attorney General* [2005] UKHL 56; [2006] 1 AC 262**, 13 October 2005.

**What was challenged.** Members of the Countryside Alliance argued that the Hunting Act 2004 was invalid, because it was passed under the Parliament Acts procedure, and that the Parliament Act 1949 was itself invalid because it had been passed using the procedure created by the Parliament Act 1911.

**What was decided.** The Hunting Act was valid. The 1911 Act could be used to pass the 1949 Act; nothing in the Parliament Acts prevented their being used to amend their own procedure, absent an express restriction.

**⚠⚠ But the decision is not why the case matters.** Three members of the Appellate Committee said, in terms, that parliamentary sovereignty might have limits enforceable by courts.

**Lord Steyn:** *"In exceptional circumstances involving an attempt to abolish judicial review or the ordinary role of the courts, the Appellate Committee of the House of Lords or a new Supreme Court may have to consider whether this is a constitutional fundamental which even a sovereign Parliament acting at the behest of a complaisant House of Commons cannot abolish."*

**Lord Hope:** *"Parliamentary sovereignty is no longer, if it ever was, absolute … The rule of law enforced by the courts is the ultimate controlling factor on which our constitution is based."*

**Baroness Hale:** *"The courts will treat with particular suspicion (and might even reject) any attempt to subvert the rule of law by removing governmental action affecting the rights of the individual from all judicial scrutiny."*

**Why this is the most important passage in the report.**

The programme's central claim is that legislative authority has moved to the judges. The usual answer is that it has not, because Parliament remains sovereign and courts have never refused to apply an Act. *Jackson* is where three senior judges said that in a sufficiently extreme case they might.

It was obiter — said in passing, not deciding the case, and therefore binding on nobody. **That is the strongest argument against relying on it, and the report must say so.** But it is also the argument the programme should meet rather than avoid, because obiter remarks of that seniority are how the common law tests an idea before adopting it, and because the specific trigger Lord Steyn and Baroness Hale both identify — **an attempt to remove governmental action from judicial scrutiny** — is precisely what an ouster clause does.

⚠⚠ **And the programme proposes ouster clauses.** Section 3 of the Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022 is one, and it goes further than most by purporting to exclude review of *the limits or extent* of the power as well as its exercise. **Whether that clause has been tested, and with what result, is the single most useful thing the citator could tell this report, and it is not yet known.**

**Alternative views on this issue:**

1. **The remarks are constitutionally irrelevant.** They are obiter, they bind nobody, no court has acted on them in twenty years, and the orthodox position — Parliament may make or unmake any law — is unchanged and was restated in the same case.
2. **They are a warning that has been heeded.** No government since has legislated the case Lord Steyn described, and the reason may be that everyone understood the warning. On this reading the constraint operates precisely by never being tested.
3. **They are the programme's case, stated by the other side.** If senior judges believe there are limits on what Parliament may enact, then the question of who holds legislative authority is genuinely open, and describing it as settled — in either direction — is the thing that cannot honestly be done.

*Sources: R (Jackson) v Attorney General [2005] UKHL 56, [2006] 1 AC 262. Quotations taken from a secondary source and **each must be verified against the judgment text before print** — see the citation flag above.*
