# Part 5 · Limitations of this analysis

## 5 · Limitations of this analysis

Every limit on what this paper can support is collected here, with the figure that establishes it. Nothing below is a reason to discount a finding; it is what a reader needs in order not to be misled by one.

## What the corpus does not contain

- **BAILII, the principal free repository of British case law, is not used at all.** No judgment text has been taken from it and no request has been made to it. Case law here comes from the National Archives' published judgments.
- **Early day motion signatures are not held.** The record holds 60,995 motions and, for each, only the member who tabled it. Parliament publishes 2,125,547 signatories across those motions — an average of thirty-five each. Where this paper reports who has supported something in Parliament, it is drawing on divisions and debates, never on the signature record.
- **Government publications outside legislation.gov.uk are not ingested**, including post-implementation reviews published on gov.uk alone. ⚠ **This is a gap worth closing, and closing it is on the list of work to do.**

**Both search methods — matching on words and matching on meaning — were confirmed working on the day the analysis was produced.** One chapter of an earlier draft was produced while both were misconfigured; it has been re-run, and the figures here come from the re-run. A check that stops a run starting when either method is misconfigured has been written but is not yet in service, so confirmation is currently a manual step before each run.

## Where the record begins

A count that appears to show absence may only show that the record has not begun.

| Record | Begins |
|---|---|

| Recorded divisions of the House of Lords | 24 November 1999 |
| Judgments of the courts of England and Wales | 2003 |
| Judgments of the Scottish courts | 1999 |

A count of how often a member has voted on a subject is a count since 2016, not a count over a career.

**The record of court judgments contains no House of Lords judgments at all** — none, in 74,896 rows. Every authority from before 2009 has been checked against published sources outside this corpus, and where one is quoted the paragraph number is given.

## The transcript record, and how far it has been checked

David's recorded words are **285 recordings, 128 hours, 1,172,546 words**, July 2016 to August 2026, timestamped and searchable in full.

⚠ **A machine transcript is checked against a second, independently produced one. That second transcript exists for three of the 285 recordings — 1.3 per cent.** For the rest, a passage has been read once, by one engine, and nothing has disagreed with it because nothing else has read it. **Absence of a disagreement is not agreement.**

This is not theoretical. In one recording, one engine renders a name as "Israeli" where a second renders it "Disraeli". **Any quotation should be checked against the recording**, and the timestamp is given so that it can be.

## What the counts mean

**No count in this paper is presented as complete.** Every figure is a count of what the index holds, not of what exists. The three strengths of evidence are recorded separately and are never added together.

**Two of this paper's own figures were wrong and are corrected here.** Counts of actions and of open challenges were counts of revisions rather than of things: the Human Rights Act chapter showed eight actions where there are four, and eighty-nine open challenges where the run described raised fifty-nine. Figures for provisions and judgments were unaffected.

**Impact assessments.** The corpus holds **1,169 impact assessments** across 869 documents. A figure of 18,700 has been used elsewhere; that is the count of *sections*, not of assessments. Of the 1,169, **71 are genuine post-implementation reviews**. Around a thousand sections carry that heading but are the front-sheet question *"Will the policy be reviewed?"*, asked before a measure commences. A promise to review is not a review. Two gaps in the feed, 2008–2016 and 2024–2025, were re-checked in September 2026 and are still real.

**Reviews promised and not delivered.** Of 231 assessments that promised a review, gave a due year and named an instrument, **196 are past their due date with no review deposited**. Because a review published on gov.uk alone would not appear here, that should be read as **"at least 196"**, never as "196".

## The citator, and why its two halves differ

**References from judgments to legislation are strong** — 1,288,630 of them across 52,861 judgments, 99.0% resolving to an identified provision, from the publisher's own tagging.

⚠ **Judicial treatment — whether a case has been followed, distinguished, doubted or overruled — is weak and has no measured error rate.** There are 1,749 treatment records and **none has been validated**, so every statement in this paper about how a case has been treated should be read as unverified. Three classes of error are already known: a hypothetical clause read as a holding; a word used in another sense (a person "distinguishable" from another is not a case "distinguished"); and one quotation from a Victorian statute recorded as overruling a section of the Human Rights Act 1998.

Four further limits:

- **It sees citations inward only** — which later judgments cite a case, never what that case cited.
- **Most citations are not treatments and are correctly left unclassified** — 16,455 of 18,211 candidate passages produce no record.
- **Whether a cited authority is itself held has three states, not two** — 24.1% held, 54.9% not, and **21.0% undetermined**, because the same case is often cited under two references. Treating the undetermined fifth as "not held" understates what is available.
- ⚠ **Nothing in this system records that a provision or a case is no longer good law, and no such statement is made anywhere in this paper.**

## Who has taken which position

16,196 recorded positions have been extracted. **They are not published in this paper as findings.** Checked one by one against their sources, 44% were wrong.

⚠ **That 44% is a rate of misinterpretation, not of invention.** A recorded quotation is found in the document it is attributed to **98.4% of the time**; what is unreliable is the further claim that the passage shows the person holding the position ascribed to them. So the question to put to any entry is not *is this quotation real* but *does it show what it is said to show* — which is why every entry carries the quotation rather than a summary. Anything that could not be confirmed against the quotation has been removed rather than qualified.

**No per-measure register of supporters and opponents is offered**, because the connection between a broad proposal and specific parliamentary business is made by phrase-matching and does not survive inspection: two of the twelve measures connect to a recorded division (254 and 189 members), eight connect only to an early day motion and so return one name each, two connect to nothing, and ten of the twelve connections rest on a two- or three-word phrase. Several are the wrong subject — the arm's-length bodies proposal matched a motion on advertising standards; the civil service and diversity proposals both matched a motion about civil service pensions.

## Cost and benefit

**No costing has been produced for any proposal here, and that is a gap in the system rather than a judgement about the proposals.** None of the 119 drafted actions carries a cost range; there is one cost line in the whole database. On all twelve measures the heading "Cost and duration" is empty.

Where the paper asks what an existing measure has cost, there is often no figure at all. For the public sector equality duty: **no impact assessment of it is held, no post-implementation review was carried out, and no statistical series measures it.** That is a finding about the public record, not a failure of the search.

## What this paper's own analysis was checked against

Each proposal was tested against a fixed set of questions about whether it amounts to a strategy. **Until 9 September 2026 those tests were being applied to an incomplete statement of each proposal** — a title and two lists, with the diagnosis, obstacle, guiding policy and plan all missing. **No verdict from a test run before that date appears in this paper**, and the assessments in the appendices are being reproduced against the complete text.

## Two further gaps

- On each measure, between six and eleven retrieved sources sit under no heading in the research record and are therefore invisible to a reader working through it — roughly ninety-five sources in total. They are present in the data and listed in the appendix.
- The question "what is devolved about this?" returned nothing on any of the twelve measures, including those where the devolution settlements are directly engaged. The devolution material in this paper was assembled by reading, not returned by search.

## The standing rule on citations

Five fabricated citations were produced during research for the first draft — plausible numbers, plausible authors, plausible dates, for documents that do not exist, each with a real near-neighbour. **No citation appears in this paper unless it has been confirmed against a second source or found in the corpus.**
