# Part 2.1 · Does repeal remove the rights?

## 2.1 Does repeal actually remove the rights?

**This is the question the whole programme turns on.** If repealing the Human Rights Act leaves the rights in place, then repeal is not the mechanism, and everything downstream changes.

**David's claim, put plainly:** repeal is not enough, because the courts have absorbed the Convention into ordinary English law. Repeal would leave the substance standing. Something stronger is needed.

### David is right — and the confirmation comes from David's own side

Lord Wolfson KC, the Shadow Attorney General, in advice to the Leader of the Opposition dated 2 October 2025, at paragraph 345:

> "It is, however, clear that leaving the ECHR and repealing the HRA would not amount to a 'factory reset' of UK law to its state pre-ECHR and pre-HRA; to achieve anything like that would involve a complicated legislative programme aimed at rolling back case law and relevant legislative provisions."

**That is David's central claim, reached independently, by the most senior lawyer on David's own side of the argument.**

It is quoted here from the published version. The document carries a confidentiality marking on every page but was published by the Conservative Party and reported widely; no privileged access to it is claimed or implied.

### Why repeal does not restore 1997

Two lines of court decisions carry it, running in opposite directions from 1998.

**Before the Act, English law already protected several of these rights on its own.** Natural justice in *Ridge v Baldwin* (1964). Access to the courts in *ex p Leech* (1994) and *ex p Witham* (1998). Freedom of expression in *Derbyshire County Council v Times Newspapers* (1993), decided expressly on ordinary common-law grounds. And in *ex p Simms* (2000), Lord Hoffmann held that fundamental rights cannot be taken away by vague or general words in a statute — a rule of interpretation that owes nothing to the Human Rights Act.

**Since 2013 the Supreme Court has repeatedly said that a rights question starts with English law, not with the Convention.** *Osborn v Parole Board* (2013), *Kennedy v Charity Commission* (2014), *A v BBC* (2014), *Pham* (2015).

**The clearest single example is *R (UNISON) v Lord Chancellor* (2017).** Employment tribunal fees were struck down on the ordinary common-law right of access to justice. The Convention argument was secondary. **Nothing in that decision depends on the Human Rights Act, and it would stand unchanged if the Act had never been passed.**

### But the absorption is uneven, and that cuts against David

In *Moohan v Lord Advocate* (2014) the Supreme Court declined to find a common-law right to vote. **So the common law is not simply a copy of the Convention.** It is strong on access to justice, natural justice and interpretation; thin or absent elsewhere.

⚠ **A programme built on the assumption that ordinary law now replicates the Convention across the board would be built on a misreading — one that runs in David's favour.** Which rights are actually absorbed has to be tested right by right, and has not been.

### And one finding David will not have expected

Wolfson notes that the Convention's influence has *expanded* common-law protection rather than reduced it — because where English law already went further, there was no reason to cut it back to Convention level.

**So leaving the Convention cannot mechanically shrink the common law back down.** There is no reverse gear. Whatever is to be rolled back has to be rolled back deliberately, by legislation, naming what it is displacing.

### ⚠ And a limit on the remedy hiding in the same paragraph

Wolfson says the Convention's influence on English law is so widespread that **it would be impossible to give a complete account of the law affected.**

Read that carefully. It confirms the diagnosis and simultaneously limits the cure: **if no complete list can be made, no programme can claim to have rolled all of it back.** Any list — including one this platform could produce — is necessarily partial and must say so. A declared partial list is useful. An undeclared one would be exactly the defect this document exists to avoid.

# Part 2.2 · Annulment

## 2.2 "Annulment" — does the Henry VIII device do what it promises?

David's answer to the absorption problem is not repeal but **annulment**: declaring the provisions never to have had effect, on the model of Henry VIII's marriage annulments.

**The answer is that it does not reach the problem David has identified, and this is the finding most likely to be unwelcome.**

### It aims at the wrong target

Declaring a statute never to have had effect reaches **the statute**. The problem David has identified lives in **the case law**.

Where a court has held that *the common law* protects a right, that holding never depended on the statute in the first place — so removing the statute does not remove the holding. *UNISON* is the demonstration: annul the Human Rights Act tomorrow and *UNISON* still stands, because it was not decided under it.

**Repeal leaves the judicial reasoning in place. Annulment leaves it in place too.** David's diagnosis and David's remedy do not meet.

### The comparison is doing more work than it can bear

Annulling a marriage is a decision about the status of one relationship. Annulling a statute would operate on everything done under it across twenty-seven years — convictions, damages, decisions quashed, other statutes reinterpreted. Either those are saved, in which case it is not an annulment; or they are unpicked, which is not achievable.

### ⚠⚠ But here the record turns back in David's favour, and this is the most useful thing in Part 2

The received wisdom is that clauses excluding the courts always fail. In *Anisminic* (1969) the House of Lords read a total exclusion down to nothing. In *Privacy International* (2019) the Supreme Court did the same to a modern one.

**That wisdom is now out of date. In 2022 Parliament drafted an exclusion clause that worked.**

Section 2 of the Judicial Review and Courts Act 2022 removed judicial review of certain tribunal permission decisions. It was upheld in *Oceana* (2023) and by the Court of Appeal in *LA (Albania)* (2023).

**Four reasons it survived, and they are transferable:**

1. **It was partial, not total.** Review was kept for serious jurisdictional error, fundamental breach of natural justice, an improperly constituted tribunal, and bad faith. A court asked to accept a complete exclusion refuses. A court asked to accept a bounded one need not.
2. **It closed the escape route expressly**, by bringing "purported" decisions inside the clause — the exact device *Anisminic* used to get round the 1950 wording.
3. **It restored a standard the courts had themselves preferred**, rather than inventing a new one.
4. **The body protected was an expert judicial one**, not a minister.

⚠ **The lesson is precise, and it is not the one David has drawn.** The device that worked was narrow, surgical and drafted around the known failure. The devices that failed were broad and declaratory. **Annulment, as described, is a broad declaratory device.**

**The programme's difficulty is not that its aim is unachievable. It is that the mechanism named for it is aimed at the wrong target.**

# Part 2.3 · Four plans

## 2.3 Four plans for the same problem — and two of them contradict

Within about a year, four documents on the same side of this argument have proposed **four different ways** of doing it. They agree on the diagnosis to a striking degree and disagree completely on the remedy.

**On the published record, nobody has put that in front of David or PopCon.**

| | Who | What they propose | Timing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Starkey** | **Annul** — declare the provisions never to have had effect | One Act, all at once. Sequential change is defeated by the remainder |
| **2** | **Prosperity Institute** (Braverman and Dampier, Sept 2025) | A **Case Law Review Commission** with a deadline, keeping some Strasbourg case law; annual correction Bills | Two Bills, six-month transition |
| **3** | **Lord Wolfson KC** (advice to the Leader, Oct 2025) | **Seven options, and he declines to choose between them**, saying the choice is political and outside the scope of legal advice | Six months' notice under Article 58 |
| **4** | **Lord Sumption** (Nov 2025) | **Leave the Convention, keep the substance** — re-enact the same rights in an ordinary domestic statute | Not specified |

### ⚠⚠ The finding: two of them recommend opposite things

**Wolfson warns against a new Bill of Rights modelled on the Convention. Sumption proposes exactly that.**

A former Justice of the Supreme Court and the Shadow Attorney General, writing within weeks of each other, both persuaded the present position is untenable, reach **opposite conclusions about the replacement.**

**What turns on it:** if Sumption is right, withdrawal is a change of *forum* — the rights survive in domestic form and the argument is about who interprets them. If Wolfson is right, a Convention-shaped domestic instrument brings the problem straight back in, so withdrawal has to be a change of *substance* too.

**They produce entirely different Bills.** A programme that has not chosen between them cannot be drafted at all.

### Sumption's diagnosis, and why it matters most

His objection is precise, and it is **not to the words of the Convention**. He says he has no problem with the text. His objection is to the Strasbourg court's practice of treating it as a "living instrument" — expanding the text by analogy to produce a large number of new rights. He calls this "an essentially legislative process" and "a usurpation of the function of democratic legislatures."

And then this:

> "We do not need the Human Rights Convention in order to protect human rights."

**That is section 2.1's proposition, stated by a man who sat on the court the programme proposes to abolish.**

⚠ **But note what he does with it, because it is the opposite of what the programme does.** For Sumption the conclusion is that the rights can safely be brought home — the protection does not depend on Strasbourg. For the programme, the conclusion is that the rights have taken root and must be pulled up. **Same premise, opposite remedy.** The premise is not in dispute. What to do about it entirely is.

### The strongest case against, stated fairly

**Dominic Grieve KC**, former Attorney General, accepts the starting point: if the UK left the Convention but kept the Human Rights Act as a domestic bill of rights, rights here **"would continue to be pretty well protected."**

⚠ **That is a significant concession and it should be printed**, because it means the domestic-protection argument is common ground across every serious figure in this section.

His objection is that domestic protection was never the point. The Convention was joined to raise standards elsewhere in Europe and to show willingness to be bound. He lists specific consequences of leaving:

- The **UK–EU Trade and Cooperation Agreement** would be put at risk
- **Horizon** participation would become impossible, because the data-sharing it requires rests on shared Convention adherence
- **Northern Ireland**, where he argues Convention adherence is written into the Belfast/Good Friday Agreement both explicitly and implicitly
- On frequency: since the Brighton Declaration of 2012, which he negotiated, adverse judgments breaking new ground have been very rare — on his recollection, two in the last year

⚠ **That last point is checkable and should be checked**, because it is doing real work. If adverse judgments are as rare as he says, the practical case for leaving rests on their severity rather than their number.

**And note a fifth option hiding in his answer:** leave the Convention but keep the Human Rights Act as a purely domestic instrument. Nobody among the four proposes it, and it is closer to Sumption's position than to any of the others.

### Two balance points, recorded without comment

- **Wolfson told the House of Lords in 2023 that he supported UK membership of the Convention.** His advice of October 2025 reaches a different conclusion. ⚠ *To be checked against Hansard, not against anyone's characterisation, before this is relied on.*
- **There is an internal Conservative counter-position** from the Conservative European Forum, whose president is Sir David Lidington. The Forum reports that Wolfson said his advice should be reconsidered if meaningful reform of the Convention system were achieved. ⚠ *That is the Forum's account. It must be checked against the advice itself. If it holds, it is a significant qualification the other three documents do not carry.*

# Part 2.4 · Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland

## 2.4 What repeal does to Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland

**A Westminster repeal programme does not stop at Westminster**, and this is the part of the programme with the least published work behind it.

### The convention that gets in the way

Under what is called the **Sewel convention**, Westminster does not normally legislate on matters devolved to Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland without the consent of the relevant parliament or assembly. It is a convention, not a law — the Supreme Court confirmed in the first *Miller* case that the courts will not enforce it.

**So consent can be refused and Westminster can proceed anyway.** What that costs is political, not legal, and it is a cost the programme has to be willing to pay knowingly.

⚠ **The most authoritative legal analysis of ECHR withdrawal does not address this at all.** The word "Sewel" does not appear in Lord Wolfson's advice, and neither does the Government of Wales Act. The nearest thing to an answer is a drafting instruction at his paragraph 374: that a manifesto should specify the UK would leave and repeal the Act **irrespective of the stance of devolved administrations.**

That is a political answer to a political problem, and it is a legitimate one. But it means the detailed work of amending the three devolution statutes has not been done by anyone.

### The three statutes

The Human Rights Act is written into the founding legislation of all three devolved administrations. Repealing it without amending them would leave three statutes referring to an Act that no longer exists.

- **Scotland Act 1998** — section 29(2)(d) puts Convention rights beyond the Scottish Parliament's competence, and section 57(2) does the same for Scottish Ministers
- **Government of Wales Act** — ⚠ *the exact provision is disputed between the reading taken here and the Prosperity Institute's, and is not settled. Both are named rather than one chosen.*
- **Northern Ireland Act 1998** — sections 6 and 24 and Schedule 2

⚠ **The Northern Ireland position is different in kind, not just in detail**, because Convention adherence is bound up with the Belfast/Good Friday Agreement — an international treaty with Ireland, not just a domestic statute.

### The two expert camps on Northern Ireland, and they disagree

**Neither side is obviously right, and this document does not adjudicate between them.**

- **Policy Exchange** (Conor Casey, Richard Ekins and Sir Stephen Laws, 2025) argue that withdrawal would not itself breach the Belfast Agreement, though some domestic rights protection would still be needed in Northern Ireland.
- **Northern Ireland public lawyers** including Aoife O'Donoghue and Colin Murray, with the Committee on the Administration of Justice, argue that Convention incorporation is deeply embedded in the Agreement's architecture and that withdrawal creates significant treaty consequences.

⚠ **And the two documents on the programme's own side contradict each other on this.** Wolfson relies on the Northern Ireland Court of Appeal in *Re Dillon* holding that the Convention falls outside Article 2 of the Windsor Framework, so there is no obstacle. The Prosperity Institute treats Article 2 as a live obstacle requiring primary legislation. **Incompatible readings of the same case law, published a month apart, on the same side.** An appeal to the Supreme Court is pending.

### And the Equality Act does not apply there at all

This is the clearest example of why the register's entries matter.

**The Equality Act 2010 does not extend to Northern Ireland**, apart from three specified provisions — section 82 (offshore work), section 105(3) and (4), and section 199. Equal opportunities and discrimination are devolved matters.

**Section 149 is not among the three. The public sector equality duty has never applied in Northern Ireland.** A Westminster Bill repealing it would change nothing there.

The nearest equivalent is **section 75 of the Northern Ireland Act 1998**, which arises from the Belfast/Good Friday Agreement and came into force on 1 January 2000. ⚠ **It is an analogue, not an equivalent:**

- It has **two duties of different strength** — "due regard" to promoting equality of opportunity, and the weaker "regard to the desirability" of promoting good relations, across only three of the grounds. Section 149 has one standard throughout.
- **It is enforced completely differently** — through equality schemes approved by the Equality Commission for Northern Ireland, with screening and impact assessments. Section 149 has no such machinery and is enforced by people going to court.

⚠ **So if the objective is to remove public-sector equality duties across the United Kingdom, section 75 of the Northern Ireland Act 1998 is a separate target — and it is not on the list.** Because it derives from the Belfast Agreement, it is a materially harder one. That is a question for David, not a conclusion of this document.

**One more thing falls out of the same section.** The Equality Act does not apply uniformly even across Great Britain: section 190 and Part 15 do not extend to Scotland. **The Act is already a patchwork, and a repeal Bill has to be drafted against the patchwork rather than against the Act's title.**

# Part 2.5 · Who else has said what

## 2.5 Who else has published on this, and what they say

⚠ **A note on method.**** This section did not exist in the first plan. It was added after David's own reading turned up two senior figures — Lord Sumption and Dominic Grieve KC — that the research had missed entirely, because it had covered *the law* and never asked *who else has published on this*. Every item below was found in a handful of ordinary searches. **The lesson: this kind of sweep is cheap and high-yield.**

This is not a literature review and does not claim to be complete. It is what a competent searcher finds quickly, set out so David can say what has been missed.

### On the Human Rights Act — the best-covered measure by a distance

| Who | Position |
|---|---|
| **Lord Wolfson KC**, Shadow Attorney General | Withdrawal justified; warns against a new Bill of Rights modelled on the Convention; says leaving would not be a "factory reset" |
| **Lord Sumption**, former Supreme Court Justice | Objects to Strasbourg's expansive interpretation, not to the text; would replace it with a domestic instrument that looks much the same |
| **Prosperity Institute** (Braverman and Dampier) | Withdrawal plus a Case Law Review Commission under a deadline |
| **Dominic Grieve KC**, former Attorney General | Against withdrawal — but concedes domestic protection would survive it |

**Still unworked and worth having:** Sumption's 2019 Reith Lectures and *Trials of the State* argue at length that law has expanded into territory belonging to politics. ⚠ **That is the programme's own diagnosis, made from the bench, six years before the programme.** It is the single most useful piece of corroboration nobody has used.

### On the Equality Act — two findings that change what the register means

**The repeal of section 149 is already announced Conservative policy.** Kemi Badenoch, in a speech at the Institute for Government on 9 June 2026, said:

> "That's why I can announce today that a Conservative government will repeal the Public Sector Equality Duty in its entirety. We do not need to replace the duty."

And she drew the distinction herself, naming the alternative:

> "Reform… announced that they would abolish the entire Equality Act. So let me be clear, that would mean that it would be legal to discriminate against white people."

> "The Equality Act protects a white man as much as a black woman. But the Public Sector Equality Duty does not. That's why we are going to overhaul the Act, starting by removing this Duty."

⚠⚠ **That puts three positions on one line, and the programme is at the far end of it:**

| Position | Whose |
|---|---|
| Repeal the duty, keep the substantive protections | **Conservative** |
| Abolish the entire Equality Act | **Reform UK** |
| Repeal the Equality Act | **The programme, on this reading** |

**The programme's position is nearer Reform's than the Conservatives', and the Leader of the Opposition has publicly argued against it by name.** That is a fact about the landscape a Bill would be drafted into, not a view about who is right.

**The strongest published rebuttal came two days later:** the Institute for Government (Stuart Hoddinott and Cassia Rowland, 11 June 2026) argue the duty does not require equality of outcomes or positive action, that frontline staff do not name it as a barrier, and that repeal misdiagnoses the problem.

**Second finding: part of what the programme objects to has already been decided by the courts.** See register entry 9 and Part 4.2.

### On the civil service — the measure that looked thinnest, and is not

**Lord Maude of Horsham's Independent Review of Governance and Accountability in the Civil Service, published 13 November 2023**, examines exactly the question the programme raises.

⚠⚠ **And it does not reach the programme's conclusion.** The review states that it proceeds "on the basis that the UK's current system of a permanent and politically impartial Civil Service will be maintained." It proposes major changes to governance and appointments and **stops short of the remedy.**

**That makes it the hardest document in this report for that measure, and hard in a more useful way than opposition would be.** Maude is a former Cabinet Office minister and one of Whitehall's most persistent critics. He reaches the same diagnosis and stops. **The question it puts is not "are you right?" but "what does Maude know that makes him stop where he stops?"**

Responses to it exist across the spectrum and are all findable: the Institute for Government, the UCL Constitution Unit ("institutional fixes for political problems"), and Re:State.

⚠ **The UCL framing is the one the programme most needs to answer**, because it is the strongest available objection to its whole method: that changing institutions does not fix problems that are political in origin.

### And a slip in David's own words, flagged rather than corrected

In one recorded talk the transcript has David referring to *"the secretary of the First Division Civil Servants Association, Mark Sirota"*. **Mark Serwotka led the PCS union, not the FDA**, whose general secretary is Dave Penman. Two different unions.

⚠ **This may be the transcription mishearing rather than the speaker mis-speaking, and a machine transcript cannot settle which.** It is flagged because a report quoting it uncorrected would put an error into David's mouth in print — and because if it is David's error rather than the machine's, David would rather know.

# Part 2.6 · Already before Parliament

## 2.6 What is already before Parliament

⚠⚠ 

The report treated "what was tried before" as a historical question. It is not. **Two Bills are before the House of Commons now which do, in narrower form, two of the twelve things the programme proposes. Both are sponsored by Conservative MPs.**

| Bill | Sponsor | What it does | Where it is |
|---|---|---|---|
| **European Convention on Human Rights (Notification of Withdrawal) Bill** | **Mike Wood MP** (Kingswinford and South Staffordshire) | Gives the Prime Minister power to notify withdrawal from the Convention under Article 58 | First reading 22 June 2026. Second reading listed for **11 September 2026** |
| **Public Sector Equality Duty (Repeal) Bill** | **Joy Morrissey MP** (Beaconsfield) | Repeals section 149 and Schedule 18 of the Equality Act 2010, with consequential provision | First reading 22 June 2026. Second reading listed for **5 March 2027** |

### What this means in practice, stated carefully

**Both are Presentation Bills.** A backbencher can present a Bill without a debate, and it goes into a queue. Bills of this kind rarely reach a substantive second reading and almost never become law without government time. Parliament's own website marks forward dates as provisional.

**So this is not a prediction that either will pass.** What it is, is this: **the drafting question the programme has not yet answered has already been answered twice, by other people, in the form of actual long titles.**

⚠⚠ **And neither Bill has any published text at all.** Parliament's page for each records: *"There are no publications for this Bill yet."* They state an intention and no mechanism. **That is why the work in Part 4.2 is not duplicating anyone — the consequential detail has not been written by anybody.**

### The three long titles answer the question differently

- **Wood's Bill takes withdrawal only.** It does not repeal the Human Rights Act. That is the "change of forum" option.
- **Morrissey's Bill takes section 149 and Schedule 18 only.** It does not touch the Act's substantive anti-discrimination provisions.
- **On this reading the programme takes the whole Act in each case.**

**These are the narrowest available versions of two of the programme's measures, and they are the ones that have reached the Order Paper.** That is information David should have, whether they agree with the narrowing or not.

### The counter-movement, also live, and with government time behind it

| Bill | What it does | Where it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Public Office (Accountability) Bill** — a Government Bill, from a Labour manifesto commitment | Creates a statutory **duty of candour** on public authorities and officials, new criminal offences, and codes of ethical conduct; replaces the common-law offence of misconduct in public office | Commons third reading 14 July 2026; Lords second reading 1 September 2026 |

⚠ **This runs in precisely the opposite direction to the programme, and it is the one with government time.** Where the programme would take civil service management back to the Crown's own powers, this Bill adds statutory duties to officials. **A programme that repeals the 2010 Act while this is on the statute book has to say how the two fit together** — and if it becomes law first, it is a thirteenth thing to unwind rather than a twelfth.
