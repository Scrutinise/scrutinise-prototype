# Part 6 · What happened when the proposals were tested

## 6.1 · What happened when the proposals were tested

### ⚠⚠ The finding, first

**The programme was put in front of a prepared opponent: a public lawyer, expert, well motivated against it, and assumed to have thought about this already. The question asked was not whether the programme is right. It was: how would I stop it?**

**Across the twelve measures the opponent found between 47 and 49 separate ways to stop them. The plan, as it stands, closes none.**

| | Run 1 | Run 2 | Run 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ways found to stop a measure | 49 | 47 | 47 |
| **The plan closes it** | **0** | **0** | **0** |
| The plan partly closes it | 5 | 4 | 3 |
| The plan does not close it | 33 | 35 | 33 |
| ⚠ **The plan makes it easier** | **11** | **8** | **11** |
| Measures with every route left open | 7 of 12 | 8 of 12 | 9 of 12 |

**143 readings across three runs. Not one route was judged closed.** Between 90 and 94 per cent were left wholly open.

⚠ **Three measures had every route open on every run** — the Equality Act, the Supreme Court, and gender self-identification.

**Why three runs.** The test does not give the identical answer twice: the number of routes moved by two, and ten of the routes changed category between readings. **What did not move at all is the zero.** Pairing the routes from the first run against the later ones, thirty-five kept their verdict and ten shifted — **and not one shifted into "closed"**. The ranges above are printed because they are ranges. The zero is printed because it is not.

**What this does and does not mean.** It is not a finding that the programme is wrong, or that it cannot be done. It is a finding that **the programme is currently a statement of intent rather than a plan that survives contact with opposition** — and that each of the 47 to 49 routes is a specific, nameable thing that can be answered. Answering them is the next piece of work, and it is work only David's team can authorise.

⚠⚠ **One result deserves separate notice.** The opponent was never told that the courts have been moving rights protection onto the common law. It found that on its own, named *R (UNISON) v Lord Chancellor* unprompted, and rated the programme's proposal to fall back on the common law as **making the problem worse rather than better** — because the common law is the ground the other side has spent a decade preparing.

**The eleven routes marked "makes it easier" are the ones to read first.** They are places where the plan as drafted hands the opponent something.

---

### The four tests behind that finding

Each measure worked in full was put through four tests before it was written up. The first asks whether it holds as a strategy. The second marks it against nine criteria — whether the problem is stated as a problem, whether the diagnosis names what has to be defeated, whether the guiding policy rules anything out, whether the actions bear on the obstacle named. The third traces the chain from causes to actions and looks for breaks in it. The fourth reads the proposal as a hostile reviewer would.

**The results are printed here rather than summarised**, including the two measures that did not pass, because a set of tests that only ever confirms the proposal would not be worth running.

---

### The results

| | Strategy test | Nine criteria | Chain from causes to actions | Issues raised against it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **The Human Rights Act** | Weak | 8 of 9 | Holds — no defects *(see below)* | 5 |
| **The Equality Act** | Weak | 8 of 9 | Holds — no defects *(see below)* | 6 |
| **The civil service** | Weak | 6 of 9 | ⚠ **Does not hold — 4 defects** | 6 |

**All three are marked weak as strategies while passing most of the criteria**, and that combination is the useful result. A proposal can satisfy the formal tests and still not be a strategy, because what fails is the fit between the parts rather than any part on its own.

⚠⚠ **One column in that table is less reliable than the others, and saying so is the point of printing it.** The nine criteria are stable: read four times over an unchanged proposal, the mark never moved by more than one test of the nine. **The chain-of-reasoning test is not stable.** Read four times over the same unchanged text, the Human Rights Act measure came back *holds*, then *does not hold*, then *holds*, then *holds*.

**So a single chain-of-reasoning verdict is a reading, not a measurement**, and nothing in this document rests on one. What the civil service result rests on instead is direction: it is the one measure that fails on all four tests, consistently, whichever reading is taken. That is a finding. A lone "does not hold" with a defect count attached would not be.

---

### What each test found, in its own terms

**On the Human Rights Act.** The diagnosis holds, and the chain of reasoning held on three of the four readings. What fails consistently is the obstacle: the proposal names "political and institutional inertia" as the thing standing in the way, which restates the fact that the change has not happened rather than explaining why. And it identifies a concrete obstacle the proposal had not confronted —

⚠⚠ **the Convention is embedded in the devolution settlements, so withdrawal carries a risk to the Union.**

That is the most consequential single finding in this document. It is not an objection from an opponent of the programme. It is the result of testing the programme's own flagship measure against its own stated intention, and it names a cost that the programme's published material does not address.

**On the Equality Act.** The diagnosis is sound and the chain held on the reading taken. Two things fail. The guiding policy does not rule anything out, which makes the choice less sharp than a choice has to be. And the actions are a repetitive list rather than a sequenced plan.

**On the civil service.** This is the measure that fails, and it fails coherently enough to be worth stating precisely.

The proposal's own diagnosis is that the obstacle is the *prohibitively high political cost* of reform, arising from a broad cross-party constitutional consensus. Its guiding policy is then to challenge that consensus directly. In the marker's words:

> It proposes to pay the very cost the diagnosis identifies as prohibitive.

And it contradicts itself on the instrument. The stated approach rules out primary legislation; the actions set out two plans that both require it. A bill is drafted twice, under different actions, with no sequencing between them.

**None of that is a reason to abandon the measure.** It is a list of the things that have to be settled before the measure can be drafted, and settling them is work for David and his team rather than for anyone else.

---

### ⚠⚠ Why these results can be relied on, and how far

Until 9 September 2026 the four tests were reading an incomplete statement of each proposal — a title and two lists, with the problem, the diagnosis, the guiding policy and the plan all absent. The failures they recorded were accurate readings of what they were shown and told you nothing about the proposals. **No verdict from before that date appears in this document.**

The correction is visible without reading a word of any test output, in the size of what each marker was given:

| Measure | Before | After | Score before | Score after |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The Human Rights Act | 1,827 → 2,400 | **3,829** | 2 of 9, then 1, then 1 | **8 of 9** |
| The Equality Act | 1,801 | **3,549** | 2 of 9 | **8 of 9** |
| The civil service | 1,841 | **3,332** | 6 of 9 | **6 of 9** |

**The third row is the control, and it is the reason the first two can be believed.** The civil service measure was given nearly twice the material and its score did not move at all — 6 of 9 before, 6 of 9 after. If the correction simply made the marker generous, that row would have risen with the others. It did not.

That also settles what the earlier failures were. They were not harsh marking of real weaknesses. They were arbitrary, because the marker was reading a blank page — which is why none of them could be argued with in either direction.

**And the same method was then turned on the tests themselves.** Each was run repeatedly against a proposal that had not changed between readings, to establish how much of a verdict is the proposal and how much is the reading.

| Test | Behaviour across four readings of an unchanged proposal |
|---|---|
| The nine criteria | **Stable** — never moved by more than one test of the nine |
| The chain from causes to actions | ⚠ **Not stable** — one measure returned *holds*, *does not hold*, *holds*, *holds* |

**No test in this document is presented as more precise than it has been shown to be.** Where a result is a single reading it is described as one.

### The decisions the tests could not make

Working the three measures generated **nine alternatives across four decision points on the Human Rights Act alone**, and comparable sets on the other two: what scope the repeal should have, what the root cause actually is, which obstacle is pivotal, and which instrument to use.

Each is recorded with the option taken, the reason for taking it, and the case for each option not taken. **None is resolved.** They are set out in full in Volume 3 because they are decisions for the proposers, and a document that made them silently would be substituting its own judgement for David's.

To take the first: the Human Rights Act analysis proceeds on immediate repeal together with notice under Article 58. The alternative of repealing the Act while remaining a signatory is recorded, with the case for it — domestic supremacy restored, while the United Kingdom keeps a voice in how the Convention develops. **That is a different programme, and the choice between them is not a technical one.**
