# Part 4.1 · How opponents would overturn it

## 4.1 · How opponents would overturn it

### ⚠⚠ The finding, first

David's Programme was put in front of a prepared opponent: our best AI model, briefed to read it as a public lawyer would — expert, well motivated against it, and assumed to have thought about it already. The question asked was not whether the Programme is right. It was: how would I stop it?

Across the twelve measures the opponent found between 47 and 49 separate ways to stop them. The plan, as it stands, blocks none of the avenues of attack. **All forty-nine are listed below**, measure by measure, with how each one works and what would block it.

| | Run 1 | Run 2 | Run 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ways found to stop a measure | 49 | 47 | 47 |
| **The plan blocks it** | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| The plan partly blocks it | 5 | 4 | 3 |
| The plan does not block it | 33 | 35 | 33 |
| ⚠ **The plan makes it easier** | 11 | 8 | 11 |
| Measures with every attack route left open | 7 of 12 | 8 of 12 | 9 of 12 |

**143 readings across three runs. Not one route was judged blocked.** Between 90 and 94 per cent were left wholly open.

⚠ **Three measures had every attack route open on every run** — the Equality Act, the Supreme Court, and gender self-identification.

**Why three runs.** The test does not give the identical answer twice: the number of routes moved by two, and ten of the routes changed category between readings. **What did not move at all is the zero.** Pairing the routes from the first run against the later ones, thirty-five kept their verdict and ten shifted — **and not one shifted into "blocked"**. The ranges above are printed because they are ranges. The zero is printed because it is not.

**What this does and does not mean.** It is not a finding that the Programme is wrong, or that it cannot be done. It is a finding that it is currently a statement of intent rather than a plan that survives contact with opposition — and that each of the 47 to 49 routes is a specific, nameable thing that can be answered. Answering them is the next piece of work, and it is work only David's team can authorise.

⚠⚠ **One result deserves separate notice.** The opponent was never told that the courts have been moving rights protection onto the common law. It found that on its own, named *R (UNISON) v Lord Chancellor* unprompted, and rated the proposal to fall back on the common law as **making the problem worse rather than better** — because the common law is the ground the other side has spent a decade preparing.

**The eleven routes marked "makes it easier" are the ones to read first.** They are places where the plan as drafted hands the opponent something.

#### M-01 · Human Rights Act 1998 and the European Convention on Human Rights

> The proposal's 'clean break' is an illusion. By focusing exclusively on the Human Rights Act and the ECHR treaty, it fails to address, and in some cases strengthens, alternative legal sources for the very rights it seeks to remove from judicial oversight. The plan mistakes repealing a statute for closing a field of law.

- **1. Fundamental rights, including those mirroring the ECHR, will be re-asserted and developed by domestic courts through the common law, free from the textual constraints of the Convention.** ⚠⚠ **makes it easier** — *The right survives from another source.* **What would block it:** The Repeal Bill would need to contain express, unambiguous clauses ousting the jurisdiction of the courts to develop common law rights in these areas, a measure that would face extreme legal and political opposition.
- **2. ECHR-equivalent rights will remain legally entrenched in Northern Ireland and Scotland, creating a fractured human rights landscape and a route for challenges to UK-wide legislation as it applies in those jurisdictions.** ◐ partly blocks — *It is fixed in another statute or treaty.* **What would block it:** A new international agreement with Ireland and the consent of the Northern Ireland parties to amend the Good Friday Agreement's human rights provisions, which is politically inconceivable.
- **3. The UK's obligations under other international treaties that incorporate or reference ECHR rights will remain, providing a basis for legal challenges in domestic and international courts.** ⚠ **does not block** — *It is fixed in another statute or treaty.* **What would block it:** A comprehensive plan to renegotiate every international agreement that references or relies on the ECHR, a multi-year diplomatic effort for which the current proposal makes no provision.
- **4. Courts will narrowly construe the Repeal Bill, particularly any transitional provisions, to preserve rights in ongoing cases and interpret any ambiguity in favour of the individual, citing the rule of law.** ⚠ **does not block** — *The courts read the new Act narrowly.* **What would block it:** Extraordinarily precise and comprehensive statutory language covering all possible transitional scenarios, which is practically impossible to draft.

#### M-02 · Equality Act 2010

> The proposal correctly identifies that repealing the statutory duty requires primary legislation, but it fatally underestimates the resilience of the underlying legal principle. By removing the specific, contained duty in Section 149, the plan creates a vacuum that will be filled by broader, less predictable, and potentially more onerous duties derived from common law, the Human Rights Act, and international treaties. The repeal will not end the 'compliance industry'; it will simply make its work more complex and litigation more uncertain.

- **1. The common law duty of procedural fairness will be interpreted to require consideration of equality impacts, effectively recreating a duty like the public sector equality duty.** ⚠⚠ **makes it easier** — *The right survives from another source.* **What would block it:** The Bill would need to include a provision explicitly ousting or limiting the common law duty of fairness as it applies to equality considerations. This would be highly controversial and subject to restrictive interpretation by the courts.
- **2. The Human Rights Act 1998 will be used to compel public bodies to conduct pre-emptive analysis of policies to avoid discriminatory outcomes under ECHR Article 14.** ⚠ **does not block** — *It is fixed in another statute or treaty.* **What would block it:** Repeal or significant amendment of the Human Rights Act 1998, which is a far larger and more constitutionally significant undertaking than this proposal.
- **3. The repeal will be ineffective or create significant legal uncertainty in Scotland and Wales, where specific, devolved equality duties are built upon the foundation of Section 149.** ⚠ **does not block** — *A court declines to apply the new provision.* **What would block it:** The plan would need to include a comprehensive strategy for engaging with devolved administrations and securing Legislative Consent Motions, or explicitly limit the repeal to England-only public bodies, which would undermine the stated goal.
- **4. The UK's international treaty obligations will be used to argue that a duty to proactively consider equality impacts remains a requirement of domestic law.** ⚠ **does not block** — *It is fixed in another statute or treaty.* **What would block it:** The Bill would need to contain a clause directing courts to disregard specific international treaty obligations when interpreting domestic law, a constitutionally novel and highly provocative step. Alternatively, the UK would have to withdraw from the relevant treaties.

#### M-03 · The United Kingdom Supreme Court

> The proposal creates a political mechanism that is legally irrelevant to the courts. It establishes a parliamentary review process but fails to create any instrument that could alter judicial interpretation of existing statutes, leaving the core judicial function untouched.

- **1. The Committee's findings will be deemed legally irrelevant to statutory interpretation.** ⚠ **does not block** — *The courts read the new Act narrowly.* **What would block it:** The Bill would need to enact a new, radical rule of statutory interpretation that explicitly directs courts to treat the Committee's post-hoc reports as authoritative statements of legislative intent, overriding the enacted text. This would be constitutionally unprecedented.
- **2. The judiciary's exclusive role in interpreting law will be asserted as a fundamental constitutional principle, immune from interference by a parliamentary committee.** ⚠ **does not block** — *It is fixed in another statute or treaty.* **What would block it:** The plan would require a clear and explicit statutory provision subordinating the judicial function of interpretation to the findings of a parliamentary committee, which would provoke a constitutional crisis and likely be resisted by the courts as fundamentally unlawful.
- **3. The requirement for judges to give evidence on their reasoning will be declined as unconstitutional.** ⚠ **does not block** — *A court declines to apply the new provision.* **What would block it:** The Bill would need to include unprecedented and explicit enforcement powers against the judiciary, such as contempt proceedings, which would be politically and constitutionally explosive.
- **4. The court's interpretative approach will be re-grounded in common law constitutionalism, making statutory 'intent' secondary.** ⚠⚠ **makes it easier** — *The right survives from another source.* **What would block it:** The Bill would need to include a 'notwithstanding' clause that explicitly ousts the application of all common law constitutional principles when interpreting the target statutes, a measure that would be seen as tyrannical.

#### M-04 · The arm's-length body estate

> The proposal creates a powerful new centralising tool, but its attempt to 'supersede' a complex landscape of existing statutes and constitutional principles with a single Act is its primary vulnerability. The plan will be defeated not by blocking the Act, but by using established principles of statutory interpretation to read its powers down, preserving the independence of the very bodies it was designed to capture.

- **1. The Act's powers of removal and reform will be disapplied for any body exercising judicial or quasi-judicial functions, preserving their independence.** ⚠ **does not block** — *A court declines to apply the new provision.* **What would block it:** The new Act would need to contain express words making clear that its powers apply to bodies exercising judicial functions, and explicitly repeal the specific statutory provisions that guarantee their independence. This would be constitutionally explosive.
- **2. The independence of bodies established or protected under international law will survive, as the new domestic Act will be read as subject to the UK's treaty obligations.** ⚠ **does not block** — *It is fixed in another statute or treaty.* **What would block it:** The Act would need to include a 'notwithstanding' clause, explicitly authorising ministers to make orders that are incompatible with specified international treaties.
- **3. The new Act will be read as creating a parallel power for reform, not as extinguishing the specific powers, duties, and protections contained in each ALB's founding statute.** ⚠ **does not block** — *The courts read the new Act narrowly.* **What would block it:** The new Act would need to include a schedule explicitly repealing the relevant sections of every founding statute it intends to replace, rather than relying on the ambiguous term 'superseding'.
- **4. The 'default presumption of abolition' will be neutered by reading the 'strict statutory criteria' for survival as a procedural checklist, allowing any Accounting Officer who follows the process to certify an ALB's continued existence.** ⚠ **does not block** — *The same result arrives by another procedure.* **What would block it:** The Act would need to remove the certification step and instead frame the decision as a pure, subjective discretionary power of the Minister (e.g., 'the Minister may abolish the body if they consider it appropriate'), which would be much harder to challenge.

#### M-05 · Judicial review of executive decisions

> The proposal attempts to solve a political problem with a technical solution, but in doing so, it creates new and fertile ground for legal challenges. By replacing broad common law principles with specific statutory definitions, it invites litigation over the meaning of those definitions, and fails to address fundamental constitutional arguments that the courts' supervisory jurisdiction cannot be ousted by any statutory formula.

- **1. A court could rule that the supervisory jurisdiction of the High Court to correct errors of law is a fundamental constitutional principle derived from the common law, which cannot be removed by statute, however clearly worded.** ⚠ **does not block** — *A court declines to apply the new provision.* **What would block it:** This route is fundamental and likely cannot be closed by legislative drafting alone; it would require a constitutional shift or a definitive Supreme Court ruling in the government's favour on this specific point.
- **2. A court could interpret the new statutory definitions of 'merits' and 'legality' narrowly, finding that any given error of law by the minister takes the decision outside the statutory scheme altogether, making it a reviewable error of jurisdiction rather than a non-reviewable decision on the 'merits'.** ◐ partly blocks — *The courts read the new Act narrowly.* **What would block it:** Statutory language that could exhaustively list every possible ministerial action and categorise it as 'merits', while also defining every possible error of law as falling within that category. This is practically impossible.
- **3. A court could find that a decision was reached in a procedurally unfair manner, and that such a breach of natural justice is a fundamental error that nullifies the decision, irrespective of whether the substance of the decision falls within the statutory definition of 'merits'.** ⚠ **does not block** — *The same result arrives by another procedure.* **What would block it:** An explicit and unambiguous statutory provision stating that decisions under the Act are not reviewable on grounds of procedural unfairness or breach of natural justice. This would be a constitutionally radical step.
- **4. A court could find that the statutory definitions of 'merits' and 'legality' are incompatible with rights under the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR), as incorporated by the Human Rights Act 1998 (HRA), and either 'read down' the definitions to be compatible or issue a declaration of incompatibility.** ⚠ **does not block** — *The right survives from another source.* **What would block it:** Explicit provisions in the primary legislation disapplying sections 3 and 4 of the Human Rights Act 1998 in relation to this specific power, which would be highly controversial and politically difficult.

#### M-06 · The permanent, appointed civil service

> The proposal is legally coherent but constitutionally fragile. It correctly identifies the statutory target (CRAG 2010) but fatally underestimates the resilience of the constitutional principles the statute codified. Repealing the Act does not erase the principles; it revives their common law and prerogative forms, which are harder to control and offer multiple grounds for legal challenge that the plan fails to close.

- **1. The Prerogative Snap-Back: Reviving Common Law Constraints.** ⚠⚠ **makes it easier** — *The right survives from another source.* **What would block it:** The new Bill would need an explicit clause stating that appointments may be made without regard to the common law principles of merit or impartiality, and that the exercise of this power is non-justiciable on those grounds. This would be a constitutionally explosive 'ouster clause'.
- **2. Unfair Dismissal as a Constitutional Check.** ⚠ **does not block** — *The same result arrives by another procedure.* **What would block it:** The Bill would need to explicitly exclude Senior Civil Servants from the protections of the Employment Rights Act 1996, making them a class of employee dismissible at will without recourse to a tribunal.
- **3. The Devolution Veto.** ⚠ **does not block** — *It is fixed in another statute or treaty.* **What would block it:** The plan would need to either secure Legislative Consent Motions from all devolved legislatures in advance, or the Bill would need to explicitly carve out all civil servants working for devolved administrations from its provisions.
- **4. The Accounting Officer's Shield.** ◐ partly blocks — *The courts read the new Act narrowly.* **What would block it:** This route could only be closed by securing the explicit agreement of the Public Accounts Committee and potentially a resolution of the House of Commons to fundamentally change its century-old system of financial scrutiny. This is a political and procedural challenge, not just a legal one.

#### M-07 · Operational independence of the Bank of England

> The proposal is legally incoherent. It simultaneously advocates for new primary legislation while claiming to use an existing reserve power that is, by design, almost unusable. This internal contradiction provides the primary line of attack: the legislative route is vulnerable to being re-framed as an unnecessary and disproportionate attempt to circumvent the high legal and political bar set by Parliament in the existing Act.

- **1. The 'reserve power' in Section 19 is a legal trap. Any attempt to use it outside of a genuine, catastrophic economic crisis would be quashed by the courts as an abuse of power, making the proposal's stated 'approach' impracticable.** ⚠ **does not block** — *The courts read the new Act narrowly.* **What would block it:** The Bill would need to explicitly redefine 'extreme economic circumstances' to mean 'at the discretion of the Chancellor', which would expose the policy's true intent and face immense political opposition.
- **2. Repealing the 1998 Act does not automatically grant ministers control. It revives the Bank's common law duties and powers under its Charter, creating a new legal battleground where ministerial directives could be challenged as unlawful.** ⚠⚠ **makes it easier** — *The right survives from another source.* **What would block it:** The new Bill would need to go beyond simple repeal and create an explicit, broad, and non-justiciable statutory power for the Chancellor to direct the Bank on all matters of monetary policy.
- **3. The UK's obligations under international agreements, particularly with the IMF, require an independent central bank as a condition of financial credibility and support. Repealing independence could be framed as a breach of these settled understandings, triggering international financial and diplomatic consequences.** ⚠ **does not block** — *It is fixed in another statute or treaty.* **What would block it:** The plan would need a comprehensive diplomatic and economic strategy to renegotiate the UK's position within the IMF and other international financial bodies, or to manage the consequences of being in breach of their core tenets.
- **4. Even with formal control, ministers will be forced to recreate a functionally independent advisory body to provide credible analysis, which will then be used by parliamentary committees to hold the minister to account. This effectively resurrects the MPC's function, just in a different room.** ◐ partly blocks — *The same result arrives by another procedure.* **What would block it:** The plan would need to include measures to curtail the powers of the Treasury Select Committee to scrutinise the new advisory body or the Chancellor's decisions, which would be constitutionally explosive.

#### M-08 · Diversity, equity and inclusion practice in the civil service

> The proposal is legally fragile because it attempts to use executive guidance to override a statutory duty that has been broadly interpreted by the courts. This core action is vulnerable to judicial review, leaving the rest of the plan without a legal foundation.

- **1. The 'legally authoritative' guidance will be declared unlawful by the courts.** ⚠ **does not block** — *A court declines to apply the new provision.* **What would block it:** Primary legislation amending Section 149 of the Equality Act 2010 to redefine the scope of the duty.
- **2. The statutory duty to 'advance equality of opportunity' will be used to justify proactive DEI measures, irrespective of the new guidance.** ◐ partly blocks — *The courts read the new Act narrowly.* **What would block it:** Amending or repealing Sections 149(1)(b) and 149(3) of the Equality Act 2010.
- **3. The Civil Service Commission will assert its statutory independence to maintain its existing recruitment principles, which already balance merit with diversity.** ⚠ **does not block** — *It is fixed in another statute or treaty.* **What would block it:** Primary legislation amending the Constitutional Reform and Governance Act 2010 to make the Commission subject to ministerial direction on the interpretation of its recruitment principles.
- **4. Repealing internal DEI policies and guidance creates a legal vacuum, forcing decision-makers to fall back on the unconstrained, judicially-interpreted public sector equality duty, potentially leading to more cautious and expansive applications than before.** ⚠⚠ **makes it easier** — *Unintended consequence of removing a compliance framework while leaving the underlying duty intact.* **What would block it:** *The opponent stated no remedy for this route.*

#### M-09 · Gender self-identification

> The proposal over-relies on a broad, aggressive reading of a single (and fictional) court judgment. It seeks to use statutory guidance to achieve what would require primary legislation amending two separate Acts, creating guidance that is ultra vires and vulnerable to immediate legal challenge. The plan fails to address the surviving, and potentially enhanced, protections under the Human Rights Act 1998.

- **1. The effect of the Gender Recognition Act 2004 survives any change to the Equality Act 2010, because the proposal fails to amend the GRA itself. A court will read the two Acts together, preserving the 'for all purposes' effect of a GRC.** ⚠ **does not block** — *It is fixed in another statute or treaty.* **What would block it:** The amending legislation would need to state explicitly that the new definition of 'sex' applies 'notwithstanding section 9(1) of the Gender Recognition Act 2004'.
- **2. The statutory guidance will be struck down as ultra vires because it purports to resolve a conflict between two Acts of Parliament (the GRA 2004 and EA 2010), which is a function reserved for Parliament itself or the highest courts. A Minister cannot use guidance to effectively amend or repeal primary legislation.** ⚠ **does not block** — *The same result arrives by another procedure.* **What would block it:** Abandoning the guidance route in favour of new primary legislation that explicitly amends both the Equality Act 2010 and the Gender Recognition Act 2004.
- **3. By removing the statutory protection of 'sex' under the Equality Act for GRC holders, the proposal forces them to rely on their less specific but potentially more powerful rights to privacy and family life under the Human Rights Act 1998.** ⚠⚠ **makes it easier** — *The right survives from another source.* **What would block it:** The plan would need to include provisions to disapply or modify the Human Rights Act 1998 in this context, which would be exceptionally difficult and controversial.
- **4. The Supreme Court's judgment in *For Women Scotland* will be read down to apply only to the specific context of single-sex service exceptions, not as a wholesale redefinition of 'sex' for all purposes of the Equality Act.** ⚠ **does not block** — *The courts read the new Act narrowly.* **What would block it:** The plan would need to be based on new primary legislation rather than a contestable interpretation of case law.

#### M-10 · Publicly funded charities campaigning on government policy

> The proposal uses a contractual tool (grant conditions) to solve a political problem, leaving it vulnerable to being dismantled by established principles of statutory interpretation, public law, and human rights law, which the plan does not and cannot address.

- **1. The grant condition's key terms will be read down by the courts to apply only to the most direct and explicit forms of opposition, leaving most advocacy work untouched.** ⚠ **does not block** — *The courts read the new Act narrowly.* **What would block it:** Primary legislation defining the terms and explicitly limiting the scope of judicial interpretation, which would be highly contentious and difficult to draft.
- **2. The right to campaign is relocated to the charity's privately-raised funds, rendering the grant condition practically ineffective due to the fungibility of money.** ⚠⚠ **makes it easier** — *The right survives from another source.* **What would block it:** A ban on any charity in receipt of any public funds from campaigning on any issue, which would be legally and politically unviable.
- **3. The mandatory imposition of the grant condition will be challenged via judicial review as a breach of the legitimate expectations established by the Government's own Compact with the third sector.** ⚠ **does not block** — *It is fixed in another statute or treaty.* **What would block it:** The formal withdrawal from or renegotiation of the Compact, which would cause a significant political backlash from the entire third sector.
- **4. A charity can bypass the campaigning restriction entirely by challenging the government policy it opposes directly in court through judicial review.** ⚠ **does not block** — *The same result arrives by another procedure.* **What would block it:** Primary legislation to severely restrict the standing of charities to bring judicial review claims, which would be a major and controversial constitutional change.
- **5. The grant condition will be challenged as an unlawful and disproportionate interference with a charity's right to freedom of expression under the Human Rights Act 1998.** ⚠ **does not block** — *A court declines to apply the new provision.* **What would block it:** Primary legislation that explicitly disapplies the Human Rights Act for this purpose, or repealing the Act itself.

#### M-11 · The Sentencing Council and sentencing guidelines

> The proposal successfully creates a political veto over the Sentencing Council, but it fails to control the primary alternative source of sentencing law—the Court of Appeal—and creates a new, reviewable statutory power for the Lord Chancellor that is vulnerable to legal challenge.

- **1. The Court of Appeal continues to issue binding guideline judgments, bypassing the vetoed Sentencing Council.** ⚠ **does not block** — *The right survives from another source.* **What would block it:** Primary legislation explicitly ousting or limiting the Court of Appeal's jurisdiction to issue guideline judgments, which would represent a profound constitutional conflict.
- **2. A decision by the Lord Chancellor to reject a guideline is challenged and quashed by judicial review.** ⚠⚠ **makes it easier** — *The same result arrives by another procedure.* **What would block it:** An explicit and robust ouster clause in the primary legislation, seeking to exclude the jurisdiction of the courts from reviewing the Lord Chancellor's decision. Such clauses are constitutionally controversial and often read down by the courts.
- **3. In the absence of a statutory guideline due to a veto, courts revert to common law principles, leading to less predictable and potentially less politically palatable sentencing outcomes.** ⚠⚠ **makes it easier** — *The right survives from another source.* **What would block it:** A comprehensive statutory code that explicitly displaces the common law of sentencing in its entirety, a legislative task of enormous scale and complexity.
- **4. The requirement for Lady Chief Justice approval is read as a constitutional check requiring her to withhold consent if the Lord Chancellor's refusal is politically motivated and threatens judicial independence.** ⚠ **does not block** — *The courts read the new Act narrowly.* **What would block it:** Amending the Bill to state that the Lady Chief Justice's consent may only be withheld on specified, narrow, non-political grounds, effectively subordinating her role to the executive's.

#### M-12 · The Great Repeal: the programme as a single instrument

> The proposal correctly identifies that its targets are not a single legal structure but a multi-layered settlement. However, its proposed omnibus Act, designed for political force, creates fatal legal weaknesses. By bundling distinct legal problems—common law rights, international treaties, and devolved settlements—into a single instrument, it ensures that the strongest of these defences (the Belfast Agreement) will render the entire legislative project ineffective or, worse, provoke the judiciary into strengthening the very principles the Act seeks to dismantle.

- **1. The repeal of the Human Rights Act 1998 will be rendered ineffective by the courts developing and applying common law constitutional rights to fill the gap, making the rights landscape less predictable and harder for Parliament to control.** ⚠⚠ **makes it easier** — *The right survives from another source.* **What would block it:** The Act would require unprecedentedly clear and express words to abrogate fundamental common law rights and principles, a step so constitutionally radical it is often considered un-draftable and would be fiercely resisted by the courts as a breach of the rule of law.
- **2. The repeal of the Human Rights Act 1998 will not take effect in Northern Ireland because the Act's own provisions make the repeal contingent on the successful renegotiation of the Belfast/Good Friday Agreement, an event that will not occur.** ⚠ **does not block** — *It is fixed in another statute or treaty.* **What would block it:** The Act would have to repeal the HRA's application to Northern Ireland unilaterally and unconditionally, thereby explicitly breaching the Belfast/Good Friday Agreement. The current plan deliberately avoids this due to the diplomatic crisis it would cause.
- **3. The 'strong ouster clauses' intended to prevent judicial review will be narrowly construed by the courts to be ineffective against challenges based on fundamental errors of law or breaches of the rule of law.** ⚠ **does not block** — *The courts read the new Act narrowly.* **What would block it:** Closing this route would require drafting an ouster clause so explicit about precluding review even for the most fundamental errors that it would amount to a direct legislative assault on the rule of law itself, a challenge the Supreme Court is highly likely to resist.
- **4. The provisions overriding the Sewel Convention will be interpreted by the courts as politically significant but legally insufficient to prevent a successful judicial review of executive actions taken under the new Act that affect devolved matters, on the grounds that the underlying devolution settlement remains a mandatory consideration.** ⚠ **does not block** — *The same result arrives by another procedure.* **What would block it:** The Act would need to state not only that the Sewel Convention is overridden, but that no duty to consult, consider, or even acknowledge the views of devolved institutions exists for any purpose relating to the Act. This would be politically explosive and constitutionally unprecedented.

### The four tests behind that finding

Each measure worked in full was put through four tests before it was written up. The first asks whether it holds as a strategy. The second marks it against nine criteria — whether the problem is stated as a problem, whether the diagnosis names what has to be defeated, whether the guiding policy rules anything out, whether the actions bear on the obstacle named. The third traces the chain from causes to actions and looks for breaks in it. The fourth reads the proposal as a hostile reviewer would.

**The results are printed here rather than summarised**, including the two measures that did not pass, because a set of tests that only ever confirms the proposal would not be worth running.

### The results

| | Strategy test | Nine criteria | Chain from causes to actions | Issues raised against it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The Human Rights Act | Weak | 8 of 9 | Holds — no defects | 5 |
| The Equality Act | Weak | 8 of 9 | Holds — no defects | 6 |
| The civil service | Weak | 6 of 9 | ⚠ **Does not hold — 4 defects** | 6 |

All three pass most of the criteria and are still marked weak as strategies. That combination is the useful result: **what fails is the fit between the parts, not any part on its own.** In each case it is one specific, fixable thing:

- **The Human Rights Act.** The obstacle is named as "political and institutional inertia". That restates the fact that the change has not happened; it does not say what is standing in the way.
- **The Equality Act.** The guiding policy rules nothing out, so it is not making a choice; and the actions are a repetitive list rather than a sequence.
- **The civil service.** The diagnosis says the political cost of reform is prohibitive, and the plan then proposes to pay exactly that cost.

⚠ **One column is less reliable than the others.** The nine criteria are stable: read four times over an unchanged proposal, the mark never moved by more than one test of the nine. The chain-of-reasoning test is not: read four times over the same unchanged text, the Human Rights Act measure came back *holds*, then *does not hold*, then *holds*, then *holds*. A single chain-of-reasoning verdict is therefore a reading, not a measurement, and nothing here rests on one. The civil service result rests on direction instead: it is the one measure that fails on all four tests, consistently, whichever reading is taken. **That is a finding. A lone "does not hold" with a defect count attached would not be.**

**No test in this document is presented as more precise than it has been shown to be.** Where a result is a single reading it is described as one.

### What each test found, in its own terms

**On the Human Rights Act.** The diagnosis holds, and the chain of reasoning held on three of the four readings. What fails consistently is the obstacle, above. And the test identified a concrete obstacle the proposal had not confronted —

⚠⚠ **the Convention is embedded in the devolution settlements, so withdrawal carries a risk to the Union.**

That is the most consequential single finding in this document. It is not an objection from an opponent of David's Programme. It is the result of testing the flagship measure against its own stated intention, and it names a cost that the published material does not address.

**On the Equality Act.** The diagnosis is sound and the chain held on the reading taken. The two failures are the ones set out above.

**On the civil service.** This is the measure that fails, and it fails in two ways that can be stated exactly.

- The diagnosis is that reform is blocked by a prohibitively high political cost, arising from a broad cross-party constitutional consensus. The plan is then to challenge that consensus head-on. In the marker's words: *"It proposes to pay the very cost the diagnosis identifies as prohibitive."*
- The stated approach rules out primary legislation, and the actions then set out two plans that both require it. A Bill is drafted twice, under different actions, with no sequencing between them.

None of that is a reason to abandon the measure. It is a list of the things that have to be settled before the measure can be drafted, and settling them is work for David and his team.

### The decisions the tests could not make

Working the three measures threw up choices that the tests cannot make and this document will not make silently.

**What fails without them:** on the Human Rights Act alone there are nine alternatives across four decision points — what scope the repeal should have, what the root cause actually is, which obstacle is pivotal, and which instrument to use. Until each is settled, the drafting cannot start, because the four answers produce four different Bills.

**The solution:** each decision is recorded in Volume 3 with the option taken, the reason for taking it, and the case for each option not taken. None is resolved here. They are decisions for the proposers.

**The first of them, as an example.** The Human Rights Act analysis proceeds on immediate repeal together with notice under Article 58. The alternative — repealing the Act while remaining a signatory — is recorded, with the case for it: domestic supremacy restored, while the United Kingdom keeps a voice in how the Convention develops. That is a different programme, and the choice between them is not a technical one.
