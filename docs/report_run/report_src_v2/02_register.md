# Part 1 · What David said, and what the law would have to do

## The register: what David said, and what the law would have to do

**This is the heart of the document.** The left of each entry is what David said, in David's own words wherever the record allows. The right is a reading of what would have to change in the law to deliver it, and why.

**Where David has been read wrongly, this is the page to correct.** Everything downstream depends on these entries being right.

### How to read an entry

Each measure has five parts:

- **What David said** — David's words. Where these come from a recorded talk, they are machine-transcribed and marked for checking.
- **What this appears to mean** — a reading of it, which may be wrong.
- **The law that would have to change** — the specific Acts and sections, taken from a search of the statute book.
- **What stands in the way** — the single biggest obstacle identified.
- **Status** — whether anyone has ever tried this.

**A note on the twelve.** A widely circulated list of "twelve points" is in general use. It has been checked against 287 transcripts of David speaking. **Four items on that list cannot be traced to anything David is recorded as saying, and two things David says repeatedly are not on it.** David's words are used throughout, and the four unsourced items are set out separately so David can confirm or delete them.

## 1 · The Human Rights Act and the European Convention

**What David said:** *"we need to repeal an awful lot of law that has empowered and indeed politicised the judiciary, like the Human Rights Act, the Equalities Act"* — and, separately, on the need for *"much much clearer language about the need to repeal the European Convention and the realization of both the legal and the symbolic importance of the convention."*

**What this appears to mean:** Repeal the Human Rights Act 1998, and give notice to leave the European Convention on Human Rights under Article 58 of the Convention.

**The law that would have to change:** Human Rights Act 1998 — the whole Act, but section 21 in particular, which defines what "the Convention rights" are and lists what the Act repealed when it came in. Withdrawal from the Convention itself is not an Act of Parliament at all; it is a notice given to the Council of Europe, which takes six months. The search also turned up section 18 of the European Union (Withdrawal Agreement) Act 2020 as touching the same ground.

**What stands in the way:** These are two separate legal acts, not one. Repealing the Act does not take the UK out of the Convention, and leaving the Convention does not by itself remove the Act. A programme has to say which it wants, or say that it wants both and in what order. **Part 2.1 sets out the larger problem: whether either removes the rights at all.**

**Status:** The most attempted of the twelve. A Bill of Rights Bill was introduced in June 2022 and abandoned in June 2023 without ever reaching a second reading. **And there is a Bill before the Commons now** — see Part 2.6.

## 2 · The Equality Act 2010

**What David said:** the same sentence as above — *"the Human Rights Act, the Equalities Act"*.

**What this appears to mean:** Repeal the Equality Act 2010, including the public sector equality duty in section 149.

**The law that would have to change:** Equality Act 2010, and in particular section 149 (the duty), Schedule 18 (the exceptions to it), Schedule 19 (the list of public bodies it binds), and sections 150 to 157, which exist only to make section 149 work. Also the Equality Act 2006, sections 31, 32 and 34, which govern the Equality and Human Rights Commission.

**What stands in the way:** Three things, and all are in Part 4.2. The duty has never applied in Northern Ireland. Most of the working machinery built on it is Scottish and Welsh. And the official Opposition has publicly taken a narrower position than this one.

**Status:** **A Bill to repeal section 149 is before the Commons now.** No government review in fifteen years has recommended repealing the whole Act.

## 3 · The Supreme Court

**What David said:** *"We formally express the supremacy of the judiciary in Britain by the extraordinary creation of a Supreme Court. How you can have a Supreme Court alongside a sovereign parliament is deranged."*

**What this appears to mean:** Abolish the UK Supreme Court and send final appeals back to the House of Lords, as before 2009.

**The law that would have to change:** Constitutional Reform Act 2005, Part 3. The search returned nine separate provisions of that Act, including sections 24, 40 and 148 and six paragraphs across Schedules 1, 9 and 17 — which is a fair indication of how much consequential tidying a repeal would involve.

**What stands in the way:** Nobody has drafted this. See Status.

**Status:** ⚠ **No Bill has been introduced in fifteen years to abolish the Supreme Court and return appeals to the Lords.** Three independent research passes each searched and found none. This measure would be starting from nothing.

## 4 · The arm's-length bodies

**What David said:** *"we need to abandon, kill off, privatise or renationalise fully into the structures of government and Parliament, all of these quangos"* — and elsewhere, *"these 400 or so quangos that are effectively running the country and everything else and sentencing guidance, you name it, advertising standards."*

**What this appears to mean:** Abolish, privatise, or absorb into departments the several hundred bodies that sit between ministers and the public.

**The law that would have to change:** There is no single Act. Every body has its own founding statute. The nearest existing tool is the Public Bodies Act 2011, and the search returned six of its sections — 1, 5, 7, 8, 11 and 12 — which are the powers to abolish, merge and modify public bodies by order.

**What stands in the way:** ⚠ **The 2011 Act already gives ministers much of this power, and it has been used sparingly.** That is worth knowing before drafting a new one: the obstacle may not be the absence of a power.

**Status:** Attempted before, in the Public Bodies Act 2011. What happened to that attempt is the most useful precedent available and has not been examined.

## 5 · Judicial review

**What David said:** *"the system Blair created does not work because you know of judicial review of constant intervention by judges of the incompetence of quangos."*

**What this appears to mean:** Narrow the grounds on which courts can review ministers' decisions.

**The law that would have to change:** There is no single Act to repeal — judicial review is a creation of the courts, not of Parliament. Restricting it means writing a restriction into whichever Act confers the power in question. The search returned recent examples of Parliament doing exactly that: the Judicial Review and Courts Act 2022 (section 2), the Tribunals, Courts and Enforcement Act 2007 (section 11A), and the Border Security, Asylum and Immigration Act 2025 (section 40).

**What stands in the way:** ⚠ **Courts have historically read general restrictions down to nothing.** Since a 1969 case called *Anisminic*, a clause saying "no court may question this decision" has been treated as protecting only lawful decisions — so an unlawful one is reviewable anyway. Recent governments have therefore used narrow, specific clauses instead of broad ones. That is a drafting lesson, not a legal barrier, but it is decisive for how a Bill would be written.

**Status:** The government's own review — chaired by Lord Faulks KC in 2021 — **looked for systemic judicial overreach and did not find it.** The Judicial Review and Courts Act 2022 followed and made modest changes.

## 6 · The permanent civil service

**What David said:** *"the most eminent public figure who passionately opposes the creation of the modern civil service, the Northcote-Trevelyan reforms, is Disraeli."*

**What this appears to mean:** Reverse the model of a permanent, politically impartial civil service appointed on merit, and return control of senior appointments to ministers.

**The law that would have to change:** Constitutional Reform and Governance Act 2010, Part 1 — the part that put the civil service on a statutory footing. The search returned ten provisions, including sections 2, 3, 10, 12 and 51 and five paragraphs of Schedule 1.

**⚠⚠ What stands in the way — and this is the most important single entry in the register:**

**The target David names and the law that exists are not the same thing.** David objects to the Northcote-Trevelyan reforms, which date from 1854. The 2010 Act is the modern written-down version of that settlement. **Repealing the 2010 Act would return civil service management to the Crown's own powers, but would leave the 170-year-old convention entirely intact** — and the convention, not the statute, is what actually determines how Whitehall behaves.

The analysis reached the same conclusion independently, and put it in the first line of the diagnosis: *"David misidentifies the target."*

**Status:** ⚠⚠ **No Bill has ever been introduced to repeal Part 1. No government or independent review has recommended it. No developed legal case for it exists.** Three independent research passes each searched and found nothing. And the most senior sympathetic review — Lord Maude's, of November 2023 — reaches the same diagnosis about civil service accountability and expressly keeps the permanent impartial service, on the basis that the alternative brings "delay and discontinuity".

**Of the twelve, this is the one with the largest gap between what is said and what would have to be drafted.**

## 7 · The Bank of England

**What David said:** *"the gross mismanagement of the Bank of England since its complete political independence in 1998."*

**What this appears to mean:** Reverse the operational independence given to the Bank in 1998.

**The law that would have to change:** Bank of England Act 1998. The search returned ten provisions — sections 12, 13, 19, 20 and 30F, three paragraphs of Schedule 1, plus section 6 of the Bank of England and Financial Services Act 2016 and section 3 of the Financial Services Act 2012.

**What stands in the way:** The obstacle is not legal. It is a settled cross-party consensus that independence is what keeps borrowing costs down — and, more awkwardly, the fact that independence lets whichever government is in office avoid blame for interest rate rises. **The people who would have to vote for this are the people it would make answerable.**

**Status:** David mentions this once in the whole corpus, as a complaint rather than a worked proposal. ⚠ **Confidence that this is intended as one of the twelve is lower than for the others.** It is here so David can confirm or remove it.

## 8 · Diversity and inclusion practice in the civil service

**What David said:** *"then foisting the whole DEI agenda on the civil service, still worse under her…"*

**What this appears to mean:** End the diversity, equity and inclusion practice governing civil service recruitment and conduct.

**The law that would have to change:** ⚠ **Possibly none.** The practice is administrative, not statutory. The search returned the Equality Act 2010 sections 1, 149 and 153, Schedule 18 and Schedule 26 — the same provisions as measure 2.

**What stands in the way:** The Institute for Government's published argument is that the public sector equality duty does not require any of the practice complained of. **If that is right, repealing section 149 would not by itself end it, and the lever here is guidance and ministerial direction rather than legislation.**

**Status:** ⚠⚠ **This is substantially the same measure as number 2.** See Part 3 — the two share six provisions of the Equality Act and eight court judgments between them.

## 9 · Gender self-identification

**What David said:** *"…you get the beginning with people like Maria Miller and Penny Mordaunt of the beginning of gender self identification for transsexuals."*

**What this appears to mean:** Reverse the move towards gender self-identification.

**The law that would have to change:** Equality Act 2010 sections 7 and 11 and Schedule 1; Gender Recognition Act 2004 section 9.

**⚠⚠ What stands in the way — this measure may already be delivered:**

**Self-identification was never enacted in Great Britain.** The Gender Recognition Act 2004 has always required a certificate granted on evidence. And in April 2025 the Supreme Court held, in *For Women Scotland Ltd v The Scottish Ministers*, that "sex" in the Equality Act means biological sex.

**The position objected to has been substantially resolved by litigation, in the direction the programme wants, without any legislation at all.** What remains is a gap between what the law now says and what public bodies are still doing — which is an enforcement problem, not a legislative one.

**Status:** Resolved by the courts. ⚠ **This is a finding David and PopCon should welcome and should also find uncomfortable.** It supports the diagnosis — the courts did move. It complicates the remedy — because if the objectionable reading has gone, what is left to repeal?

## 10 · Publicly funded charities

**What David said:** *"the charities may most of them are no longer properly independent. They're sock puppets."*

**What this appears to mean:** Stop charities that receive public money from campaigning against the policy of the body funding them.

**The law that would have to change:** ⚠ **David names no instrument, and this matters.** Legislation, the conditions attached to grants, and Charity Commission guidance are all available and produce entirely different results. The search returned Charities Act 2011 sections 2, 3, 4 and 74.

**What stands in the way:** Nothing legal. The analysis proposed the narrowest route — making the official who signs off departmental spending personally answerable for the grant conditions, rather than creating any new ban. **

**Status:** Rules already exist. The published analysis is that they are not enforced rather than that they are absent.

## 11 · The Sentencing Council

**What David said:** named within the quango complaint — *"sentencing guidance, you name it, advertising standards."*

**What this appears to mean:** Take sentencing guidelines out of the hands of an independent council and return them to ministers or Parliament.

**The law that would have to change:** Coroners and Justice Act 2009, section 120 — and sections 19 and 20 of the Sentencing Act 2026.

**What stands in the way:** The independence was a deliberate design choice in 2009, made to keep sentencing consistent and out of day-to-day politics. Reversing it means arguing that the reason for it was wrong, not that it was an accident.

**Status:** ⚠ David names this as an example of the quango problem rather than as a separate measure. It is worked separately so the register can show what a single named body costs to deal with. **Whether it is one of the twelve is a question for David.**

## 12 · The Great Repeal itself

**What David said:** *"the campaign for the great repeal and restoration of the legislation passed under the Blair and Brown governments from 1997 to 2010"* — and on the standard it must meet: *"there's got to be that idea of the great repeal sounds easy. It's not. it will be fought every inch of the way by the whole of that establishment. So it's got to be legally watertight."*

**What this appears to mean:** One Act repealing the constitutional legislation of 1997 to 2010, passed all at once, because proceeding measure by measure would be defeated by whatever had not yet been reached.

**The law that would have to change:** ⚠ **David defines the target by a date range, not by a list of Acts.** That is the first drafting question and it is unanswered. The search returned eleven provisions, spread across the Human Rights Act 1998 (sections 3 and 21), the Constitutional Reform Act 2005, the Victims and Prisoners Act 2024, and six sections of the Legislative and Regulatory Reform Act 2006 — that last one being the existing power to amend legislation by order, which is the nearest tool in the statute book to what an omnibus Act would need.

**What stands in the way:** The Supreme Court's decision in the first *Miller* case closes the shortcut. Ministers cannot do this with the Crown's own powers; it has to be an Act of Parliament, which means the House of Lords.

**And the largest obstacle is stated by the programme's own side.** Lord Wolfson KC, the Shadow Attorney General, wrote in October 2025 that leaving the Convention and repealing the Human Rights Act *"would not amount to a 'factory reset' of UK law"* and that achieving anything like that *"would involve a complicated legislative programme aimed at rolling back case law and relevant legislative provisions."* **That is a description of a document nobody has yet written.** Part 2.1 is a first attempt at what it would contain.

**Status:** Never attempted at this scale. **Part 3 tests the claim that the measures must move together, and finds it is not supported in the way the programme assumes.**
