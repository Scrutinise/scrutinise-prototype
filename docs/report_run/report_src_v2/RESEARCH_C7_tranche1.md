## 1 · Grieve's "two adverse judgments last year" — checked

**For: Part 2.5, and the register entry for measure 1.**

Charlie asked for the check to be done rather than flagged. The authoritative source is the European Court of Human Rights' own country profile for the United Kingdom, last updated July 2026.

| Year | UK applications decided | Judgments delivered | Judgments finding at least one violation |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2024 | 332 | 4 | not stated in the profile summary |
| 2025 | 397 | 10 (concerning 12 applications) | **4** |
| 2026, January–July | 127 | 1 | not stated |

**Of the 397 UK applications decided in 2025, 385 were declared inadmissible or struck out.**

**The finding, stated for print:** the order of magnitude in Grieve's claim is right and the specific number depends on which year he was speaking about. In 2025 the Court found at least one violation against the United Kingdom in **four** judgments, out of 397 applications decided, 385 of which never reached judgment at all. Over the whole period to the end of 2021 the Court had delivered 563 judgments concerning the UK, of which 327 found at least one violation and 144 found none.

⚠ **This cuts both ways and the report should say so.** Four adverse judgments a year is a small number, which supports the case that the Strasbourg court is not the operative constraint. But the programme's complaint is not primarily about Strasbourg judgments — it is about domestic courts applying the Convention through sections 3 and 6 of the Human Rights Act. **The Strasbourg number is not a measure of the thing the programme objects to**, and quoting it as though it were would be a mistake either way round.

*Source: European Court of Human Rights, country profile — United Kingdom, updated July 2026; House of Commons Library CBP-8049.*

## 2 · The Safety of Rwanda Act — what was tried, and what happened

**For: a new subsection in Part 2, and the demonstration in Part 1's framework section.**

**What the Act did.** The Safety of Rwanda (Asylum and Immigration) Act 2024 was the strongest instrument Parliament has yet used against judicial and Convention constraint. Its eleven sections included:

- **Section 2 — Safety of the Republic of Rwanda.** Required every decision-maker, including courts and tribunals, to treat Rwanda as a safe country. It did not invite that conclusion; it deemed it.
- **Section 3 — Disapplication of the Human Rights Act 1998.** Switched off named provisions of the HRA for decisions under the Act.
- **Section 4 — Decisions based on particular individual circumstances.** Preserved individual claims, which is where the Act's practical grip loosened.
- **Section 5 — Interim measures of the European Court of Human Rights.** Provided that a Rule 39 interim measure — the mechanism that had grounded the first flight in June 2022 — did not bind a Minister.

**What happened.** No removal flight to Rwanda ever departed under the Act. The Act was **repealed on 2 December 2025** by sections 40 and 65(3)(a) of the Border Security, Asylum and Immigration Act 2025.

**Why this is the most important single case study available to the programme.** It is the controlled experiment. Parliament did the maximum: it legislated a finding of fact, disapplied the Human Rights Act, and released ministers from interim measures. And the policy still did not operate. **That is either the strongest evidence for the programme or the strongest evidence against it, and the report should set out both readings without adjudicating.**

**Alternative views on this issue:**

1. **It proves Parliament is not sovereign in practice.** Parliament legislated as clearly as it is possible to legislate, and the outcome was still determined by litigation, delay and individual claims under section 4. If the most explicit statutory language available cannot deliver a policy, the constraint is real and is not removable by ordinary drafting.
2. **It proves the opposite — that the Act was not the constraint.** The Act was repealed by a new Parliament with a different majority, which is democratic politics working exactly as intended. The flights did not run because of the general election, not because of the courts. On this reading the Act's failure shows that a programme depending on a single Parliament's majority is not a constitutional settlement at all.
3. **It proves the drafting was defective, not the sovereignty.** Section 4 preserved individual claims and section 5 left the Minister with a discretion rather than a duty. A future Act could close both. On this reading the lesson is technical, and the programme should be studying the drafting rather than the constitution.

⚠ **Reading 2 is the one the report cannot omit.** It is the argument a hostile reader will make first, and it goes to the heart of the Great Repeal proposition: an Act passed by one majority is repealable by the next.

*Source: Safety of Rwanda (Asylum and Immigration) Act 2024, legislation.gov.uk (recorded as repealed).*

## 3 · The 100-day question — and a conflict with the programme's own source

**For: Part 6, and a new subsection in Part 2.**

**The problem as stated.** Delivery is wanted inside 100 days. Article 58(1) of the Convention requires six months' notice to the Secretary General of the Council of Europe. Six months is longer than 100 days, so the two cannot both be satisfied unless the notice runs concurrently with the legislation.

**Charlie's proposed sequence:** notify first, legislate during the notice period.

⚠⚠ **The Prosperity Institute — a source the programme's own side relies on — proposes the opposite order.** Its paper *Why and How to Leave the European Convention on Human Rights* (July 2025) sequences it as:

1. Parliament passes an **ECHR (Notification of Withdrawal) Bill** giving the Prime Minister authority to issue the Article 58 notice;
2. the notice is given, and the six-month period begins;
3. during that period Parliament passes an **ECHR (Withdrawal) Bill** repealing the Human Rights Act 1998 and providing that Strasbourg jurisprudence is no longer to be taken into account;
4. the period ends and withdrawal takes effect.

Its reason is *Miller I*: "the executive cannot unilaterally withdraw from international treaties that have been incorporated into domestic law without parliamentary authorisation" (pp. 35–36).

**The report should present this as a live disagreement, because the answer determines whether 100 days is achievable at all.**

**Alternative views on this issue:**

1. **A Bill is needed before notice (the Prosperity Institute position).** *Miller I* held that prerogative could not be used where withdrawal would remove rights Parliament had given domestic effect. The Human Rights Act gives Convention rights domestic effect. Therefore notice requires statutory authority. On this view the first Bill is unavoidable and the earliest possible completion is roughly a Bill plus six months.
2. **Notice may be given by prerogative (the faster route).** *Miller I* turned on the European Communities Act 1972 operating as a conduit for directly effective EU law, so that leaving the treaty automatically stripped rights out of domestic law. **The Human Rights Act does not work that way.** Schedule 1 sets out the Convention rights as text within the Act itself. Denouncing the treaty would not of itself remove a single right from domestic law; only repealing the Act does that. If nothing is removed by the notice, the *Miller I* objection does not obviously bite, and the notice could be given at once with the repealing Bill passing during the six months.
3. **The distinction cuts against the programme as well as for it.** If Schedule 1 rights survive denunciation, then **denunciation on its own achieves nothing domestically.** The operative step is always the repeal of the Act, and the Convention question is separate from — and slower than — the domestic one.

**What holds the line in the interim, and does a challenge run out of time?** During the notice period the United Kingdom remains bound and Strasbourg retains jurisdiction; the Prosperity Institute states that cases commenced before the period ends "would still be treated under the old rules" (p. 37). So a challenge does *not* simply run out of time: proceedings begun during the window survive it. **This needs a practitioner's confirmation before print** — it is the single point on which the 100-day claim most depends, and it is being taken here from a policy paper rather than from the Convention's own case law.

*Sources: Prosperity Institute, "Why and How to Leave the European Convention on Human Rights", July 2025, pp. 26, 32–33, 35–37; Article 58 ECHR.*

## 4 · Miller I and Miller II, and the legislative answer

**For: Part 1's framework section — this is the sharpest available illustration of the political/legal constitution distinction.**

**The razor-thin line.** In *Miller II* (September 2019) the Supreme Court held unanimously that the prorogation of Parliament was unlawful, that the prerogative power of prorogation was justiciable, and that the prorogation was **void ab initio** — of no effect at all, so that Parliament resumed the next day and the session continued as though it had never been ended.

**The distinction the programme should press.** The Court did not refuse to apply an Act of Parliament. It declared an *executive* act void. That is the orthodox constitutional line: courts may quash what ministers do, and may not disapply what Parliament enacts. **The programme's case is that the line is thinner than it looks**, because the standard the Court applied — whether the prorogation "frustrated or prevented" Parliament's constitutional functions without reasonable justification — was one the Court derived itself, from principle, rather than from statute.

**And Parliament answered.** Section 3 of the Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022, headed **"Non-justiciability of revived prerogative powers"**, provides in full:

> A court or tribunal may not question—(a) the exercise or purported exercise of the powers referred to in section 2, (b) any decision or purported decision relating to those powers, or (c) the limits or extent of those powers.

Paragraph (c) is the operative innovation. An ouster clause that stops a court reviewing the *exercise* of a power leaves the court free to decide what the power *is*; paragraph (c) attempts to close that too.

**This is the point to put to a legislator.** The exchange — court declares an executive act void on a principle of its own derivation; Parliament legislates the power beyond review, including its extent — is the political constitution and the legal constitution arguing with each other in real time, through the only instruments each possesses. **It has already happened. The programme is proposing to do it again, wider.**

⚠ **What is not yet checked:** whether section 3 has been tested in court, and with what result. That is a citator question and should go to CC.

*Sources: R (Miller) v The Prime Minister [2019] UKSC 41; House of Commons Library CBP-9006; Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022, section 3, legislation.gov.uk.*

## 5 · The Public Office (Accountability) Bill — and it is still live

**For: Part 2.6, which currently states that the corpus of Bills stops at 3 August 2026.**

**What it is.** Known as the Hillsborough Law. It creates a statutory duty of candour on public authorities in dealings with inquiries and investigations; creates criminal offences for breach of that duty and for misleading the public, replacing the common law offence of misconduct in public office; and provides non-means-tested legal aid for bereaved families at inquests where a public authority is represented.

**Where it has got to.**

| Stage | Date |
|---|---|
| Introduced | 16 September 2025 |
| Second reading — passed without division | 3 November 2025 |
| Public Bill Committee | 27 November, 2 and 4 December 2025 |
| Report stage listed, then postponed | 14 January 2026, then 19 January 2026 |
| Carry-over motion, continuing into the new session | 27 April 2026 |
| Latest amendments published | 14 July 2026 |

**The arguments, with who made them.**

*For.* The Prime Minister described Hillsborough as an injustice and a cover-up by the institutions meant to protect and serve, and placed it alongside Horizon, Grenfell Tower and infected blood. For the Liberal Democrats, Jess Brown-Fuller MP supported the Bill while arguing the legal aid provision was insufficient and whistleblower protection inadequate. Backbenchers pressed for the duty to extend to the intelligence services, subcontractors and the media, and for personal liability for senior leaders.

*Against, or for narrowing.* Shadow Justice Minister Dr Kieran Mullan MP, while supporting the principle, questioned the clarity of "public interest" and "seriously improper" as statutory tests. In committee the intelligence services scope was the main contested point; the Government undertook to address it before report stage, and amendments published in July 2026 now require officials to pass information to the head of their organisation, who must disclose it to an inquiry unless applying for a restriction or converting the inquiry under the Inquiries Act 2005. Government Amendment 7 extended the duty to local authority inquiries into serious incidents.

**Why this matters to the programme, beyond being another Bill.** It runs in the *opposite* direction to the civil service measure. The programme's complaint at measure 6 is that officials are insufficiently answerable to ministers; this Bill makes officials answerable to inquiries and to the criminal law. **A legislator will ask how the two fit together, and the report should have an answer rather than be shown the question.**

*Sources: House of Commons Library CBP-10424 and CBP-10359; Hansard, Public Office (Accountability) Bill second sitting, 27 November 2025.*

## 6 · Bellamy — he exists, and the argument is directly on point

**For: Part 1's framework section. The "if it exists" flag comes out.**

**Richard Bellamy, *Political Constitutionalism: A Republican Defence of the Constitutionality of Democracy*, Cambridge University Press, 2007.** ISBN 978-0-521-86510-4 hardback, 978-0-521-68367-8 paperback. He has also published *Defending the Political Constitution* with Oxford University Press.

**The argument, from the publisher's own description.** Judicial review by constitutional courts, though widely treated as essential to democracy, is neither effective nor legitimate. "The democratic mechanisms of open elections between competing parties and decision-making by majority rule offer superior and sufficient methods for upholding rights and the rule of law." Judicial review is "a form of arbitrary rule which lacks the incentive structure democracy provides to ensure rulers treat the ruled with equal concern and respect", and rights-based judicial review "undermines the constitutionality of democracy", favouring "privileged against unprivileged minorities". The remedy proposed is electoral reform and stronger parliamentary oversight, not written constitutions and wider judicial authority.

⚠⚠ **Why this is worth more to the programme than any of the political sources.** Bellamy is not a critic of the Human Rights Act from the right. He is an academic political theorist making a republican-democratic case, and his conclusion — that rights are better protected by elections and majority rule than by courts — is the programme's central premise arriving from an entirely different direction. **Citing him removes the "British versus foreign" and "left versus right" framings at once**, which is the framing the programme is most vulnerable to.

*Source: Cambridge University Press, front matter and publisher's description, ISBN 978-0-521-86510-4.*
