# Part 5 · The other ten measures

## 5 · The other ten measures, in brief

**Each of these went through the instrument in full — the same process as the two worked in Part 4.** What follows is the short version: what the search found, and what the instrument proposed once it had read the record.

⚠ **Where the instrument proposed something different from David, that is set out plainly.** It is not a recommendation. It is what a machine that had read the record concluded, and it is offered so that David can say where it is wrong.

---

### 5.1 · The Supreme Court

**Search returned:** 128 pieces of evidence, 111 with a citation. Nine provisions of the Constitutional Reform Act 2005, sixteen court judgments.

**What the instrument concluded:** that the 2005 Act, by creating a physically and institutionally separate court, *"inadvertently fostered a judicial culture that sees itself as a constitutional guardian rather than a creature of statute"* — and that this persists because **Parliament has no standing mechanism to review how its own constitutional statutes are being interpreted.**

**What it proposed instead of abolition:** a Constitutional Scrutiny Bill creating a joint committee of both Houses with a mandate to review judicial interpretation of constitutional statutes against what Parliament actually intended, and to recommend corrective amendments, with a guaranteed slot for Parliament to vote on them.

⚠ **We had told it that a former Justice of the Supreme Court is reported to have called the 2005 change largely cosmetic**, so this is not an independent conclusion. What it shows is that, given that material, it proposed filling the vacuum rather than abolishing the court.

**The hard fact:** no Bill has been introduced in fifteen years to abolish the Court. This would be starting from nothing.

---

### 5.2 · The arm's-length bodies

**Search returned:** 103 pieces of evidence, 89 with a citation, fourteen judgments. Six sections of the Public Bodies Act 2011.

**What the instrument concluded, and it is worth reading twice:** *"The accountability gap is not an accident but a design feature. The ALB estate is fragmented across hundreds of individual statutes precisely because this diffusion of responsibility benefits both ministers, who can delegate blame for controversial decisions, and officials, who can exercise power without direct accountability. This deliberate fragmentation is why piecemeal reform always fails; there is no single lever to pull."*

**What it proposed:** attacking the fragmentation rather than the bodies — one statutory mechanism forcing every arm's-length body onto one of a few mandatory templates (full absorption, statutory body with ministerial override, privatisation, or abolition) on a fixed rolling timetable.

⚠ **This is a genuinely different plan from "abolish 400 quangos", and it is a more deliverable one.** It also explains why the Public Bodies Act 2011 — which already gives ministers much of this power — has been used sparingly. **The obstacle is not the absence of a power. It is that nobody has an incentive to use it.**

---

### 5.3 · Judicial review

**Search returned:** 109 pieces of evidence, 96 with a citation, fourteen judgments.

**What the instrument concluded:** that despite the government's own review finding no systemic overreach, the executive perceives that in high-stakes cases judicial review blurs legality into merits — and that the root of it is the constitutional tension between Parliament's sovereignty and the rule of law, which is not resolvable by a clause.

**What it proposed:** narrow, targeted legislation defining the exact boundaries of review for one specific statutory power, drafted so explicitly that setting it aside would require a court to challenge Parliament directly rather than merely "interpret" it. **Not a general ouster.**

⚠ **That is the same lesson as Part 2.2, reached independently from the case law**: the one modern exclusion clause the courts upheld was narrow, surgical and drafted around the known failure.

---

### 5.4 · The permanent civil service

**Search returned:** 118 pieces of evidence, 103 with a citation, eleven judgments. Ten provisions of the Constitutional Reform and Governance Act 2010.

**⚠⚠ What the instrument concluded, in its own first line:** *"The proposer misidentifies the target. The obstacle is not the Constitutional Reform and Governance Act 2010, but the 170-year-old Northcote-Trevelyan settlement… Repealing CRAG Part 1, as proposed, would return civil service management to the royal prerogative but leave the underlying and more powerful convention intact."*

**What it proposed:** not the repeal, which it calls legally ineffective, but **making the missing argument first** — publishing a formal proposal for a ministerially-appointed civil service, and forcing the debate onto the foundational principle rather than a peripheral statute.

⚠ **We supplied the material behind this, so it is not independent.** But the conclusion is the same one three separate research passes reached: **no Bill has ever been introduced to repeal Part 1, no review has recommended it, and the most senior sympathetic review — Lord Maude's, November 2023 — reaches the same diagnosis and expressly keeps the impartial permanent service.**

**Of the twelve, this has the largest gap between what is said and what would have to be drafted.**

---

### 5.5 · The Bank of England

**Search returned:** 119 pieces of evidence, 105 with a citation — and **ten separate provisions across three Acts**, which is more legislative detail than most of the twelve produced.

**⚠ What the instrument concluded, on material we did not supply:** that the 1998 Act *"reclassified the inherently political and distributional choices of monetary policy as a technical exercise in inflation targeting"* — and that **the obstacle is not legal but political**: a cross-party consensus that independence is what keeps borrowing costs down, which *"allows incumbent governments to outsource blame for unpopular but necessary decisions."*

**In plain terms: the people who would have to vote for this are the people it would make answerable for interest rates.**

**What it proposed:** not repeal, but attacking the legitimacy of the consensus — systematically exposing the political choices currently presented as technical ones, until the status quo becomes politically indefensible.

⚠ **This measure produced ten findings that contradicted its own draft — the highest of the twelve.** That is a sign of a measure where the evidence pushes hard against the first instinct.

---

### 5.6 · Diversity and inclusion practice in the civil service

**Search returned:** 110 pieces of evidence, 97 with a citation — and **the highest count of official guidance of any of the twelve**, which is itself the finding.

**What the instrument concluded:** the practice was created without specific democratic authorisation and is justified by an expansive reading of the "due regard" duty — but persists because of institutional inertia, where officials and HR functions benefit from a system rewarding visible compliance.

**What it proposed:** using existing central government powers to impose a narrower, legally authoritative reading of the duty — **forcing a distinction between what the law requires and what is discretionary policy**, so that ministers are visibly accountable for the second.

⚠ **No new legislation.** And note what the register already says: this is substantially the same measure as the Equality Act. See Part 3.

---

### 5.7 · Gender self-identification

**Search returned:** 83 pieces of evidence, 68 with a citation. **The lowest count of the twelve, and that is the finding.**

**What the instrument concluded:** that the Supreme Court's decision in April 2025 *"has resolved the legal ambiguity"*, and **the problem is now the gap between the law as clarified and the administrative practice still reflecting the old understanding.**

**What it proposed: no new legislation at all** — using existing statutory and regulatory powers to close the implementation gap.

⚠⚠ **This build asked every question it was allowed, reviewed all 600 sources available to it, was not cut short, and still found little.** That is a third independent route to the same conclusion, after our own reading and a separate verification pass: **there is not much there because the thing has already happened.**

**A reader comparing row counts across the twelve will misread this measure unless they read this paragraph.**

---

### 5.8 · Publicly funded charities

**Search returned:** 75 pieces of evidence, 60 with a citation — and **only four pieces of legislation, the lowest of the twelve.**

**⚠ Why the count is low, and it is not a defect:** David names no legal instrument for this measure, so there was no statute for the search to converge on. **A measure with no named instrument produces a thin legislative result.** That is the search telling you something true.

**What the instrument concluded, on its own:** *"Public money underwrites campaigning against the policy of the funding body not because rules are absent, but because they are not enforced."* The bodies issuing grants have no incentive to police the boundary, and often benefit from the political cover that independent voices provide.

**What it proposed — and this is the sharpest single output of the twelve:** *"Fix accountability for the funding decision with the grantor, not by creating a new blanket ban on charity campaigning."* Specifically, make the departmental Accounting Officer — **who is already personally responsible to Parliament for the proper use of public money** — explicitly answerable for ensuring grant conditions prevent public funds being used to lobby the grantor.

⚠ **Given a measure with no stated instrument, it did not invent one. It found where accountability already sits and proposed the narrowest thing that would reach it.** No new Act, no new offence, no new regulator.

---

### 5.9 · The Sentencing Council

**Search returned:** 96 pieces of evidence, 78 with a citation, eight judgments. Section 120 of the Coroners and Justice Act 2009 and two sections of the Sentencing Act 2026.

**What the instrument concluded:** that the independence was a deliberate design choice in 2009, made to keep sentencing consistent and out of day-to-day politics — and that this has created a democratic deficit in which an unelected body sets policy that ministers are held publicly accountable for.

**What it proposed:** not abolition, but a control point — amending the 2009 Act so that a guideline cannot take effect without the explicit approval of both the Lord Chancellor and the Lady Chief Justice, with published reasons for approval or refusal.

⚠ **Note what that does: it keeps the Council and makes a named politician answerable for the output.** That is a narrower measure than abolition and reaches the stated complaint.

---

### 5.10 · The Great Repeal itself

**Search returned:** 52 pieces of evidence, 39 with a citation. **The lightest of the twelve, and the reason matters.**

⚠ **David defines this measure by a date range — the legislation of 1997 to 2010 — not by a list of Acts.** A date range has no statute for a search to find. **The thinness of this result is the same finding as the register's: the first drafting question is which Acts are in scope, and it is unanswered.**

**What the instrument concluded:** *"The constitutional reforms of 1997-2010 are not merely an interlocked set of statutes but have created a new, self-defending constitutional order… entrenched through a vast body of case law, new political conventions, and international treaty obligations, which reproduce the effects of the reforms even if the parent Acts are repealed."*

**What it proposed:** the omnibus Act as a **political and procedural vehicle** to prevent death by a thousand cuts — but **internally differentiated**, with a distinct legal mechanism for each kind of thing being dismantled: domestic statutes, devolved powers, treaty obligations, and case law. Not one repealing clause, but one Bill containing four different sorts of instrument.

⚠ **That is a more sophisticated version of David's own proposal, not a rejection of it** — and it is consistent with Part 2.1's finding that repeal alone does not reach the case law.

**Read Part 3 next.** It tests the claim this measure rests on.
