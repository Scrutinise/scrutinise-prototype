### 1. Ministerial appointment and removal decisions will remain subject to judicial review at common law for irrationality, improper purpose, and procedural unfairness.

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** Common law principles of public law, particularly the grounds for judicial review established in cases like CCSU v Minister for the Civil Service [1985] AC 374 (the GCHQ case).

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan is to "repeal Part 1 of CRAG 2010" and create a "new statutory framework vesting appointment and removal powers". This new statutory power would, like any other, be subject to judicial review unless explicitly excluded.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan contains no provision to oust the jurisdiction of the courts. Removing the specific statutory duty for 'fair and open competition' does not remove the overarching common law duty for a public authority to exercise its powers rationally and fairly. A court would still strike down a decision to appoint a manifestly unqualified candidate or to remove a competent one for purely political reasons.

*What would close it:* An explicit and unambiguous ouster clause in the new Act attempting to prevent any judicial review of ministerial decisions on SCS appointments or removals. Even this would be heavily contested and read down by the courts.

### 2. Repealing the statute may revert control of the Civil Service to the Royal Prerogative, which is governed by deeply entrenched conventions of impartiality and merit that a court could enforce.

**Mechanism:** `ENTRENCHED_ELSEWHERE`

**Rests on:** The Royal Prerogative as the historical basis for managing the Civil Service, and the constitutional conventions of the Northcote-Trevelyan settlement that shape its exercise.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The proposal's own diagnosis states that repealing CRAG Part 1 'would simply return civil service management to the Royal Prerogative, leaving the core conventions of permanence and impartiality in place.'

**⚠⚠ **the plan makes it worse**** — The plan aims to replace statute with a new, more permissive statute. But if the new statute is legally defective or successfully challenged, the default is not a legal vacuum but a reversion to the prerogative. This would hand opponents a stronger weapon: instead of arguing about interpreting a new Act, they could argue that ministers are bound by 170 years of constitutional convention that the courts, post-GCHQ, are willing to protect against arbitrary executive action.

*What would close it:* The new Act would need to explicitly abolish the prerogative power in this area and state that the statutory scheme is the sole and exhaustive source of power for civil service management.

### 3. Senior civil servants will use employment law to challenge politically motivated removals as unfair dismissals.

**Mechanism:** `PROCEDURAL_SURVIVAL`

**Rests on:** The Employment Rights Act 1996 and the common law of the contract of employment.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan proposes to 'explicitly vest appointment and removal powers for SCS roles in the relevant Secretary of State' but is silent on the grounds for removal or the applicability of general employment law.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The proposal does not amend or disapply the Employment Rights Act 1996. A senior civil servant is an employee. A minister exercising a 'power of removal' would be effecting a dismissal, which must be for a fair reason (conduct, capability etc.) and follow a fair process. A dismissal for purely political reasons would be an automatically unfair dismissal, leading to a successful and embarrassing claim in an Employment Tribunal. The plan provides no defence against this.

*What would close it:* The new Act would need to explicitly designate all Senior Civil Service roles as a special category of office-holder exempt from the unfair dismissal protections of the Employment Rights Act 1996.

### 4. The courts will read down the new ministerial powers by holding that they must be exercised compatibly with the surviving constitutional principle of an impartial civil service.

**Mechanism:** `NARROW_READING`

**Rests on:** The principle of legality, as articulated in cases like R v Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex p Simms [2000] 2 AC 115, which states that Parliament must use express and unambiguous language to override fundamental constitutional principles.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan's action to 'publish a new Civil Service Code... replacing impartiality with direct accountability to the minister' attempts to redefine a constitutional principle via a non-statutory code, rather than in the primary legislation itself.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan does not include express words in the proposed Bill to abrogate the constitutional principle of impartiality. A court will therefore presume that Parliament did not intend to do so. It will interpret the new 'power of appointment' as being constrained by this principle, requiring it to be exercised in a politically neutral way, thereby defeating the entire purpose of the proposal.

*What would close it:* The new Bill would need to state in clear and express terms that the power of appointment and removal may be exercised for reasons of political affinity and that the constitutional principle of political impartiality does not apply to its exercise.
