### 1. Even if the Human Rights Act is repealed, its core protections will be preserved for Northern Ireland because they are entrenched in an international treaty the UK cannot unilaterally change.

**Mechanism:** `ENTRENCHED_ELSEWHERE`

**Rests on:** The Belfast/Good Friday Agreement 1998.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> Part (c) will ... make repeal of the domestic statute contingent on that process.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan concedes that the repeal of the HRA in respect of Northern Ireland is contingent on a successful renegotiation of the Belfast Agreement. This is not a plan for repeal; it is a plan to ask permission from external partners (notably Ireland) who have no incentive to grant it. The protection therefore survives because the condition for its removal will not be met.

*What would close it:* The Act would have to repeal the HRA's application to Northern Ireland unconditionally, thereby explicitly breaching the Belfast/Good Friday Agreement. The current plan stops short of this.

### 2. The repeal of 'constitutional statutes' like the devolution acts will be read down by the courts to preserve the fundamental principles of the devolved settlement, frustrating the 'factory reset' objective.

**Mechanism:** `NARROW_READING`

**Rests on:** The doctrine of constitutional statutes and the principle of legality, articulated in cases such as Thoburn v Sunderland City Council [2002] EWHC 195 (Admin) and R (Privacy International) v Investigatory Powers Tribunal [2019] UKSC 22.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The Bill will include strong ouster clauses to limit judicial review of its provisions.

**◐ partly closed** — The plan correctly identifies the need for express repeal and ouster clauses. However, it fails to appreciate that courts will subject these provisions, particularly ouster clauses, to an intensely restrictive interpretation to protect fundamental constitutional principles. Merely stating the clauses will be 'strong' does not guarantee they will survive this judicial resistance, which will seek to preserve the substance of the devolved settlements.

*What would close it:* The Act would require exceptionally clear and specific wording, not only repealing the statutes but also explicitly negating the constitutional principles they embody and ousting judicial review in terms that are wholly unambiguous, leaving no room for restrictive interpretation.

### 3. Repealing the Human Rights Act will not erase fundamental rights from UK law; it will activate the common law as an alternative source, leading to the development of a less predictable, UK-specific rights jurisprudence.

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** Common law constitutional rights, as developed in cases like R v Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex p Simms [2000] 2 AC 115 and AXA General Insurance Ltd v Lord Advocate [2011] UKSC 46.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> Part (a) will use straightforward repeal clauses.

**⚠⚠ **the plan makes it worse**** — The plan focuses entirely on the statutory source (the HRA) and has no provision for addressing the common law. By removing the clear framework of the ECHR, the Act creates a vacuum that the judiciary will be compelled to fill by developing common law rights. This hands the courts a creative power, free from the textual constraints of the ECHR, potentially leading to stronger and less predictable rights protections than those the proposal seeks to remove.

*What would close it:* The Act would need to contain express provisions not only repealing the HRA but also prospectively limiting the judiciary's ability to develop the common law in the area of fundamental rights, a constitutionally unprecedented and highly challenging task.

### 4. The plan to override the Sewel Convention, while legally possible, will be treated by the Supreme Court as a breach of a fundamental constitutional convention, triggering a procedural route for a successful legal challenge to Scottish independence.

**Mechanism:** `PROCEDURAL_SURVIVAL`

**Rests on:** The Supreme Court's reasoning on the constitutional status of the Sewel Convention in R (Miller) v Secretary of State for Exiting the European Union [2017] UKSC 5.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> Part (b) will include express provisions overriding the Sewel Convention.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan correctly states it can legally override the convention. However, it fails to address the consequence. The Supreme Court in Miller established the convention's fundamental status. A deliberate legislative override would provide the Scottish Government with the precise legal and political grounds to argue for a second independence referendum, potentially without Westminster's consent, framing it as a necessary response to a fundamental breach of the Union's terms. The proposal's action survives, but it enables an alternative procedural route to the dissolution of the Union it seeks to preserve.

*What would close it:* This route cannot be closed by domestic legislation. Closing it would require securing the consent of the Scottish Parliament for the changes, which is the opposite of the plan's intention.
