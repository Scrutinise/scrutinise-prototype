### 1. The repeal of CRAG Part 1 revives the Royal Prerogative as the source of authority for managing the Civil Service, which itself is bound by the long-standing constitutional convention of a merit-based, impartial service.

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** The Royal Prerogative concerning the management of the Civil Service, and the constitutional principle that statute abrogates the prerogative only to the extent of the inconsistency, as established in cases like Attorney-General v De Keyser's Royal Hotel Ltd [1920] AC 508.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan's focus on repealing 'Part 1 of the Constitutional Reform and Governance Act 2010 (CRAG 2010)' addresses only the statutory footing, not the underlying prerogative power that the statute codified and supplanted.

**⚠⚠ **the plan makes it worse**** — By repealing the statute, the plan removes a clear, codified set of rules and throws the matter back to the uncodified, convention-based prerogative. This gives the courts far greater latitude to interpret and enforce what they see as fundamental constitutional principles, potentially creating stronger, judge-made constraints on ministerial power than existed under the Act.

*What would close it:* The new Bill would need to explicitly oust the Royal Prerogative in relation to Civil Service management and appointments, a constitutionally momentous step that goes far beyond simply repealing CRAG.

### 2. Ministerial decisions on appointment and removal under the new statutory framework will be subject to judicial review on common law grounds of procedural fairness, rationality, and proper purpose.

**Mechanism:** `PROCEDURAL_SURVIVAL`

**Rests on:** The common law principles of judicial review, which apply to any statutory power unless explicitly and successfully ousted (Anisminic Ltd v Foreign Compensation Commission [1969] 2 AC 147).

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan states the Bill will 'create a new statutory framework vesting appointment and removal powers'. It makes no mention of ousting the jurisdiction of the courts to review how those powers are exercised.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan creates a new public law power without shielding it from public law remedies. Any senior civil servant dismissed, or any candidate rejected, will be able to seek judicial review. A court could find that a 'rational' or 'fair' decision must be based on evidence of competence, effectively re-importing a merit principle through the back door.

*What would close it:* The Bill would need to contain a tightly drafted and effective ouster clause attempting to exclude judicial review of ministerial appointment and removal decisions, though such clauses are notoriously difficult to enforce.

### 3. The transfer of Accounting Officer responsibilities to Ministers will be read narrowly by the courts and the National Audit Office, leaving Permanent Secretaries with residual common law and fiduciary duties to protect public funds from improper or irregular use.

**Mechanism:** `NARROW_READING`

**Rests on:** The distinct statutory role of the Comptroller and Auditor General (under the National Audit Act 1983) and the common law duties of officials handling public money. The principle that statutory change must be explicit to override fundamental principles.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan is to 'revise 'Managing Public Money' to transfer Accounting Officer responsibilities'. 'Managing Public Money' is Treasury guidance, not primary legislation. Revising guidance does not extinguish the underlying statutory and common law duties that attach to the role of a department's most senior official.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan mistakes Treasury guidance for the ultimate source of accountability. The formal responsibility to Parliament for regularity and propriety, audited by the NAO, will be held by courts to remain with the Permanent Secretary as the de facto steward of the department's resources, unless primary legislation explicitly and unambiguously transfers this personal culpability to a Minister.

*What would close it:* Primary legislation would be required to amend the Exchequer and Audit Departments Act 1866 and the National Audit Act 1983 to formally designate Ministers as Accounting Officers and make them directly answerable to the Public Accounts Committee and the NAO.

### 4. The new Civil Service Code's attempt to replace impartiality with 'direct accountability to the minister' will be disapplied in any context where a civil servant is required to act in the public interest, uphold the rule of law, or prevent irregularity.

**Mechanism:** `DISAPPLICATION`

**Rests on:** The overarching common law duty of all public officials to uphold the rule of law and act in the public interest, which cannot be overridden by a non-statutory code of conduct. This is supported by case law on the duties of civil servants, such as R (FDA) v Prime Minister [2023] EWHC 949 (Admin).

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan is to 'publish a new Civil Service Code and Ministerial Code reflecting these changes, replacing impartiality with direct accountability to the minister as the primary duty'.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — A Civil Service Code is not law. A court will find that a civil servant's primary duty is to the law and the constitution, not to a minister's instruction, especially if that instruction is unlawful, irrational, or improper. In any conflict between the new code and the rule of law, the code will be disapplied, and the civil servant's duty to refuse the instruction will be upheld.

*What would close it:* This route is almost impossible to close. It would require primary legislation to state that a civil servant's sole duty is to the minister of the day, effectively dismantling the concept of a government under law. Such a provision would face fundamental constitutional challenge.
