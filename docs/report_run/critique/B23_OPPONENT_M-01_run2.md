### 1. Following repeal of the HRA, courts will develop common law constitutional rights to fill the gap, potentially creating stronger and less predictable constraints on the executive than the ECHR.

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** The principle of legality and the body of case law on fundamental common law rights (e.g., R (Daly) v SSHD [2001] UKHL 26; R (UNISON) v Lord Chancellor [2017] UKSC 51).

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan's actions are entirely focused on repealing a statute (the HRA) and denouncing a treaty (the ECHR). It contains no provisions to oust or limit the inherent jurisdiction of the courts to protect fundamental rights at common law.

**⚠⚠ **the plan makes it worse**** — The plan removes the HRA, which channelled rights arguments through a specific statutory framework tied to the ECHR's text. Without this framework, the judiciary will be invited to develop domestic common law rights more dynamically, free from the textual and jurisprudential constraints of the ECHR. This replaces a known quantity with a potentially more assertive and less constrained form of judicial review.

*What would close it:* The Repeal Bill would need to contain exceptionally clear and specific ouster clauses attempting to abrogate fundamental common law rights and limit the courts' interpretive role under the principle of legality. This would be constitutionally unprecedented and fiercely contested.

### 2. The human rights provisions in the devolution acts, particularly the Northern Ireland Act 1998, will survive the HRA's repeal, creating a legal basis for disapplying the repeal act itself in the devolved nations.

**Mechanism:** `ENTRENCHED_ELSEWHERE`

**Rests on:** The Scotland Act 1998, the Government of Wales Act 2006, and particularly the Northern Ireland Act 1998, which is underpinned by the Good Friday Agreement and the Windsor Framework (Article 2). See The Northern Ireland Human Rights Commission and JR 295’s Applications [2024] NIKB 44.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan's action to 'make necessary consequential amendments to other legislation, including the devolution acts' acknowledges the problem but presents it as a simple legislative tidy-up. It is not.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — This action is an aspiration, not a closed route. Amending the human rights components of the devolution settlements, especially the Northern Ireland Act 1998, is not a simple 'consequential amendment'. It would require reopening foundational constitutional settlements, likely breaching international treaty obligations under the Windsor Framework, and would face immense political and legal opposition. The plan has no strategy to overcome this, rendering the intended 'consequential amendments' impracticable.

*What would close it:* A politically viable and legally sound agreement with the devolved administrations to amend their foundational statutes, and a mechanism for satisfying the UK's international obligations under the Windsor Framework regarding non-diminution of rights in Northern Ireland.

### 3. The repeal of the HRA will not extinguish the UK's other international human rights obligations, which courts will continue to use as interpretive aids for domestic statutes and the common law.

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** The UK's status as a signatory to other human rights treaties (e.g., ICCPR, UNCAT) and the established legal principle that Parliament is presumed to legislate consistently with the UK's binding international obligations.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The proposal's diagnosis and actions are exclusively focused on the HRA and the ECHR. The plan's audit is limited to legislation 'reliant on the HRA/ECHR', ignoring the wider landscape of international law.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan is silent on the UK's other treaty obligations. While these are not directly incorporated, they remain a mandatory consideration for courts interpreting ambiguous statutes or developing the common law. Repealing the HRA may lead litigants and judges to place greater weight on these alternative sources of international norms, preserving a channel for human rights arguments in domestic courts.

*What would close it:* The plan would need to include a denunciation of all other major international human rights treaties to which the UK is a party.

### 4. The 'Repeal and Denunciation Bill' itself will be subject to judicial interpretation under section 3 of the Human Rights Act during the six-month period between Royal Assent and the date of ECHR withdrawal.

**Mechanism:** `PROCEDURAL_SURVIVAL`

**Rests on:** Human Rights Act 1998, s.3 (interpretive obligation) and s.6 (duty on public authorities). The HRA remains in force until the repeal takes effect, and denunciation is not effective for six months after notification.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan sequences denunciation to occur 'immediately' upon Royal Assent, triggering a 'six-month withdrawal period' during which a transitional audit occurs. This creates a temporal gap.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — During the six-month gap between notification and effective withdrawal, the HRA remains law. Any executive actions taken under the new Repeal Act during this period would be acts of a public authority and thus subject to challenge under s.6 of the HRA. Courts would be required by s.3 of the HRA to read the new Repeal Act, as far as possible, compatibly with Convention rights, potentially frustrating its immediate intended effects.

*What would close it:* The Repeal Bill would need to include a clause making its own provisions effective immediately upon Royal Assent and explicitly disapplying sections 3 and 6 of the HRA to itself and any actions taken under it. This would be a complex and highly controversial piece of legislative drafting.
