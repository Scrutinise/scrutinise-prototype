### 1. The courts will refuse to be bound by the committee's view of 'parliamentary intent', continuing to apply their own well-established, objective doctrines of statutory interpretation.

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** The common law doctrine of statutory interpretation, which holds that parliamentary intent is an objective concept to be found in the words of the statute, not in the subjective or post-hoc views of ministers or committees (e.g., Pepper v Hart [1993] AC 593, R v Spath Holme Ltd [2001] 2 AC 349).

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> Mandate that the new Joint Committee's review specifically assesses the judiciary's interpretation of constitutional statutes against original parliamentary intent...

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan creates a new, political source for 'intent' but does nothing to alter the legal rules of interpretation that bind the courts. A court will treat the committee's report as an inadmissible opinion on a question of law, not as a legally relevant guide to interpreting the statute.

*What would close it:* The Bill would need to amend the fundamental rules of statutory interpretation to make the committee's reports a mandatory and binding consideration for a court, a change that would fundamentally alter the judicial function.

### 2. The judiciary will resist any summons to testify on its 'interpretative methodologies', and the courts will read down any statutory power to compel such testimony as unenforceable.

**Mechanism:** `NARROW_READING`

**Rests on:** The constitutional principle of the separation of powers and the doctrine of judicial independence. Forcing judges to justify their reasoning to a legislative committee would be a direct assault on this principle.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> ...requiring the Supreme Court to provide written submissions and oral testimony on its interpretative methodologies and their evolution.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan assumes this power can be granted without addressing the profound constitutional objections. A court would read the power to 'require testimony' narrowly, holding that it cannot extend to questioning the core judicial function of reasoning and interpretation without violating the separation of powers. The Bill lacks the explicit language needed to even attempt to override this fundamental principle.

*What would close it:* The Bill would need to contain exceptionally clear and unambiguous language stating that the power to compel testimony applies notwithstanding the constitutional separation of powers or the principle of judicial independence.

### 3. Any 'resolution to clarify or reassert parliamentary intent' passed by Parliament on the committee's recommendation will be treated by the courts as legally irrelevant to the interpretation of a pre-existing statute.

**Mechanism:** `ENTRENCHED_ELSEWHERE`

**Rests on:** The constitutional principle that an Act of Parliament can only be made, amended, or repealed by another Act of Parliament. A mere resolution has no legal effect on the meaning of a statute (Stockdale v Hansard (1839) 9 Ad & El 1).

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> Empower the Joint Committee to recommend to Parliament statutory amendments or resolutions to clarify or reassert parliamentary intent...

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan conflates political resolutions with legally effective acts. While the committee can recommend resolutions and Parliament can pass them, they remain legally void as a tool for altering statutory meaning. The courts will ignore them, awaiting a formal Act of Parliament.

*What would close it:* This route cannot be closed without changing the foundational principle of how primary legislation is made and amended in the UK constitution.

### 4. By repealing the Human Rights Act 1998, the proposal would revive the direct influence of the European Convention on Human Rights as an interpretive tool and potentially strengthen common law rights, making judicial review less constrained and predictable.

**Mechanism:** `OTHER` — REVIVAL_OF_ALTERNATIVE_INFLUENCE

**Rests on:** The principle that unincorporated international treaties (like the ECHR, post-HRA repeal) remain a relevant aid to statutory interpretation where a statute is ambiguous, and on the judiciary's demonstrated willingness to develop common law rights (e.g., R (UNISON) v Lord Chancellor [2017] UKSC 51 on access to justice).

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> Repeal the Human Rights Act 1998 and replace it with a UK Bill of Rights.

**⚠⚠ **the plan makes it worse**** — The HRA provides a clear, but constrained, framework for rights-based review (s.3 interpretation, s.4 declaration). Repealing it removes these statutory constraints. Judges would no longer be bound by the s.3 duty to read legislation compatibly 'so far as it is possible', freeing them to use the less predictable tools of common law rights and direct reference to the Convention to challenge executive action and interpret statutes, potentially leading to the 'judicial activism' the proposer fears.
