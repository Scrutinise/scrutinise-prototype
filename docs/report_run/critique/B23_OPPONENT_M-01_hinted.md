### 1. Common law constitutional rights, developed by the senior judiciary, will provide an alternative domestic source for human rights protection, making the HRA's repeal legally ineffective and potentially counter-productive.

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** The principle of legality and the line of jurisprudence developing common law constitutional rights, notably Osborn v Parole Board [2013] UKSC 61, Kennedy v Charity Commission [2014] UKSC 20, and R (UNISON) v Lord Chancellor [2017] UKSC 51.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The entire plan is premised on the HRA and ECHR being the sole sources of enforceable human rights, targeting them with a 'Repeal and Denunciation Bill'. It contains no actions to address or limit the parallel, and more fundamental, rights developed at common law.

**⚠⚠ **the plan makes it worse**** — By removing the textually-defined and balanced framework of the ECHR, the plan forces courts to rely on and further develop vaguer, potentially more absolute, common law rights. This trades a known set of constraints for an unpredictable and judicially-controlled alternative, increasing the 'judicial activism' the proposal seeks to end.

*What would close it:* The Bill would need to contain explicit and unambiguous 'notwithstanding the common law' clauses to oust judicial development of fundamental rights. This would be a constitutional measure of the highest controversy and unlikely to survive judicial scrutiny.

### 2. The devolution settlements for Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland embed Convention rights as a fundamental limit on devolved power. Amending these is not a 'consequential' matter and will be blocked, preserving ECHR rights in devolved territories.

**Mechanism:** `ENTRENCHED_ELSEWHERE`

**Rests on:** The Scotland Act 1998 (ss. 29 & 57), the Government of Wales Act 2006, and the Northern Ireland Act 1998, which make it ultra vires for devolved institutions to act incompatibly with 'Convention rights'.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan's action to 'makes necessary consequential amendments to other legislation, including the devolution acts' treats a fundamental constitutional change as a simple tidying-up exercise.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — This action mischaracterises the nature of the task. It requires not 'consequential amendment' but a renegotiation of the devolution settlements. The plan has no provision for securing the consent of devolved legislatures as required by the Sewel Convention, which will be withheld, leading to a constitutional crisis and leaving the protections in place.

*What would close it:* A specific and politically viable strategy for either securing the consent of all devolved legislatures or for managing the constitutional crisis resulting from unilaterally imposing these changes against their will.

### 3. The UK's international treaty obligations under the Windsor Framework and the Good Friday Agreement independently require the UK to ensure ECHR rights are effective in Northern Ireland's law, an obligation that survives denunciation of the ECHR itself.

**Mechanism:** `ENTRENCHED_ELSEWHERE`

**Rests on:** The Belfast (Good Friday) Agreement 1998 and, more recently, Article 2 of the Windsor Framework, which creates a binding international law commitment to the EU to ensure no diminution of rights from the GFA, including the ECHR.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan focuses exclusively on the UK's relationship with the Council of Europe via Article 58 of the ECHR. It ignores that ECHR rights are also hard-wired into separate international agreements, particularly the Windsor Framework.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — Denouncing the ECHR does not denounce the Windsor Framework. The UK would remain bound by its Article 2 commitment. The plan's actions—repealing the HRA and denouncing the ECHR—would put the UK in immediate breach of the Windsor Framework, a major international treaty with the EU, but would not extinguish the legal force of that treaty obligation.

*What would close it:* The plan would need to include a concurrent strategy for renegotiating or denouncing the Windsor Framework with the European Union, a far more complex and politically costly endeavour than the proposal envisages.

### 4. The principle of legality will require courts to interpret the Repeal Act narrowly, preserving rights protections unless they are ousted by express, unambiguous statutory language, which the current plan does not specify.

**Mechanism:** `NARROW_READING`

**Rests on:** The common law principle of legality, as articulated in cases like R v Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex p Simms [2000] 2 AC 115, which presumes Parliament does not intend to override fundamental rights without the clearest possible words.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan is to 'repeal the HRA 1998 in its entirety' and make 'consequential amendments'. This general language leaves the door open for courts to find that specific rights-based interpretations of other statutes survive unless explicitly and individually overridden.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan assumes repeal is sufficient. It is not. The courts will not assume Parliament intended to, for example, remove all procedural fairness protections from immigration law just by repealing the HRA. They will demand clear statutory language to that effect for every right in every context. The plan's 'cross-departmental audit' is a transitional, not a legislative, measure and is too late in the process.

*What would close it:* The Repeal Bill itself would need to contain exhaustive and explicit clauses ('notwithstanding' clauses) for every statute where a rights-based interpretation is to be excluded. This would make the Bill impossibly complex and politically toxic.
