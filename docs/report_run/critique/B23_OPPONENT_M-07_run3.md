### 1. The new politically-set monetary policy decisions will be subject to judicial review on common law grounds of irrationality and improper purpose, re-introducing a form of judicial oversight that mimics the effect of independence.

**Mechanism:** `PROCEDURAL_SURVIVAL`

**Rests on:** The common law principles of judicial review of executive action, particularly irrationality (Wednesbury unreasonableness) and improper purpose. Key authorities include Associated Provincial Picture Houses Ltd v Wednesbury Corporation [1948] 1 KB 223 and Padfield v Minister of Agriculture, Fisheries and Food [1968] AC 997.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The action to "Develop and publish a new framework for monetary policy decision-making, outlining how elected officials...will set policy". This new framework, and every decision made under it, will be a reviewable exercise of public power.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan focuses on transferring the decision-making power from the MPC to a minister. It does not, and likely cannot, insulate that minister's decisions from the ordinary principles of judicial review. Any attempt to do so with an ouster clause would be fiercely contested and read down by the courts.

*What would close it:* An explicit and effective ouster clause in the new primary legislation that successfully prevents any judicial review of monetary policy decisions. This is legally and politically fraught and may be deemed unconstitutional by the courts.

### 2. The repeal of statutory independence will be argued to create a vacuum filled by a revived, non-statutory Royal Prerogative power over monetary policy, which is itself subject to judicial review and potential constraint by international law.

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** The doctrine of the Royal Prerogative and the principle that prerogative powers are displaced, not extinguished, by statute (Attorney-General v De Keyser's Royal Hotel Ltd [1920] AC 508). Once the statutory scheme is repealed, the underlying prerogative power may revive.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The action to "repeal the relevant sections of the Bank of England Act 1998 that confer operational independence". This action removes the statutory framework, creating the legal conditions for the argument that the prerogative power, previously held in abeyance, has revived.

**⚠⚠ **the plan makes it worse**** — The proposal aims to create a new, politically accountable power but instead risks reviving an ancient, legally obscure power. Prerogative powers are less defined and their exercise is subject to judicial review on grounds that may be even broader than for statutory powers (CCSU v Minister for the Civil Service [1985] AC 374). This introduces significant legal uncertainty and opens the door to challenges the current Act forecloses.

*What would close it:* The new legislation would need to explicitly abolish any prerogative power relating to monetary policy and create a comprehensive and exclusive statutory code for its exercise by ministers.

### 3. A court will read down the new Act, using the principle of legality, to require that any political direction on monetary policy must still be exercised consistently with the long-established objective of price stability, unless Parliament uses express words to authorise setting interest rates for short-term political gain.

**Mechanism:** `NARROW_READING`

**Rests on:** The principle of legality, which presumes that Parliament does not intend to override fundamental principles or rights without clear and express language. Key authorities include R v Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex p Simms [2000] 2 AC 115.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The action to "Draft a Bill to amend or repeal the relevant sections of the Bank of England Act 1998". The drafting of this Bill is the opening. Unless it contains unusually explicit wording, the courts will presume it is not intended to permit arbitrary or irrational economic management.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan assumes repealing the mechanism of independence is enough. It does not account for how courts will interpret the replacement power. The judiciary will not infer a power to act against fundamental economic stability without express words, which would be politically explosive to include in the Bill. The plan as stated creates a power that will be immediately read down.

*What would close it:* The new Act would need to contain explicit wording stating that monetary policy may be set for purposes other than price stability, including for political objectives, and that the decision-maker is not required to follow economic evidence.

### 4. The UK's obligations under international financial agreements will be used to argue that the repeal of central bank independence is a breach of treaty obligations, creating grounds for legal challenge against the new regime.

**Mechanism:** `ENTRENCHED_ELSEWHERE`

**Rests on:** International agreements to which the UK is a party, such as the IMF Articles of Agreement or potentially financial stability provisions within the UK-EU Trade and Cooperation Agreement (TCA). The argument is that central bank independence is a binding international norm of good economic governance.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The proposal's exclusive focus on the domestic "Bank of England Act 1998" as the sole source of independence. It fails to consider or even mention the UK's position within the international financial architecture.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan is entirely domestic in scope. It proposes repealing a UK statute without any consideration for the UK's international legal obligations. A domestic Act cannot, by itself, abrogate international treaty commitments. The conflict between the new domestic law and international obligations would be the basis for the legal challenge.

*What would close it:* The plan would need to include a strategy for renegotiating or withdrawing from any international agreements that mandate or presuppose central bank independence, alongside the domestic legislative change.
