### 1. Fundamental rights, including those mirroring the ECHR, will be re-asserted and developed by domestic courts through the common law, free from the textual constraints of the Convention.

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** The principle of legality and common law constitutional rights, as articulated in cases like R (UNISON) v Lord Chancellor [2017] UKSC 51 and R v Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex p Simms [2000] 2 AC 115.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The entire plan focuses on repealing a statute (the HRA) and denouncing a treaty (the ECHR). It contains no actions or provisions to limit the inherent power of the UK's senior courts to develop and protect fundamental rights at common law.

**⚠⚠ **the plan makes it worse**** — By removing the HRA, which provided a clear textual framework for rights, the plan creates a vacuum that the judiciary will be compelled to fill using less predictable, uncodified common law principles. This could lead to the development of rights that are stronger and less constrained by precedent than their ECHR counterparts, handing more, not less, interpretive power to domestic judges.

*What would close it:* The Repeal Bill would need to contain express, unambiguous clauses ousting the jurisdiction of the courts to develop common law rights in these areas, a measure that would face extreme legal and political opposition.

### 2. ECHR-equivalent rights will remain legally entrenched in Northern Ireland and Scotland, creating a fractured human rights landscape and a route for challenges to UK-wide legislation as it applies in those jurisdictions.

**Mechanism:** `ENTRENCHED_ELSEWHERE`

**Rests on:** The Northern Ireland Act 1998 (incorporating the Good Friday Agreement) and the Scotland Act 1998, which make it ultra vires for devolved institutions to act incompatibly with Convention rights.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan's action to make 'necessary consequential amendments to other legislation, including the devolution acts' is a vague statement of intent, not a viable strategy for overcoming the profound constitutional obstacles, particularly the international treaty status of the Good Friday Agreement.

**◐ partly closed** — The plan acknowledges the need to amend the devolution acts but underestimates the legal and political difficulty. While Parliament is sovereign and could theoretically amend the Scotland Act over Holyrood's objections, amending the Northern Ireland Act's human rights provisions would likely breach the Good Friday Agreement. The plan does not close this route; it walks directly into a constitutional crisis that will likely force a retreat, leaving rights entrenched in at least Northern Ireland.

*What would close it:* A new international agreement with Ireland and the consent of the Northern Ireland parties to amend the Good Friday Agreement's human rights provisions, which is politically inconceivable.

### 3. The UK's obligations under other international treaties that incorporate or reference ECHR rights will remain, providing a basis for legal challenges in domestic and international courts.

**Mechanism:** `ENTRENCHED_ELSEWHERE`

**Rests on:** The UK-EU Trade and Cooperation Agreement (TCA), specifically Article 524 which conditions law enforcement and judicial cooperation on continued commitment to the ECHR, and other international instruments like the Aarhus Convention.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan's actions are entirely focused on the HRA and the ECHR itself. The 'cross-departmental audit' is framed as a domestic exercise to amend UK legislation, not as a process for renegotiating or denouncing multiple other international treaties that depend on ECHR membership.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — Denouncing the ECHR does not automatically terminate the UK's obligations under other treaties. The TCA, for example, would likely see cooperation suspended, triggering significant political and operational consequences. This creates a powerful external constraint that the plan has no answer for, potentially forcing the government to continue adhering to ECHR principles to maintain other agreements.

*What would close it:* A comprehensive plan to renegotiate every international agreement that references or relies on the ECHR, a multi-year diplomatic effort for which the current proposal makes no provision.

### 4. Courts will narrowly construe the Repeal Bill, particularly any transitional provisions, to preserve rights in ongoing cases and interpret any ambiguity in favour of the individual, citing the rule of law.

**Mechanism:** `NARROW_READING`

**Rests on:** The common law presumption against retrospective legislation that removes vested rights, and the principle that ouster clauses are to be construed strictly, as seen in R (Privacy International) v Investigatory Powers Tribunal [2019] UKSC 22.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan mentions 'provisions for the orderly transition of ongoing cases' and 'transitional guidance for the justice system'. The drafting of these provisions will be a critical battleground. Any lack of absolute clarity will be exploited.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — It is impossible for drafters to foresee every scenario. Courts will be asked to rule on cases that straddle the repeal date. Faced with ambiguous transitional provisions, judges will default to a common law reading that protects existing rights up to the moment of repeal and potentially beyond, frustrating the 'clean break' objective by creating a long tail of HRA-based litigation.

*What would close it:* Extraordinarily precise and comprehensive statutory language covering all possible transitional scenarios, which is practically impossible to draft.
