### 1. The Court of Appeal continues to issue binding guideline judgments, bypassing the vetoed Sentencing Council.

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** The inherent jurisdiction of the Court of Appeal (Criminal Division) to lay down sentencing principles in its judgments, which was not ousted by the Coroners and Justice Act 2009.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The actions are exclusively focused on amending the Coroners and Justice Act 2009 to control guidelines 'issued by the Sentencing Council'. This leaves the separate, non-statutory, case-law-based jurisdiction of the Court of Appeal untouched.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan amends the statutory process for the creation of guidelines by the Council. It does not contain any provision that would or could limit the constitutional role of the Court of Appeal to give guidance on sentencing in the course of deciding appeals. If the Lord Chancellor's veto creates a vacuum, the Court of Appeal is empowered to fill it.

*What would close it:* Primary legislation explicitly ousting or limiting the Court of Appeal's jurisdiction to issue guideline judgments, which would represent a profound constitutional conflict.

### 2. A decision by the Lord Chancellor to reject a guideline is challenged and quashed by judicial review.

**Mechanism:** `PROCEDURAL_SURVIVAL`

**Rests on:** The common law principles of judicial review, which apply to the exercise of statutory powers by ministers (Council of Civil Service Unions v Minister for the Civil Service [1985] AC 374).

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The action to 'Develop clear criteria and a public statement of reasons for the Lord Chancellor's approval or rejection' explicitly creates the material (criteria, reasons) against which a judicial review for irrationality, illegality, or procedural impropriety would be mounted.

**⚠⚠ **the plan makes it worse**** — The plan's attempt to ensure transparency by publishing criteria and reasons actively facilitates a legal challenge. It provides a clear target for claimants to argue the Lord Chancellor has acted unlawfully (e.g., by taking into account irrelevant political considerations, or acting irrationally). Without these published criteria, a challenge would be harder to bring.

*What would close it:* An explicit and robust ouster clause in the primary legislation, seeking to exclude the jurisdiction of the courts from reviewing the Lord Chancellor's decision. Such clauses are constitutionally controversial and often read down by the courts.

### 3. In the absence of a statutory guideline due to a veto, courts revert to common law principles, leading to less predictable and potentially less politically palatable sentencing outcomes.

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** The common law of sentencing, which applies where no statutory guideline exists. The user's own research notes that guidelines for similar offences can be used, demonstrating the persistence of analogical reasoning from case law.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan's entire focus is on the approval mechanism for 'any sentencing guideline issued by the Sentencing Council'. It does not legislate for what happens when a guideline is *not* issued.

**⚠⚠ **the plan makes it worse**** — By blocking a guideline, the Lord Chancellor removes a clear, structured, and evidence-based framework, forcing sentencers to rely on the less predictable and less controllable common law developed through precedent. This replaces a system the executive can influence with one it cannot, potentially leading to the very inconsistency the Council was created to solve.

*What would close it:* A comprehensive statutory code that explicitly displaces the common law of sentencing in its entirety, a legislative task of enormous scale and complexity.

### 4. The requirement for Lady Chief Justice approval is read as a constitutional check requiring her to withhold consent if the Lord Chancellor's refusal is politically motivated and threatens judicial independence.

**Mechanism:** `NARROW_READING`

**Rests on:** The Lord Chancellor's statutory duty to uphold the independence of the judiciary under s.3 of the Constitutional Reform Act 2005, and the Lady Chief Justice's own constitutional position as head of the judiciary.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan requires approval from 'the Lord Chancellor and the Lady Chief Justice'. It treats these as equivalent, but a court will read them in light of their distinct constitutional roles.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan does not define the grounds upon which the LCJ may grant or withhold approval. A court would likely hold that the LCJ's function is not to endorse the Lord Chancellor's political judgment, but to protect the integrity of the sentencing process and judicial independence. She would be acting lawfully, even obliged, to refuse consent to a guideline if the Lord Chancellor's veto was seen as an unconstitutional political intrusion.

*What would close it:* Amending the Bill to state that the Lady Chief Justice's consent may only be withheld on specified, narrow, non-political grounds, effectively subordinating her role to the executive's.
