### 1. Repealing the statutory PSED revives a more potent and less predictable common law duty to consider equality impacts as part of the duty of rational decision-making.

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** The common law duty of rationality and the duty to take into account all mandatory relevant considerations (Associated Provincial Picture Houses Ltd v Wednesbury Corporation [1948] 1 KB 223).

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan's action to "explicitly [state] that other substantive anti-discrimination provisions of the Act remain in force" reinforces the legal status of equality as a mandatory relevant consideration for any rational public law decision.

**⚠⚠ **the plan makes it worse**** — The plan removes a codified, well-litigated, and therefore predictable statutory process. This throws public bodies back onto the general common law. A challenger can argue that without the PSED 'safe harbour', the underlying duty of rationality requires an even more substantive consideration of equality impacts, making decisions more vulnerable to review, not less.

*What would close it:* The Repeal Bill would need to contain an explicit and robust ouster clause preventing courts from judicially reviewing decisions for failure to consider equality impacts. This would be constitutionally controversial and politically difficult to enact.

### 2. The repeal will be ineffective or legally chaotic in Scotland and Wales, where the general duty is entangled with devolved specific duties and legislative competence.

**Mechanism:** `ENTRENCHED_ELSEWHERE`

**Rests on:** The Scotland Act 1998 and the Government of Wales Act 2006, which give legislative competence over public sector duties to the devolved legislatures. The specific duties regulations made in Scotland and Wales are predicated on the existence of the s.149 general duty.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan is entirely silent on the devolved administrations. The actions name UK government departments (Cabinet Office, DLUHC) and assume a simple repeal will apply uniformly across Great Britain.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan contains no actions to address the devolved dimension. It does not seek legislative consent motions, which would likely be refused. The repeal could be challenged as incompetent in its application to devolved bodies, or the devolved legislatures could simply legislate to reinstate the duty, creating a fractured and inconsistent legal framework.

*What would close it:* The plan would need to secure Legislative Consent Motions from both the Scottish Parliament and the Senedd, or explicitly limit the repeal to England-only matters, which would undermine the proposal's stated aim.

### 3. Challenges previously brought under the PSED can be reframed as claims under the Human Rights Act 1998, which imposes a positive obligation on public bodies to counteract discrimination.

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** Section 6 of the Human Rights Act 1998, which makes it unlawful for a public authority to act incompatibly with a Convention right, particularly Article 14 (prohibition of discrimination) read with other articles.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The proposal's entire focus is on the Equality Act 2010. It makes no mention of the Human Rights Act 1998, and the plan contains no actions that would amend or limit it.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan does not touch the HRA. Litigants will pivot from PSED-based judicial reviews, which focus on process, to HRA-based claims, which can focus on discriminatory outcomes and require the public body to provide a robust justification. This shifts the legal ground to one that is often more difficult for the state to defend.

*What would close it:* The plan would need to be accompanied by a repeal of or significant amendment to the Human Rights Act 1998, a far larger and more controversial undertaking.

### 4. The repeal will be read down by the courts to have minimal effect, arguing that the remaining substantive anti-discrimination duties in the Equality Act 2010 cannot be meaningfully complied with without a proactive, forward-looking process of inquiry akin to the PSED.

**Mechanism:** `NARROW_READING`

**Rests on:** The principle of legality and the interpretive duty of courts to read legislation in a way that gives effect to its purpose. The remaining duties (e.g., against indirect discrimination under s.19) require a public body to understand the differential impact of its policies.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The action to "explicitly [state] that other substantive anti-discrimination provisions of the Act remain in force" creates an interpretive tension. The courts will be asked how a public body can ensure it is not indirectly discriminating without proactively considering the impact of its policies on protected groups.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan creates a legal fiction that the process duty can be surgically removed while leaving the substantive duties fully effective. A court is likely to find that to comply with the duty to prevent indirect discrimination, a public body must, as a matter of practical necessity, undertake a process of inquiry and consideration that is functionally identical to the PSED.

*What would close it:* The Repeal Bill would need to explicitly state that no proactive duty of inquiry or consideration is implied or required by any other section of the Equality Act 2010. This would be a difficult clause to draft and would likely be seen as gutting the substantive protections.
