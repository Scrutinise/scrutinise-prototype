### 1. The repeal of the Human Rights Act 1998 will be rendered ineffective by the courts developing and applying common law constitutional rights to fill the gap, making the rights landscape less predictable and harder for Parliament to control.

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** The doctrine of common law constitutionalism and the principle of legality, articulated in cases such as R v Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex p Simms [2000] 2 AC 115 and R (UNISON) v Lord Chancellor [2017] UKSC 51.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan's actions focus exclusively on repealing 'primary legislation' and 'statutory provisions'. It has no mechanism for addressing the parallel, non-statutory rights developed by the judiciary.

**⚠⚠ **the plan makes it worse**** — By repealing the HRA, the plan removes a structured, codified set of rights that includes its own limitations. This creates a vacuum that the judiciary will feel constitutionally compelled to fill by developing more potent, less constrained, and domestically-rooted common law rights. The proposed ouster clauses will be read down using these very principles.

*What would close it:* The Act would require unprecedentedly clear and express words to abrogate fundamental common law rights and principles, a step so constitutionally radical it is often considered un-draftable and would be fiercely resisted by the courts as a breach of the rule of law.

### 2. The repeal of the Human Rights Act 1998 will not take effect in Northern Ireland because the Act's own provisions make the repeal contingent on the successful renegotiation of the Belfast/Good Friday Agreement, an event that will not occur.

**Mechanism:** `ENTRENCHED_ELSEWHERE`

**Rests on:** The Belfast/Good Friday Agreement 1998, an international treaty which commits the UK to incorporating the ECHR into Northern Ireland law, and the Northern Ireland Act 1998 which gives it domestic effect.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan states that Part (c) of the Bill will 'make repeal of the domestic statute contingent on that process' of treaty renegotiation.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan does not close this route; it builds the route into the statute itself. By making the repeal conditional on an external diplomatic process that is not within the UK's unilateral control and is certain to fail, the plan ensures the HRA will remain in force in Northern Ireland. The legislation itself provides the mechanism for its own defeat.

*What would close it:* The Act would have to repeal the HRA's application to Northern Ireland unilaterally and unconditionally, thereby explicitly breaching the Belfast/Good Friday Agreement. The current plan deliberately avoids this due to the diplomatic crisis it would cause.

### 3. The 'strong ouster clauses' intended to prevent judicial review will be narrowly construed by the courts to be ineffective against challenges based on fundamental errors of law or breaches of the rule of law.

**Mechanism:** `NARROW_READING`

**Rests on:** The common law presumption against ousting the jurisdiction of the High Court, as affirmed and strengthened in R (Privacy International) v Investigatory Powers Tribunal [2019] UKSC 22.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan's reliance on 'strong ouster clauses to limit judicial review of its provisions' assumes that such clauses can be drafted to be fully effective.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The Supreme Court has established that only the most explicit and unambiguous statutory language could even theoretically oust review for error of law, and it is doubtful that any wording could succeed. The plan's generic reference to 'strong ouster clauses' does not suggest it has a novel solution to this fundamental constitutional principle. The courts will simply read the clause down to preserve their supervisory jurisdiction.

*What would close it:* Closing this route would require drafting an ouster clause so explicit about precluding review even for the most fundamental errors that it would amount to a direct legislative assault on the rule of law itself, a challenge the Supreme Court is highly likely to resist.

### 4. The provisions overriding the Sewel Convention will be interpreted by the courts as politically significant but legally insufficient to prevent a successful judicial review of executive actions taken under the new Act that affect devolved matters, on the grounds that the underlying devolution settlement remains a mandatory consideration.

**Mechanism:** `PROCEDURAL_SURVIVAL`

**Rests on:** The Supreme Court's reasoning in R (Miller) v Secretary of State for Exiting the European Union [2017] UKSC 5, which established that constitutional conventions have legal relevance in statutory interpretation even if not directly enforceable.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan's action to 'include express provisions overriding the Sewel Convention' treats the convention as a simple procedural rule that can be switched off, rather than a foundational principle of the devolution settlement.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — Even with an express override clause, a court will still be required to interpret the powers granted under the new Act. Following Miller, it will do so in light of the fundamental constitutional change the override represents. It is likely to rule that, while the convention is overridden, any minister exercising a power under the Act that impacts a devolved area must still explicitly confront the fact of non-consent and provide compelling reasons, creating a procedural hurdle for every executive action and opening the door to judicial review on conventional public law grounds (e.g., failure to take into account a relevant consideration).

*What would close it:* The Act would need to state not only that the Sewel Convention is overridden, but that no duty to consult, consider, or even acknowledge the views of devolved institutions exists for any purpose relating to the Act. This would be politically explosive and constitutionally unprecedented.
