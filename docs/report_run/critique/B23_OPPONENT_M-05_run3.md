### 1. A court will find that the question of whether a decision falls under the statutory definition of 'merits' or 'legality' is itself a question of law for the court to determine, thereby preserving its jurisdiction to review for jurisdictional error.

**Mechanism:** `NARROW_READING`

**Rests on:** The constitutional principle of the rule of law and the High Court's inherent supervisory jurisdiction, as established in Anisminic Ltd v Foreign Compensation Commission [1969] and affirmed in R (Privacy International) v Investigatory Powers Tribunal [2019].

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The action to introduce 'statutory definitions of “merits” and “legality”'. These definitions, being statutory text, are necessarily subject to judicial interpretation. The plan cannot make them self-applying.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan concedes the point. The proposed fast-track procedure is specifically for cases 'where the claimant argues a decision falls outside the statutory “merits” definition'. This acknowledges that the boundary is contestable and requires judicial determination. It creates a new threshold question for the court, but it does not remove the court from the process.

*What would close it:* The legislation would need to grant the minister the power to conclusively determine whether a matter falls under 'merits' or 'legality', a provision which would be a direct and likely unsuccessful challenge to the separation of powers.

### 2. A challenge will be brought on grounds of procedural impropriety or a breach of natural justice, bypassing the new framework entirely.

**Mechanism:** `PROCEDURAL_SURVIVAL`

**Rests on:** Common law principles of natural justice (e.g., the right to a fair hearing, the rule against bias), which are considered a fundamental requirement of the rule of law (see Ridge v Baldwin [1964]).

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan states it will be 'specifically excluding review on grounds of common-law irrationality for that power'.

**⚠⚠ **the plan makes it worse**** — By explicitly naming and ousting only 'irrationality', the plan invites the application of the maxim 'expressio unius est exclusio alterius'. A court will conclude that Parliament considered which common law grounds to exclude and deliberately chose to leave others, such as procedural fairness, untouched. This strengthens, rather than weakens, the availability of review on those grounds.

*What would close it:* The legislation would need to explicitly oust review for breach of natural justice and all other heads of procedural impropriety, a step which even recent targeted ouster clauses (like in the Judicial Review and Courts Act 2022) have avoided.

### 3. In a case engaging fundamental rights, a court will apply the principle of legality to find that the statute is not sufficiently explicit to oust the court's jurisdiction to protect those rights.

**Mechanism:** `DISAPPLICATION`

**Rests on:** The principle of legality, which holds that Parliament must use express and unambiguous language to abrogate fundamental common law rights (R v Secretary ofState for the Home Department, ex p Simms [2000]).

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The proposal's silence on fundamental rights. It focuses on the distinction between merits and legality, and the ground of irrationality, but does not state that this ouster applies even where fundamental common law rights (like liberty or access to justice) are infringed.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan is drafted to address a general problem of review on the merits, not the specific, constitutionally-sensitive area of fundamental rights. A court will presume Parliament does not intend to override such rights without making its intention crystal clear. The current drafting does not meet that high bar.

*What would close it:* The legislation would need to state, in express terms, that the exclusion of review applies notwithstanding any infringement of rights recognised at common law.

### 4. A challenge will be brought under the Human Rights Act 1998, arguing the decision is incompatible with a Convention right, making the domestic law definitions of 'merits' and 'legality' irrelevant.

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** Section 6 of the Human Rights Act 1998, which makes it unlawful for a public authority to act in a way which is incompatible with a Convention right.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The proposal makes no mention of the Human Rights Act 1998 or its relationship with the new statutory scheme.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan is entirely focused on common law judicial review. It does not amend or disapply the Human Rights Act 1998. A claimant can therefore bring a parallel claim under the HRA, which provides a distinct statutory route for challenging the lawfulness of executive action. The court's task under the HRA (e.g., assessing proportionality) is different and is not touched by the proposal.

*What would close it:* The legislation would need to explicitly disapply or modify the application of the Human Rights Act 1998 in relation to decisions made under the new power, which would be a major and highly controversial legislative step.
