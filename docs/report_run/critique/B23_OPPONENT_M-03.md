### 1. The Committee's findings will be deemed legally irrelevant to statutory interpretation.

**Mechanism:** `NARROW_READING`

**Rests on:** The constitutional separation of powers and established case law (e.g., the principles underlying Pepper v Hart) which holds that parliamentary proceedings are generally inadmissible for interpreting statutes. The courts' position, noted in the proposer's research, is that 'it is for the courts, not a select committee, to decide questions of law'.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan empowers the Committee to 'recommend to Parliament statutory amendments or resolutions'. This wording confines the Committee's output to the political sphere, as advice to the legislature, not as a legally binding interpretative aid for the judiciary.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan creates a political process for legislative change but does not alter the existing rules of statutory interpretation. A court will continue to interpret the law as enacted, and will treat the committee's reports as part of the pre-legislative political process, irrelevant to the judicial task of interpreting the statute as it stands.

*What would close it:* The Bill would need to enact a new, radical rule of statutory interpretation that explicitly directs courts to treat the Committee's post-hoc reports as authoritative statements of legislative intent, overriding the enacted text. This would be constitutionally unprecedented.

### 2. The judiciary's exclusive role in interpreting law will be asserted as a fundamental constitutional principle, immune from interference by a parliamentary committee.

**Mechanism:** `ENTRENCHED_ELSEWHERE`

**Rests on:** The common law doctrines of the rule of law and the separation of powers. Cases like R (Privacy International) v Investigatory Powers Tribunal [2019] UKSC 22 affirm the judiciary's role in upholding the rule of law, even against statutory text, a principle that would be extended to protect the core judicial function of interpretation from political oversight.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The mandate for the Committee to 'specifically assess the judiciary's interpretation of constitutional statutes against original parliamentary intent' is a direct challenge to the judiciary's constitutional role as the independent and final arbiter of legal meaning.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The proposal establishes a mechanism for political accountability but does not, and likely cannot, legislate away the judiciary's constitutional function. The courts will maintain that while Parliament can change the law for the future, a committee cannot dictate the interpretation of existing law, as that is an exclusively judicial function protected by the separation of powers.

*What would close it:* The plan would require a clear and explicit statutory provision subordinating the judicial function of interpretation to the findings of a parliamentary committee, which would provoke a constitutional crisis and likely be resisted by the courts as fundamentally unlawful.

### 3. The requirement for judges to give evidence on their reasoning will be declined as unconstitutional.

**Mechanism:** `DISAPPLICATION`

**Rests on:** The constitutional principle of judicial independence. While parliamentary committees have broad powers to summon witnesses, a powerful convention prevents compelling judges to account to Parliament for their judgments. This is the reciprocal aspect of the sub judice rule.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The action to mandate that the review 'requir[es] the Supreme Court to provide written submissions and oral testimony on its interpretative methodologies' directly infringes on judicial independence.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The Bill can assert this power, but it cannot guarantee its enforcement. The senior judiciary would almost certainly refuse to comply on constitutional grounds, stating that they are accountable through the appeals process and their published reasons, not through political interrogation. Any attempt to compel them would be seen as an attack on the separation of powers.

*What would close it:* The Bill would need to include unprecedented and explicit enforcement powers against the judiciary, such as contempt proceedings, which would be politically and constitutionally explosive.

### 4. The court's interpretative approach will be re-grounded in common law constitutionalism, making statutory 'intent' secondary.

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** Common law principles of legality, rationality, and procedural fairness, and fundamental rights at common law (e.g., R v Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex p Simms [2000] 2 AC 115). The court can assert that its duty is not just to Parliament's 'intent' but to interpret statutes in conformity with these deeper constitutional principles.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The entire proposal is premised on holding the judiciary to account against 'original parliamentary intent'. This creates the very conflict that allows the court to assert that its primary duty is to the rule of law and fundamental principles, which it will presume Parliament does not intend to override without the clearest possible words.

**⚠⚠ **the plan makes it worse**** — By creating a political mechanism to enforce 'parliamentary intent', the plan forces the judiciary to articulate more clearly that its interpretative methodologies are grounded in the common law constitution, not just in subservience to Parliament. This could accelerate the very 'constitutional guardian' self-conception the proposal aims to curb, as the court positions itself as the defender of fundamental principles against political interference.

*What would close it:* The Bill would need to include a 'notwithstanding' clause that explicitly ousts the application of all common law constitutional principles when interpreting the target statutes, a measure that would be seen as tyrannical.
