### 1. The power to remove decision-makers for 'policy divergence' will be disapplied by the courts for any body exercising judicial or quasi-judicial functions.

**Mechanism:** `DISAPPLICATION`

**Rests on:** The constitutional principle of judicial independence, the rule of law, and Article 6 of the ECHR. Precedent for such a limitation is found in the Public Bodies Act 2011, s.7.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> Draft and introduce an 'Arm's-Length Body Accountability Bill' that establishes a unified statutory framework... including provisions for removal based on performance or policy divergence.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan's ambition for a 'unified' framework fails to account for the fundamental constitutional distinction between administrative bodies and those with judicial functions. The courts will not permit ministerial removal for 'policy divergence' to apply to, for example, tribunal members, as this would be a direct assault on judicial independence. The plan contains no language to address this.

*What would close it:* Explicitly and successfully creating a new legislative settlement for judicial independence that subordinates it to ministerial direction, which would be constitutionally revolutionary and likely fail in Parliament and the courts.

### 2. The power in the new Act to 'supersede' founding statutes via secondary legislation will be read down to prevent the removal of fundamental protections or procedures.

**Mechanism:** `NARROW_READING`

**Rests on:** The principle of legality (R v Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex p Simms [2000] 2 AC 115) and judicial reluctance to give full effect to broad Henry VIII clauses that impinge on fundamental rights or constitutional principles.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> Pass an 'Accountability of Public Bodies Act' to create a standing power for ministers to reform ALBs via secondary legislation, superseding the fragmented founding statutes.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan assumes this power will be absolute. The courts, however, will presume that Parliament did not intend to authorise the executive to override fundamental principles or rights via statutory instrument unless the parent Act uses 'express language or necessary implication'. The core protections in many founding statutes will therefore survive attempts to remove them via secondary legislation.

*What would close it:* The new Act would need to contain exceptionally clear and specific language authorising the abrogation of specific rights or principles via secondary legislation, making the political passage of the bill far more difficult.

### 3. Replacing statutory accountability mechanisms with ministerial discretion will not oust the jurisdiction of the courts, but will instead channel challenges into judicial review, potentially on stronger grounds.

**Mechanism:** `PROCEDURAL_SURVIVAL`

**Rests on:** The inherent supervisory jurisdiction of the High Court and foundational cases on the limits of ouster clauses, such as Anisminic Ltd v Foreign Compensation Commission [1969] 2 AC 147.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The entire plan, which seeks to replace statutory frameworks with a system of ministerial oversight, review, and removal powers.

**⚠⚠ **the plan makes it worse**** — The plan removes established, often technical, statutory appeal routes and replaces them with high-stakes, politically-motivated decisions (e.g., removal for 'policy divergence', abolition by ministerial order). These decisions are prime targets for judicial review on grounds of irrationality, procedural impropriety, or illegality. The proposal inadvertently makes the courts, rather than Parliament or a specialist body, the primary forum for challenging ALB decisions.

*What would close it:* An explicit and effective ouster clause in the new Act attempting to exclude judicial review entirely, which would be constitutionally contentious and likely to be read down or ignored by the courts following the Supreme Court's judgment in R (Privacy International) v Investigatory Powers Tribunal [2019] UKSC 22.

### 4. The 'unified statutory framework' will not apply to ALBs whose functions are devolved, unless Legislative Consent Motions are sought and granted.

**Mechanism:** `DISAPPLICATION`

**Rests on:** The Scotland Act 1998, Government of Wales Act 2006, Northern Ireland Act 1998, and the Sewel Convention.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The proposal's undifferentiated scope, implied by the action to 'identify all existing ALBs and categorize them' without any mention of devolved competence.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan is silent on devolution. It presumes a UK-wide competence that does not exist for many ALBs operating in areas like justice, environment, or education in Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland. Any attempt to impose the framework on these bodies without the consent of the devolved legislatures would be ultra vires.

*What would close it:* The plan would need to explicitly state its limited application to England-only or reserved matters, or detail a process for securing Legislative Consent Motions from all devolved legislatures.
