### 1. The powers in the new Act will be read down so as not to apply to any body exercising a judicial or quasi-judicial function, preserving their independence.

**Mechanism:** `NARROW_READING`

**Rests on:** The constitutional principle of judicial independence, affirmed in the Constitutional Reform Act 2005, and the principle of legality which presumes Parliament does not intend to override fundamental principles by general words.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan to create a 'unified statutory framework' with 'provisions for removal based on performance or policy divergence' for 'all ALBs', without any specific carve-out for the judiciary or tribunals.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan uses general language. To override a fundamental constitutional principle like judicial independence requires express, unambiguous statutory wording. The current draft contains no such wording, so the courts will apply the principle of legality and assume Parliament did not intend to allow Ministers to remove judicial office-holders for 'policy divergence'.

*What would close it:* The Act would need to contain express words stating that its powers apply notwithstanding the constitutional principle of judicial independence and the duties under the Constitutional Reform Act 2005.

### 2. The new Act's general powers will be held ineffective to override the specific statutory independence granted to key economic or expert regulators in their own founding Acts.

**Mechanism:** `NARROW_READING`

**Rests on:** The legal maxim 'generalia specialibus non derogant' (general things do not derogate from special things), and the principle of legality. Courts will presume a later general Act does not implicitly repeal an earlier specific one.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan to create a 'standing power for ministers to reform ALBs via secondary legislation, superseding the fragmented founding statutes'.

**◐ partly closed** — The plan states its intention to supersede founding statutes, but proposes to do so via secondary legislation. Courts are highly likely to find that a general power to make secondary legislation is insufficient to override the core purpose of primary legislation, such as the statutory independence of a major regulator. The parent Act would need to be exceptionally specific to authorise this, and the current plan lacks that detail.

*What would close it:* The new primary Act would need to explicitly amend or repeal the specific independence-granting sections of the target bodies' founding statutes, rather than relying on a general, delegated power.

### 3. The new Act's powers will be interpreted as being subject to the same constraints as the Public Bodies Act 2011, preventing the removal of 'necessary protections' or interference with functions that must be exercised independently of ministers (e.g. enforcement against the Crown).

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** The established statutory limits in the Public Bodies Act 2011 (ss. 7 & 8), which will be argued to represent a settled Parliamentary view on the proper limits of executive power in this area. This view will be used as an interpretive guide to the new Act.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The stated approach to 'Utilise and extend the Public Bodies Act 2011'. This invites a direct comparison and suggests the new Act is an evolution of the old, not a complete break from its principles.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — By framing the new Act as an extension of the 2011 Act, the proposal invites the courts to read it in that context. Without an express clause in the new Act repudiating the safeguards in the 2011 Act, it will be argued that Parliament intended them to continue to apply in principle. The plan does not mention repealing or disapplying these safeguards.

*What would close it:* The new Act must contain an express provision stating that the limitations contained in sections 7 and 8 of the Public Bodies Act 2011 do not apply to orders made under the new powers.

### 4. The proposal to use secondary legislation for reform will be challenged on the basis that it is an unconstitutional attempt to bypass the proper scrutiny of primary legislation for significant changes to the powers of the state and the rights of the subject.

**Mechanism:** `OTHER` — Constitutional challenge to the use of secondary legislation.

**Rests on:** The constitutional principle that significant constitutional changes require primary legislation (a 'Henry VIII clause' challenge). This rests on common law principles regarding the separation of powers and Parliamentary sovereignty, as seen in cases like R (Privacy International) v Investigatory Powers Tribunal.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The core of the plan: 'Pass an 'Accountability of Public Bodies Act' to create a standing power for ministers to reform ALBs via secondary legislation'.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan explicitly relies on secondary legislation to achieve its aims. This is its central procedural weakness. The more constitutionally significant the ALB (e.g. a body protecting rights or with judicial functions), the more likely a court is to find that its abolition or fundamental reform cannot be achieved by ministerial order and requires a new Act of Parliament.

*What would close it:* The plan would have to abandon the use of secondary legislation for significant reforms and commit to using primary legislation for each major change, which defeats the proposal's entire purpose of creating a 'single, powerful statutory mechanism'.
