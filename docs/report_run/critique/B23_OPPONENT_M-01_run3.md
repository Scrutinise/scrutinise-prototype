### 1. ECHR rights remain enforceable in Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland because they are embedded in the devolution statutes, which the proposal cannot easily amend.

**Mechanism:** `ENTRENCHED_ELSEWHERE`

**Rests on:** Scotland Act 1998 (s.29, s.57), Northern Ireland Act 1998 (s.6, s.24), Government of Wales Act 2006 (s.81, s.108).

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan's intention to "make necessary consequential amendments to other legislation, including the devolution acts."

**◐ partly closed** — The plan correctly identifies that the devolution acts must be amended, but it mischaracterises this as a 'consequential' amendment. Changing the human rights limits on devolved power is a fundamental constitutional change to the devolution settlements, likely requiring legislative consent which will be withheld. Overriding the devolved legislatures would provoke a constitutional crisis, making the plan politically and legally impracticable as drafted.

*What would close it:* The Bill would need to contain express words overriding the relevant sections of the devolution acts, notwithstanding the Sewel convention.

### 2. The Repeal and Denunciation Act will be disapplied in Northern Ireland to the extent it diminishes rights protected by the Good Friday Agreement, as guaranteed by the Windsor Framework.

**Mechanism:** `DISAPPLICATION`

**Rests on:** Article 2 of the Windsor Framework and the precedent set in Re NIHRC and JR295's Applications [2024] NIKB 44.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan's action to "make necessary consequential amendments to other legislation, including the devolution acts."

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan treats the Northern Ireland Act 1998 as ordinary domestic legislation. It ignores that its human rights provisions are underpinned by an international treaty (the GFA) and now have a specific, judicially-enforced guarantee against diminution via the Windsor Framework. The High Court in Belfast has already disapplied primary legislation on this basis. The plan contains no mechanism to overcome this, meaning the repeal of the HRA would likely be declared of no effect in Northern Ireland.

*What would close it:* This route is extremely difficult to close without breaching the Windsor Framework. It would require, at minimum, an express statutory provision directing courts to ignore Article 2 of the Framework, which would be a clear breach of international law with profound legal and political consequences.

### 3. Repealing the HRA will revive and strengthen common law constitutional rights, with courts developing protections analogous to the ECHR but without the statutory constraints of the HRA.

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** The principle of legality and common law constitutionalism, as articulated in cases like R (UNISON) v Lord Chancellor [2017] UKSC 51 and R (Privacy International) v Investigatory Powers Tribunal [2019] UKSC 22.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The proposal's entire premise is that repealing the HRA and denouncing the ECHR removes a source of law. It has no provision for what replaces it.

**⚠⚠ **the plan makes it worse**** — The plan removes the HRA, which channelled and constrained rights adjudication through a specific statutory framework. This creates a vacuum that the common law will fill. Courts will be invited to develop domestic rights protections, potentially mirroring the ECHR but without the HRA's textual limits or its deference mechanisms (like s.3 and s.4). The government will face similar legal challenges but on a less predictable and potentially more robust legal basis, defeating the objective of restoring political 'last word'.

*What would close it:* The Repeal Bill would need to include a clause explicitly ousting the jurisdiction of the courts to develop common law rights in the areas previously covered by the HRA, a move that would face significant constitutional challenge as a breach of the rule of law.

### 4. The UK's denunciation of the ECHR will be read down by the courts to apply only prospectively, leaving all rights and obligations accrued before the withdrawal date fully intact and enforceable.

**Mechanism:** `NARROW_READING`

**Rests on:** The common law presumption against retrospective legislation that removes vested rights, and Article 58(2) of the ECHR itself.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The action to "conduct a cross-departmental audit to amend all domestic legislation, policies, and guidance reliant on the HRA/ECHR, and issue transitional guidance for the justice system."

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan's focus on 'transitional guidance' and amending legislation shows it is focused on the future legal landscape. It lacks any clear provision to retrospectively extinguish rights that vested in individuals under the HRA/ECHR regime prior to the date of withdrawal. Courts will apply a strong presumption that the repeal does not have this effect unless the statute uses exceptionally clear language, which is not mentioned in the plan. This leaves a vast legacy of ECHR-based claims and interpretations in the legal system.

*What would close it:* The Repeal Bill would need to include an explicit and unambiguous clause stating that the repeal and denunciation extinguish all pre-existing causes of action and interpretive effects of the HRA and ECHR, a provision that would be highly contentious.
