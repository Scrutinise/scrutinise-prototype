### 1. The Prerogative Snap-Back: Reviving Common Law Constraints

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** The common law principle that prerogative powers are reviewable and must be exercised in accordance with fundamental constitutional principles (CCSU v Minister for the Civil Service [1985] AC 374; R (Miller) v The Prime Minister [2019] UKSC 41).

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> Draft and introduce a Government Bill to repeal Part 1 of CRAG 2010, formally abolishing the Civil Service Commission and the statutory requirement for merit-based appointment.

**⚠⚠ **the plan makes it worse**** — The plan's core action—repealing the statute—removes a clear, albeit restrictive, framework and throws the basis for civil service management back onto the Royal Prerogative. This prerogative power is not a blank slate; it is governed by judicially-enforceable constitutional conventions of impartiality and merit. By repealing the modern statute, the plan inadvertently strengthens the argument that these older, more fundamental common law principles are the default constitutional position, providing a stronger, less defined basis for a court to strike down a politicised appointment.

*What would close it:* The new Bill would need an explicit clause stating that appointments may be made without regard to the common law principles of merit or impartiality, and that the exercise of this power is non-justiciable on those grounds. This would be a constitutionally explosive 'ouster clause'.

### 2. Unfair Dismissal as a Constitutional Check

**Mechanism:** `PROCEDURAL_SURVIVAL`

**Rests on:** The Employment Rights Act 1996 and the common law of contract.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The Bill will create a new statutory framework vesting appointment and removal powers for all Senior Civil Service roles in the relevant Secretary of State.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan focuses exclusively on the public law power to appoint and remove, but is silent on the private law consequences. Senior civil servants are employees. A new power of removal does not oust their statutory right to claim unfair dismissal. A tribunal would scrutinise the reason and procedure for any dismissal, and a removal for political disagreement or for giving impartial advice would almost certainly be found unfair, leading to compensation and significant political damage. This creates a powerful practical deterrent against using the new powers.

*What would close it:* The Bill would need to explicitly exclude Senior Civil Servants from the protections of the Employment Rights Act 1996, making them a class of employee dismissible at will without recourse to a tribunal.

### 3. The Devolution Veto

**Mechanism:** `ENTRENCHED_ELSEWHERE`

**Rests on:** The Sewel Convention, as recognised in the Scotland Act 2016 and Wales Act 2017, and the principle that the UK Civil Service is a reserved matter but its operation within devolved areas requires consent.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The Bill will create a new statutory framework vesting appointment and removal powers for all Senior Civil Service roles in the relevant Secretary of State.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan treats the Civil Service as a unitary institution controlled from Westminster. It is not. The Senior Civil Service serves devolved administrations. Altering the fundamental basis of appointment and tenure for officials who serve devolved ministers (e.g. the Permanent Secretary to the Scottish Government) would directly affect devolved functions. Under the Sewel Convention, the UK Parliament will not normally legislate on such matters without the consent of the devolved legislatures. Withholding consent would create a major constitutional conflict and could force the Bill to be amended to exclude the devolved administrations, fracturing the unified Civil Service the plan seeks to control.

*What would close it:* The plan would need to either secure Legislative Consent Motions from all devolved legislatures in advance, or the Bill would need to explicitly carve out all civil servants working for devolved administrations from its provisions.

### 4. The Accounting Officer's Shield

**Mechanism:** `NARROW_READING`

**Rests on:** The constitutional role of the Accounting Officer as personally accountable to the Public Accounts Committee (PAC) for the regularity and propriety of public spending, as set out in HM Treasury's 'Managing Public Money'.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> HM Treasury will revise 'Managing Public Money' to transfer Accounting Officer responsibilities from Permanent Secretaries to Ministers, concentrating financial accountability with the power of appointment.

**◐ partly closed** — While Treasury can revise 'Managing Public Money', it cannot unilaterally abolish a core constitutional convention of parliamentary accountability. The PAC would almost certainly refuse to recognise ministers as Accounting Officers, as ministers are already accountable to Parliament on policy grounds and cannot be compelled to give evidence in the same way. The PAC would continue to summon the most senior official. This creates a situation where the minister is the nominal Accounting Officer but the Permanent Secretary remains the de facto one, retaining significant leverage and a separate line of accountability to Parliament that ministerial appointment power cannot erase.

*What would close it:* This route could only be closed by securing the explicit agreement of the Public Accounts Committee and potentially a resolution of the House of Commons to fundamentally change its century-old system of financial scrutiny. This is a political and procedural challenge, not just a legal one.
