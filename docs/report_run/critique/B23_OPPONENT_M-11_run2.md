### 1. The Court of Appeal's jurisdiction to issue binding guideline judgments remains untouched, providing a complete bypass to the new approval mechanism.

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** The inherent common law jurisdiction of the Court of Appeal (Criminal Division) to issue guideline judgments, which was not ousted by the Coroners and Justice Act 2009.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The actions are limited to amending the statutory scheme for guidelines 'issued by the Sentencing Council' under the Coroners and Justice Act 2009. This leaves the separate, common-law-based jurisdiction of the Court of Appeal entirely unaffected.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan legislates against the statutory body (the Sentencing Council) but does not, and constitutionally could not easily, legislate to remove the inherent jurisdiction of the senior judiciary to develop the common law on sentencing. If the statutory route is blocked by a political veto, the courts will simply fill the vacuum with case law.

*What would close it:* Primary legislation explicitly ousting the jurisdiction of the Court of Appeal to issue guideline judgments, which would be a constitutional step of the highest significance and likely unachievable.

### 2. The Lord Chancellor's new power to reject guidelines is itself subject to judicial review, constrained by his statutory duty to uphold judicial independence.

**Mechanism:** `NARROW_READING`

**Rests on:** Section 3(1) of the Constitutional Reform Act 2005, which places a statutory duty on the Lord Chancellor to uphold the continued independence of the judiciary.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan creates a new statutory discretion for the Lord Chancellor. The action to 'Develop clear criteria and a public statement of reasons for the Lord Chancellor's approval or rejection' confirms this discretion is not absolute and provides the direct basis for a legal challenge.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan makes the Lord Chancellor's decision-making process more transparent, but in doing so it creates a clear target for judicial review. A court will read the new veto power as being constrained by the pre-existing, constitutional duty in the 2005 Act, meaning a veto exercised for purely political reasons against judicially-approved guidelines would be unlawful.

*What would close it:* An ouster clause attempting to prevent judicial review of the Lord Chancellor's decision. Even this would likely be read down by the courts in line with the principle of legality.

### 3. A Lord Chancellor's veto on a guideline creates a legal vacuum that the courts will fill with common law principles, potentially leading to less predictable and less constrained outcomes than the vetoed guideline.

**Mechanism:** `PROCEDURAL_SURVIVAL`

**Rests on:** The duty of sentencing courts to sentence according to law, which, in the absence of a statutory guideline, means applying common law principles established by precedent (case law).

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The entire plan is predicated on controlling the 'guideline issued by the Sentencing Council'. It has no mechanism to control what happens when no such guideline exists because of a veto.

**⚠⚠ **the plan makes it worse**** — The plan assumes that blocking a guideline is an end in itself. It is not. The justice system cannot stop. By vetoing a guideline developed by an expert body, the Lord Chancellor removes a structured, predictable framework and forces the courts to revert to developing principles on a case-by-case basis. This cedes control from the executive back to the judiciary, which is the opposite of the plan's intention.

*What would close it:* The plan would need to include a default provision specifying what sentencing principles apply if a guideline is vetoed. This would amount to the Lord Chancellor writing the guidelines himself, a politically and constitutionally fraught proposition.
