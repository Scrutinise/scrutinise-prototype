### 1. The 'reserve power' in Section 19 is a legal trap. Any attempt to use it outside of a genuine, catastrophic economic crisis would be quashed by the courts as an abuse of power, making the proposal's stated 'approach' impracticable.

**Mechanism:** `NARROW_READING`

**Rests on:** Judicial review of statutory powers, specifically the interpretation of jurisdictional facts like 'extreme economic circumstances'. Case law on ministerial discretion (e.g., Padfield v Minister of Agriculture) and the use of legislative history to confirm statutory purpose.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> THE APPROACH: Utilise the existing reserve power of the Treasury under Section 19 of the Bank of England Act 1998 to direct monetary policy in 'extreme economic circumstances'...

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan focuses on building a political case for new legislation. It does nothing to alter the high legal threshold of Section 19. By highlighting this power as an 'approach', the proposal invites opponents to demonstrate its severe limitations, thereby undermining the case for the more radical legislative option.

*What would close it:* The Bill would need to explicitly redefine 'extreme economic circumstances' to mean 'at the discretion of the Chancellor', which would expose the policy's true intent and face immense political opposition.

### 2. Repealing the 1998 Act does not automatically grant ministers control. It revives the Bank's common law duties and powers under its Charter, creating a new legal battleground where ministerial directives could be challenged as unlawful.

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** The Bank of England's Royal Charter and the common law duties of its directors. Also, the constitutional principle that prerogative powers are displaced by statute (R v Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex p Fire Brigades Union). Repealing the statute does not automatically revive the prerogative in its old form.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> ACTIONS: Draft a Bill to amend or repeal the relevant sections of the Bank of England Act 1998 that confer operational independence...

**⚠⚠ **the plan makes it worse**** — The plan assumes repealing the 1998 Act creates a legal vacuum for ministers to fill. Instead, it replaces a clear statutory framework with a complex and contestable common law arrangement. This hands opponents a stronger, less constrained legal argument that ministerial direction is an unlawful interference with the Bank's core, non-statutory duties.

*What would close it:* The new Bill would need to go beyond simple repeal and create an explicit, broad, and non-justiciable statutory power for the Chancellor to direct the Bank on all matters of monetary policy.

### 3. The UK's obligations under international agreements, particularly with the IMF, require an independent central bank as a condition of financial credibility and support. Repealing independence could be framed as a breach of these settled understandings, triggering international financial and diplomatic consequences.

**Mechanism:** `ENTRENCHED_ELSEWHERE`

**Rests on:** The UK's Articles of Agreement with the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the established body of international financial 'soft law' and best practice (e.g., Basel Committee principles) which presuppose central bank independence.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> THE DIAGNOSIS: The pivotal obstacle to reversing this is not legal but political...

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The diagnosis dismisses non-domestic obstacles. The plan contains no actions to mitigate the international financial and diplomatic fallout from abandoning a core principle of the global financial architecture. This leaves the proposal exposed to the argument that it violates the UK's international obligations and imperils its economic standing.

*What would close it:* The plan would need a comprehensive diplomatic and economic strategy to renegotiate the UK's position within the IMF and other international financial bodies, or to manage the consequences of being in breach of their core tenets.

### 4. Even with formal control, ministers will be forced to recreate a functionally independent advisory body to provide credible analysis, which will then be used by parliamentary committees to hold the minister to account. This effectively resurrects the MPC's function, just in a different room.

**Mechanism:** `PROCEDURAL_SURVIVAL`

**Rests on:** The established powers of Parliamentary Select Committees (e.g., the Treasury Committee) to scrutinise executive decisions, compel evidence from officials, and publish critical reports. The practical necessity for any Chancellor to have expert, defensible advice.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> ACTIONS: Establish new institutional arrangements within HM Treasury or Parliament to support the political decision-making process for monetary policy, including analytical capacity, advisory functions...

**◐ partly closed** — The plan acknowledges the need for new 'advisory functions' but frames this as supporting political control. It fails to recognise that this new body's advice will immediately become the benchmark against which the minister's 'political' decisions are judged by a powerful Treasury Committee. The procedure for independent scrutiny survives and attaches itself to the new advisory body, recreating the dynamic the proposal seeks to abolish.

*What would close it:* The plan would need to include measures to curtail the powers of the Treasury Select Committee to scrutinise the new advisory body or the Chancellor's decisions, which would be constitutionally explosive.
