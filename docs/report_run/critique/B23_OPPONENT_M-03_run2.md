### 1. The committee's power to compel testimony from the judiciary on its reasoning will be resisted as a breach of judicial independence and the separation of powers, and the courts will not enforce it.

**Mechanism:** `DISAPPLICATION`

**Rests on:** The constitutional principle of the separation of powers, inherent in the Constitutional Reform Act 2005, and the principle of judicial independence. Also, the principle of comity between the branches of government, as articulated in jurisprudence concerning Parliamentary privilege and judicial deference.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> requiring the Supreme Court to provide written submissions and oral testimony on its interpretative methodologies and their evolution.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The Bill can assert a power to 'require' testimony, but it provides no mechanism to overcome the fundamental constitutional objection that will be raised by the judiciary. The courts would refuse to enforce a summons against senior judges on such a matter, viewing it as an improper intrusion by the legislature into the judicial function. The plan mistakes a legislative statement for a constitutional reality.

*What would close it:* The Bill would need to contain an unprecedented and explicit clause overriding the principle of judicial independence and providing a statutory enforcement mechanism that is not reliant on the courts, which is constitutionally almost inconceivable.

### 2. The committee's 'assessment' of original parliamentary intent will be deemed legally inadmissible and irrelevant by the courts when they perform their function of statutory interpretation.

**Mechanism:** `NARROW_READING`

**Rests on:** The established legal doctrine that parliamentary intent is an objective concept to be found in the statutory text, not in the subjective views of ministers or subsequent committees (Pepper v Hart being a very narrow exception). The proposer's own research notes 'The opinion of a parliamentary committee will generally be irrelevant to the issues before a court'.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> Mandate that the new Joint Committee's review specifically assesses the judiciary's interpretation of constitutional statutes against original parliamentary intent...

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan creates a body to produce political opinions on the law. It does not, and cannot, alter the established rules of statutory interpretation applied by the courts. A court will continue to look to the words of the statute, not the later report of a select committee. The committee's findings will have no legal weight and will not bind the judiciary.

*What would close it:* The Bill would have to enact a new, radical rule of statutory interpretation, making the committee's reports an admissible, or even binding, source for construing statutes. This would fundamentally alter the judicial function.

### 3. The Supreme Court's perceived role as a 'constitutional guardian' will continue untouched through its development of common law constitutional principles, which are outside the proposal's scope.

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** The doctrine of common law constitutionalism, which establishes fundamental rights and principles (such as the principle of legality or access to justice) that were not created by statute and are used by courts to interpret statutes. Key authorities include R v Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex p Simms [2000] 2 AC 115.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The proposal's focus is exclusively on the 'judicial interpretation of constitutional statutes' and the Constitutional Reform Act 2005.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The proposal is targeted at statutory interpretation only. It provides no mechanism to scrutinise or constrain the judiciary's role in developing and applying fundamental principles of the common law. The courts can therefore continue to exercise a 'guardian' role by, for example, reading statutes as subject to common law rights, a source of judicial power the plan never touches.

*What would close it:* The plan would need to be expanded to codify and limit the application of common law constitutional principles, a far more complex and controversial undertaking.

### 4. The committee, as a statutory body, will itself be subject to the supervisory jurisdiction of the courts through judicial review.

**Mechanism:** `PROCEDURAL_SURVIVAL`

**Rests on:** The inherent judicial review jurisdiction of the High Court to ensure public bodies act lawfully, within their powers, and fairly. The principle that even statutory powers are subject to the rule of law, as affirmed in cases like R (Privacy International) v Investigatory Powers Tribunal [2019] UKSC 22.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> Draft and introduce a 'Constitutional Scrutiny Bill' establishing a joint committee of both Houses of Parliament with a mandate...

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — By creating the committee in statute, the plan makes it a public body subject to judicial review. If the committee acts outside its mandate, irrationally, or with procedural impropriety, its actions could be challenged in court. This creates the ironic possibility of the Supreme Court ultimately being asked to rule on the lawfulness of the committee established to scrutinise it.

*What would close it:* The Bill would need to include a clear and comprehensive ouster clause attempting to exclude the jurisdiction of the courts, but such clauses are interpreted with extreme hostility by the judiciary and are rarely successful.
