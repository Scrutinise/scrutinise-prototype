## M-01 — Human Rights Act 1998 and the European Convention on Human Rights

**What Lex chose.** Initiate immediate and comprehensive legislative action to repeal the Human Rights Act 1998 and concurrently trigger Article 58 of the ECHR for denunciation.

*Why:* This approach directly addresses the user's core complaint about judicial supremacy by removing both the domestic and international legal bases for ECHR enforcement, aligning with their stated desire for a decisive and complete break.

**Alternative 1.** Repeal the Human Rights Act 1998 but remain a signatory to the ECHR.

*The case for it:* This alternative would restore parliamentary supremacy in domestic law by removing the HRA's incorporation of the ECHR, while still allowing the UK to benefit from ECHR membership, such as participation in a common European legal framework and the ability to influence future interpretations of the Convention.

**Alternative 2.** Introduce a new Bill of Rights that re-enacts some ECHR rights domestically, without remaining subject to the Strasbourg court.

*The case for it:* This option, advocated by Lord Sumption, would allow the UK to define its own set of fundamental rights and their scope, tailored to domestic constitutional traditions, while still providing a framework for rights protection, but free from the direct oversight of the Strasbourg court and the interpretive evolution of the ECHR.

*The case for it:* This alternative could be seen as the root cause because it highlights the external influence on UK jurisprudence, which some argue undermines national sovereignty even if the HRA were modified.

## M-02 — Equality Act 2010

*Build v2 `d5d23c9f-f058-49e8-ac61-2ea9bf041980`. 9 alternatives across 4 decision points.*

### approach:scope  ·  field `chosenApproach`  ·  **unresolved**

**What Lex chose.** Abolish the Public Sector Equality Duty (PSED) entirely while retaining substantive anti-discrimination protections.

*Why:* This approach directly addresses the user's core complaint that the PSED's open-ended nature and judicial enforcement distorts public administration's purpose, by removing the specific statutory obligation that creates this dynamic, while maintaining essential protections against discrimination.

**Alternative 1.** Replace the PSED with an outcome-based equality duty.

*The case for it:* An outcome-based duty could provide a clearer, more measurable framework for public bodies to advance equality without the process-driven 'due regard' obligation, potentially reducing judicial intervention in procedural matters and focusing efforts on tangible results.

**Alternative 2.** Amend the PSED to limit judicial oversight and clarify ministerial accountability.

*The case for it:* Amending the PSED could retain a proactive equality duty while addressing the specific issue of judicial overreach by defining more precisely the scope of 'due regard' and explicitly vesting final accountability for compliance with ministers, rather than courts.

### diagnosis:pivotalObstacle  ·  field `pivotalObstacle`  ·  **unresolved**

**What Lex chose.** The pivotal obstacle is the statutory mandate of the PSED itself, coupled with the judicial interpretation of 'due regard', which creates a legal and cultural incentive for public bodies to prioritise demonstrating compliance to courts over delivering core services, a situation from which those benefiting from the compliance industry or a reduced accountability for service outcomes have little incentive to deviate.

*Why:* This obstacle directly addresses why the problem persists: it's not just the law, but the way it's enforced and the resulting incentives for various parties, including those who benefit from the current compliance-heavy approach, making it resistant to non-legislative solutions.

**Alternative 1.** The lack of ministerial accountability for PSED outcomes, as courts rather than elected officials hold the ultimate power to determine compliance.

*The case for it:* This alternative highlights the democratic deficit, arguing that the core issue is the erosion of ministerial responsibility and the transfer of power to the judiciary, which elected officials are unwilling or unable to reclaim.

**Alternative 2.** The entrenched culture within public bodies that prioritises process compliance and risk aversion over substantive service delivery, making internal reform difficult.

*The case for it:* This suggests the obstacle is primarily internal and cultural, meaning that even if the law changed, the ingrained habits and incentives within the civil service would continue to drive a compliance-first approach until the culture itself is fundamentally altered.

### diagnosis:rootCause  ·  field `rootCause`  ·  **unresolved**

**What Lex chose.** The Public Sector Equality Duty (PSED) imposes a broad, anticipatory 'due regard' obligation on public authorities, requiring them to proactively consider equality issues in all functions (primary-acts-2000plus:ukpga/2010/15:section-149).

*Why:* This cause is chosen as the root because it is the foundational legal requirement from which the other issues, such as judicial oversight and compliance culture, directly flow. Without the PSED, the subsequent problems of its interpretation and enforcement would not exist.

**Alternative 1.** The open-ended nature of 'due regard' means that courts, rather than ministers, ultimately determine whether public bodies have met their equality obligations, leading to a risk-averse, compliance-focused approach.

*The case for it:* This could be considered the root cause because it highlights the mechanism by which the PSED's broadness translates into a compliance culture, directly linking the legal framework to the operational problem of misplaced priorities.

**Alternative 2.** A culture has developed within public bodies that rewards demonstrating compliance with process over achieving substantive service outcomes.

*The case for it:* This alternative focuses on the human element and incentives, arguing that even with a broad duty, a different culture could lead to better outcomes. It suggests that the institutional behaviour, rather than the law itself, is the primary driver of the problem.

## M-06 — The permanent, appointed civil service

*Build v2 `61ff9933-6d1c-4caf-8cad-a67614810475`. 9 alternatives across 4 decision points.*

### approach:mainLever  ·  field `chosenApproach`  ·  **unresolved**

**What Lex chose.** Empower Ministers with direct appointment and removal powers for senior civil servants.

*Why:* This approach directly addresses the proposer's core complaint about the 'irremovable' nature of officials and the lack of ministerial control over who holds key positions, by giving Ministers the ultimate authority over personnel decisions. It aligns with the proposer's desire to reverse the permanent appointed model.

**Alternative 1.** Strengthen ministerial powers of direction and instruction over individual civil servants, without altering appointment mechanisms.

*The case for it:* This alternative would allow Ministers to issue binding instructions to officials and hold them accountable for compliance, without politicising the appointment process, thereby maintaining the impartiality of the civil service while increasing ministerial control.

**Alternative 2.** Introduce a 'cabinet' system, where each Minister appoints a small team of politically aligned policy advisors to key roles, working alongside a permanent civil service.

*The case for it:* This approach would provide Ministers with trusted political appointees to drive their agenda, offering a hybrid model that retains a permanent civil service for institutional memory and impartial advice while ensuring political direction at the top.

### diagnosis:pivotalObstacle  ·  field `pivotalObstacle`  ·  **unresolved**

**What Lex chose.** The deeply entrenched constitutional principle of ministerial responsibility, which places accountability with elected Ministers and underpins the permanent, impartial Civil Service, means there is little political will or legal precedent to shift direct accountability to individual officials or allow political appointments at senior levels.

*Why:* This obstacle directly addresses why the problem 'persists unsolved' despite calls for greater ministerial control. It highlights the systemic resistance to changing a core constitutional tenet that benefits the stability of government, even if it frustrates elected officials.

**Alternative 1.** The absence of a clear, developed legal case or political consensus for repealing Part 1 of the Constitutional Reform and Governance Act 2010, which formalised aspects of the existing civil service settlement.

*The case for it:* The user explicitly targets CRAG Part 1 as the statutory codification of the problem, and the lack of a legal case for its repeal (as noted in the orientation) is a concrete reason why the proposed solution has not gained traction.

**Alternative 2.** The perceived benefits of a permanent, impartial civil service, such as continuity, expertise, and non-partisanship, are highly valued across the political spectrum, making radical reform difficult.

*The case for it:* Many stakeholders, including parliamentary committees and government figures, consistently affirm the value of the current model (e.g., [pwdata-lords:daylord2024-11-28b:151]), creating a strong counter-argument against dismantling it.

*Why:* This doctrine is the fundamental legal and constitutional principle that dictates the relationship between Ministers and civil servants, explaining why direct accountability for officials is difficult to establish and why the permanent, impartial service persists.

*The case for it:* This is the direct mechanism that prevents Ministers from easily shaping their teams and is what the proposer explicitly objects to (the 'permanent appointed civil service model').
