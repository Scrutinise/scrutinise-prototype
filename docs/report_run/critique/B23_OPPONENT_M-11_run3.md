### 1. The Court of Appeal reasserts its common law jurisdiction to issue guideline judgments, creating a parallel, politics-proof source of sentencing law.

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** The inherent common law jurisdiction of the Court of Appeal (Criminal Division) to give guideline judgments.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The proposal's actions are focused exclusively on amending the Coroners and Justice Act 2009 to control the Sentencing Council. The proposer's own research confirms that 'the 2009 Act has not diminished the Court of Appeal's jurisdiction to promulgate judgments on sentencing principles, which bind sentencing courts'.

**⚠⚠ **the plan makes it worse**** — The plan creates the exact conditions for this to happen. If the Lord Chancellor vetoes a guideline on political grounds, the Court of Appeal can, and likely will, fill the vacuum by issuing its own guideline judgment. This judgment would be pure case law, unconstrained by the statutory process and immune to the political veto, leaving the executive with less influence than before.

*What would close it:* Primary legislation explicitly ousting or subordinating the Court of Appeal's common law jurisdiction to issue guideline judgments, which would be constitutionally unprecedented and fiercely resisted.

### 2. The Lord Chancellor's veto power is judicially reviewed and read down to exclude purely political considerations, rendering it ineffective for its stated purpose.

**Mechanism:** `NARROW_READING`

**Rests on:** The principle of legality and the duty to uphold judicial independence under s.3(1) of the Constitutional Reform Act 2005.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The action to 'Develop clear criteria and a public statement of reasons for the Lord Chancellor's approval or rejection of guidelines' explicitly creates the basis for a judicial review challenge.

**◐ partly closed** — The plan attempts to manage this risk by creating criteria, but this makes the Lord Chancellor's reasoning justiciable. Any criteria or reasons deemed by a court to be based on pure political expediency, rather than the proper administration of justice, would be quashed as an unlawful exercise of the power, contrary to the purpose of the Act and the constitutional duty to uphold judicial independence.

*What would close it:* An explicit and watertight ouster clause preventing any judicial review of the Lord Chancellor's decision, which would be unlikely to succeed against a challenge based on fundamental constitutional principles.

### 3. The Lady Chief Justice uses her co-equal power of approval as a 'judicial shield', refusing to approve any guidelines until the Lord Chancellor ceases to exercise his veto on political grounds.

**Mechanism:** `DISAPPLICATION`

**Rests on:** The proposed statutory wording requiring approval from 'the Lord Chancellor and the Lady Chief Justice'.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan relies on the dual-approval mechanism in Clause 19 of the Sentencing Bill, which gives an identical power to both the political and judicial heads.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan assumes the Lady Chief Justice will consent to the politicisation of the process. It is far more likely she will use her co-equal veto to protect the Council's independence by refusing to approve any guidelines if the Lord Chancellor is seen to be blocking them for purely political reasons. This creates a stalemate that the Lord Chancellor cannot break, defeating the objective of imposing ministerial will.

*What would close it:* The mechanism would need to be changed from a dual-key system (where either can block) to one requiring a joint objection, a proposal the proposer's own research notes the government has already rejected.

### 4. The veto over the Council's annual business plan is found to have no legal consequence for the Council's core functions, rendering it a symbolic but ineffective power.

**Mechanism:** `NARROW_READING`

**Rests on:** The principle that where a statute is silent on the consequences of non-compliance, a court will not imply a consequence that frustrates the body's primary statutory purpose.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The proposer's own research states: 'The Sentencing Bill is silent on the consequences if the Lord Chancellor does not approve the business plan submitted by the Sentencing Council.'

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan is to secure passage of the Bill as is, but it does not address this legislative silence. A court would likely rule that a failure to approve a plan does not invalidate the Council's power to perform its core statutory functions under the 2009 Act. The veto would be an empty threat.

*What would close it:* The Bill would need to be amended to explicitly state that without an approved business plan, the Sentencing Council is prohibited from exercising specific functions, such as consulting on new guidelines or expending funds.
