### 1. Challenge the decision on the grounds that the new statutory procedures themselves were not followed correctly.

**Mechanism:** `PROCEDURAL_SURVIVAL`

**Rests on:** The text of the proposed new primary legislation itself.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The actions to "Establish a new, mandatory 'pre-decision review' mechanism" and "Introduce a statutory requirement for a 'statement of reasons'... explicitly linking the decision to the statutory 'merits' and 'legality' definitions."

**⚠⚠ **the plan makes it worse**** — The plan creates new, mandatory procedural requirements for the executive. A failure to comply with these statutory requirements (e.g., a flawed pre-decision review, a statement of reasons that fails to properly link the decision to the definitions) creates a new, statutory ground of judicial review. The proposal hands opponents a new set of procedural tripwires.

*What would close it:* Removing the mandatory procedural requirements, which would gut the proposal's claim to be a structured alternative to review.

### 2. Bypass the new statutory definitions entirely by bringing a claim under the Human Rights Act 1998.

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** Human Rights Act 1998, ss. 3, 4, and 6.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The entire proposal is silent on the Human Rights Act. It focuses on ousting common law grounds of review.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The proposal does not amend or disapply the HRA. A court would still be required under s.6 to determine if a public authority acted incompatibly with a Convention right. This would often involve a proportionality assessment, which is a form of merits review that the statutory definitions of 'merits' and 'legality' do not and cannot touch. The court would either read the new ouster down under s.3 or issue a declaration of incompatibility under s.4.

*What would close it:* Explicitly disapplying the Human Rights Act 1998 for decisions made under this specific power, a constitutionally explosive and likely impracticable step.

### 3. Argue that the decision is a nullity because it is based on a fundamental error of law or breach of natural justice that falls outside the new statutory definitions, and is therefore not a 'decision' at all for the purposes of the Act.

**Mechanism:** `NARROW_READING`

**Rests on:** The common law principle of legality and the line of authority from Anisminic Ltd v Foreign Compensation Commission [1969] to R (Privacy International) v Investigatory Powers Tribunal [2019].

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan to draft legislation that "explicitly defines the statutory grounds for a decision and states that these are the only grounds on which a decision may be judicially reviewed".

**◐ partly closed** — The proposal anticipates this line of attack and tries to counter it with explicit definitions. However, courts have consistently held that ousting the High Court's supervisory jurisdiction requires the most exceptionally clear statutory language. A court could still hold that certain errors (e.g., bad faith, improper purpose) are so fundamental that they render the decision a 'purported decision' or nullity, falling outside the protection of the ouster clause, regardless of the new definitions. The definitions themselves will become the new battleground for interpretation.

*What would close it:* Language that explicitly states that a decision is not to be questioned in any court on any ground, including for bad faith or fundamental breach of natural justice, and that the decision remains valid even if it is based on an error of law. This would be a direct and unprecedented challenge to the rule of law.

### 4. Argue that the ouster clause is ineffective because it infringes the fundamental common law constitutional right of access to justice.

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** The principle of legality and the constitutional right of access to justice identified in cases like R v Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex p Simms [2000] and R (UNISON) v Lord Chancellor [2017].

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The guiding policy's aim to "make the statutory language so explicit that for a court to set it aside would require it to directly challenge Parliament's authority".

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The proposal assumes parliamentary sovereignty is absolute. The courts have indicated that there may be common law constitutional principles that Parliament cannot legislate to override. An attempt to remove the High Court's inherent supervisory jurisdiction could be framed by a court as a violation of the rule of law which Parliament, on the orthodox view, cannot do. The court could hold that Parliament did not have the power to enact such a provision, or would require impossibly clear language to do so.

*What would close it:* This route is difficult to close legislatively as it rests on the judiciary's view of its own constitutional role and the nature of parliamentary sovereignty itself. It represents the ultimate constitutional backstop.
