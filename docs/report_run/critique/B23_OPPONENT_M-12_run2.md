### 1. Common law rights expand to fill the vacuum left by the Human Rights Act's repeal, creating a less predictable and potentially more powerful rights jurisprudence.

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** The doctrine of common law constitutionalism (e.g., R v Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex p Simms [2000] 2 AC 115; R (UNISON) v Lord Chancellor [2017] UKSC 51).

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan's entire focus is on repealing 'statutory provisions' and 'legislation', with no mention of the parallel development of common law rights.

**⚠⚠ **the plan makes it worse**** — By repealing the HRA, the plan removes the statutory framework that channels and, to an extent, constrains rights jurisprudence by reference to the ECHR. This invites the judiciary to develop fundamental common law rights from first principles, potentially leading to outcomes the proposer would find even more objectionable. The plan mistakes repealing the statute for repealing the underlying principles.

*What would close it:* The Act would need to contain unprecedented clauses attempting to prospectively oust the development of common law rights in specific areas, a move that would be seen as a direct attack on the judiciary and the rule of law.

### 2. The 'strong ouster clauses' are interpreted into near-irrelevance by the courts, preserving judicial review over the Act's implementation.

**Mechanism:** `NARROW_READING`

**Rests on:** The principle of legality and the Anisminic doctrine, as most recently affirmed in R (Privacy International) v Investigatory Powers Tribunal [2019] UKSC 22.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan states the Bill 'will include strong ouster clauses to limit judicial review of its provisions.'

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan assumes that including an ouster clause guarantees its effectiveness. Decades of jurisprudence show that courts will apply intense critical scrutiny to such clauses and interpret them, wherever possible, to preserve their jurisdiction. The plan contains no novel mechanism to overcome this established judicial resistance.

*What would close it:* The Act would need to contain language of unprecedented clarity and breadth, explicitly naming and seeking to abrogate the Anisminic principle for the purposes of the Act. Even this would provoke a constitutional crisis and its success would not be guaranteed.

### 3. The repeal of the Human Rights Act is rendered ineffective because the international pre-condition—renegotiation of the Belfast/Good Friday Agreement—is never met.

**Mechanism:** `ENTRENCHED_ELSEWHERE`

**Rests on:** The Belfast/Good Friday Agreement 1998, an international treaty which requires the incorporation of the ECHR into Northern Irish law.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan makes the repeal of the HRA 'contingent on that process' of treaty renegotiation.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan correctly identifies the obstacle but outsources the solution to a diplomatic process over which it has no control and which is almost certain to fail. By making the repeal contingent, it builds a kill-switch into its own legislation which will be operated by external opponents (the Republic of Ireland, the USA, Northern Irish parties), ensuring the HRA remains in force.

*What would close it:* The plan would have to abandon contingency and legislate to breach the treaty directly, accepting the severe diplomatic, political, and economic consequences.

### 4. The express override of the Sewel Convention is legally effective but politically catastrophic, triggering a constitutional crisis that halts the entire reform programme.

**Mechanism:** `OTHER` — The legal provision works as intended but triggers a political reaction that defeats the proposal's overall objective.

**Rests on:** The Scotland Act 2016 and Wales Act 2017, which place the Sewel Convention on a statutory footing, and the Supreme Court's judgment in R (Miller) v Secretary of State for Exiting the EU [2017] UKSC 5.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan states that for devolved matters, the Bill 'will include express provisions overriding the Sewel Convention.'

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan achieves a narrow legal victory at the cost of total strategic failure. While Parliament can legislate without consent, doing so on this scale would provoke an immediate and overwhelming political crisis with the devolved nations, likely leading to calls for independence referendums. This political fallout would derail the government's entire agenda, making the legal 'success' of the override a pyrrhic victory. The plan does not close the route of political defeat, which is the practical effect of this legal mechanism.

*What would close it:* Closing this would require securing the legislative consent of the devolved administrations, which is stated by the proposer's own research to be unobtainable.
