### 1. The Act's powers of removal and reform will be disapplied for any body exercising judicial or quasi-judicial functions, preserving their independence.

**Mechanism:** `DISAPPLICATION`

**Rests on:** The constitutional principle of the separation of powers and judicial independence, as reflected in the restrictions within the Public Bodies Act 2011 (s.7).

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan's actions to create a 'unified statutory framework' with 'provisions for removal based on performance or policy divergence' and to 'supersed[e] the fragmented founding statutes' are drafted as general powers, without the necessary carve-outs for the judiciary.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan makes no provision for exempting judicial or quasi-judicial bodies. A court will not permit a general statute to abrogate fundamental constitutional principles by implication. It will either imply a limitation disapplying the Act's powers to such bodies or read down the powers to prevent interference with judicial independence.

*What would close it:* The new Act would need to contain express words making clear that its powers apply to bodies exercising judicial functions, and explicitly repeal the specific statutory provisions that guarantee their independence. This would be constitutionally explosive.

### 2. The independence of bodies established or protected under international law will survive, as the new domestic Act will be read as subject to the UK's treaty obligations.

**Mechanism:** `ENTRENCHED_ELSEWHERE`

**Rests on:** The principle of legality, which presumes that Parliament does not intend to legislate in breach of the UK's international obligations unless it uses express and unambiguous language (R v Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex p Simms [2000] 2 AC 115).

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan is entirely domestic in focus, aiming to 'Pass an 'Accountability of Public Bodies Act' to create a standing power for ministers to reform ALBs via secondary legislation'. It is silent on its relationship with international law.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan does not address international obligations. A court will presume the new Act is intended to be compatible with the UK's treaty commitments. Any secondary legislation made under it that undermined the required independence of a body (e.g., the Information Commissioner's Office) would be ruled ultra vires the parent Act.

*What would close it:* The Act would need to include a 'notwithstanding' clause, explicitly authorising ministers to make orders that are incompatible with specified international treaties.

### 3. The new Act will be read as creating a parallel power for reform, not as extinguishing the specific powers, duties, and protections contained in each ALB's founding statute.

**Mechanism:** `NARROW_READING`

**Rests on:** The presumption against implied repeal, specifically the maxim 'generalia specialibus non derogant' (general things do not derogate from special things).

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan states the new Act will be 'superseding the fragmented founding statutes'. 'Superseding' is not a precise legal term like 'repealing', and this ambiguity creates the opening for interpretation.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — A court will seek to read the new general Act and the old specific Acts as compatible. It will likely interpret 'superseding' to mean the new Act provides an alternative mechanism for reform, but does not, by its own force, repeal the detailed provisions of the founding statutes. Those protections and duties will remain in force unless and until they are explicitly and lawfully addressed by secondary legislation under the new Act on a case-by-case basis.

*What would close it:* The new Act would need to include a schedule explicitly repealing the relevant sections of every founding statute it intends to replace, rather than relying on the ambiguous term 'superseding'.

### 4. The 'default presumption of abolition' will be neutered by reading the 'strict statutory criteria' for survival as a procedural checklist, allowing any Accounting Officer who follows the process to certify an ALB's continued existence.

**Mechanism:** `PROCEDURAL_SURVIVAL`

**Rests on:** The distinction between a substantive review of a decision's merits (which courts avoid) and a procedural review of the decision-making process (which is the core of judicial review).

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan mandates a review where an Accounting Officer 'personally certifies its case for arm's-length status against strict statutory criteria'. This frames the decision as a certification against criteria, not a broad discretionary judgment by the Minister.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan creates a reviewable process. A court will not second-guess the Accounting Officer's judgment on the merits, but it will uphold their decision as lawful so long as they can show they have considered the statutory criteria and provided a rational basis for their certification. The 'presumption of abolition' becomes legally irrelevant once the certification process is correctly completed, defeating the policy's intended rigour.

*What would close it:* The Act would need to remove the certification step and instead frame the decision as a pure, subjective discretionary power of the Minister (e.g., 'the Minister may abolish the body if they consider it appropriate'), which would be much harder to challenge.
