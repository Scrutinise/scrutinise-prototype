### 1. The grant condition's key terms will be read down by the courts to apply only to the most direct and explicit forms of opposition, leaving most advocacy work untouched.

**Mechanism:** `NARROW_READING`

**Rests on:** The common law principle of contractual interpretation that ambiguous terms restricting fundamental activities are construed narrowly against the drafter (contra proferentem).

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan's reliance on creating 'clear definitions of “campaigning activities” and “directly oppose”' in a template clause.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan assumes that these terms can be defined with sufficient legal precision to prevent challenge. This is a known area of legal difficulty. Any definition will have loopholes that a charity can exploit by framing its work as 'evidence-based policy development' or 'seeking to improve, not oppose'. The plan creates the very ambiguity it will be defeated by.

*What would close it:* Primary legislation defining the terms and explicitly limiting the scope of judicial interpretation, which would be highly contentious and difficult to draft.

### 2. The right to campaign is relocated to the charity's privately-raised funds, rendering the grant condition practically ineffective due to the fungibility of money.

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** The Charities Act 2011 and Charity Commission Guidance (CC9), which establish and protect a charity's right to campaign in furtherance of its objects.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The approach explicitly states it will be 'allowing charities to campaign using other funds'.

**⚠⚠ **the plan makes it worse**** — The plan explicitly concedes this route. By creating a formal distinction, it provides charities with a clear and government-approved roadmap for how to structure their finances to continue campaigning. The action for the Charity Commission to update its guidance will likely result in official advice on how to legally ring-fence funds, thereby strengthening, not closing, this route.

*What would close it:* A ban on any charity in receipt of any public funds from campaigning on any issue, which would be legally and politically unviable.

### 3. The mandatory imposition of the grant condition will be challenged via judicial review as a breach of the legitimate expectations established by the Government's own Compact with the third sector.

**Mechanism:** `ENTRENCHED_ELSEWHERE`

**Rests on:** The 'Compact', a long-standing agreement between the UK Government and the voluntary sector which commits the government to 'recognise and support the independence of the sector, including its right within the law to campaign... irrespective of any funding relationship'.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan mandates a 'standard, legally robust “anti-lobbying” clause for all grant agreements', removing departmental discretion and potentially breaching the spirit and letter of the Compact.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan operates at an administrative level (Treasury guidance, Cabinet Office circulars) and makes no attempt to amend or resile from the higher-level policy commitment in the Compact. A court could find that mandating the clause is an unlawful fettering of discretion that contradicts the government's own settled policy.

*What would close it:* The formal withdrawal from or renegotiation of the Compact, which would cause a significant political backlash from the entire third sector.

### 4. A charity can bypass the campaigning restriction entirely by challenging the government policy it opposes directly in court through judicial review.

**Mechanism:** `PROCEDURAL_SURVIVAL`

**Rests on:** The common law and statutory right to seek judicial review of government action (Senior Courts Act 1981).

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The proposal's focus on defining and prohibiting 'campaigning activities'.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — Litigation is not 'campaigning' in the ordinary sense that the clause will capture. Any attempt to draft the clause so broadly that it includes legal challenges would almost certainly be struck down by the courts as an unlawful ouster clause attempting to prevent access to justice. This legal route to challenging policy remains completely untouched.

*What would close it:* Primary legislation to severely restrict the standing of charities to bring judicial review claims, which would be a major and controversial constitutional change.

### 5. The grant condition will be challenged as an unlawful and disproportionate interference with a charity's right to freedom of expression under the Human Rights Act 1998.

**Mechanism:** `DISAPPLICATION`

**Rests on:** Article 10 of the European Convention on Human Rights (Freedom of Expression), as given effect by the Human Rights Act 1998.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The entire plan, which makes receipt of public funds conditional on a restriction of the recipient's freedom of expression.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan is silent on human rights. As a public authority, a grant-making body must act compatibly with Convention rights. A court would have to be satisfied that the restriction on speech is prescribed by law, pursues a legitimate aim, and is necessary and proportionate. The blanket, non-discretionary nature of the proposed mandatory clause makes it highly vulnerable to being found disproportionate, and therefore unlawful, in a specific case.

*What would close it:* Primary legislation that explicitly disapplies the Human Rights Act for this purpose, or repealing the Act itself.
