### 1. A court could rule that the supervisory jurisdiction of the High Court to correct errors of law is a fundamental constitutional principle derived from the common law, which cannot be removed by statute, however clearly worded.

**Mechanism:** `DISAPPLICATION`

**Rests on:** The doctrine of common law constitutionalism, drawing on dicta in cases like R (Jackson) v Attorney General [2005] UKHL 56 and the obiter comments of Lord Carnwath in R (Privacy International) v Investigatory Powers Tribunal [2019] UKSC 22.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The entire guiding policy, which is to 'make the statutory language so explicit that for a court to set it aside would require it to directly challenge Parliament's authority'. This frames the legislation as a direct test of the limits of parliamentary sovereignty, inviting the judiciary to define those limits.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan is designed to provoke this very confrontation, not avoid it. It rests on the assumption that parliamentary sovereignty is absolute and will prevail. It contains no mechanism to prevent a court from considering or accepting the contrary constitutional argument.

*What would close it:* This route is fundamental and likely cannot be closed by legislative drafting alone; it would require a constitutional shift or a definitive Supreme Court ruling in the government's favour on this specific point.

### 2. A court could interpret the new statutory definitions of 'merits' and 'legality' narrowly, finding that any given error of law by the minister takes the decision outside the statutory scheme altogether, making it a reviewable error of jurisdiction rather than a non-reviewable decision on the 'merits'.

**Mechanism:** `NARROW_READING`

**Rests on:** The principle established in Anisminic Ltd v Foreign Compensation Commission [1969] 2 AC 147 and affirmed in Privacy International, where courts presume Parliament does not intend to permit a public body to misinterpret the law under which it operates.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The action to 'introduc[e] statutory definitions of 'merits' and 'legality''. By creating new statutory terms, the plan creates new subjects for judicial interpretation. The fight will shift from the common law grounds of review to the meaning of the words in the new Act.

**◐ partly closed** — The plan anticipates this fight by creating a fast-track procedure for it, but this only speeds up the dispute, it doesn't prevent it. While the explicit exclusion of 'common-law irrationality' closes one avenue, it cannot pre-emptively define every possible error of law out of existence. The definitions themselves become the new battleground.

*What would close it:* Statutory language that could exhaustively list every possible ministerial action and categorise it as 'merits', while also defining every possible error of law as falling within that category. This is practically impossible.

### 3. A court could find that a decision was reached in a procedurally unfair manner, and that such a breach of natural justice is a fundamental error that nullifies the decision, irrespective of whether the substance of the decision falls within the statutory definition of 'merits'.

**Mechanism:** `PROCEDURAL_SURVIVAL`

**Rests on:** Common law principles of natural justice (procedural fairness), and the precedent set by the ouster clause in the Judicial Review and Courts Act 2022, which explicitly preserves review for 'fundamental breach of the principles of natural justice'.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan's silence on procedural fairness. The actions focus on defining the substantive grounds of review ('merits' and 'legality') and excluding irrationality, but do not mention or attempt to oust review for procedural impropriety.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan does not address this ground of review at all. The drafter has focused on substantive merits and overlooked the procedural dimension. The 'statement of reasons' and 'pre-decision review' actions might improve procedure in some cases, but they do not legislate against a challenge if that procedure is flawed or unfair.

*What would close it:* An explicit and unambiguous statutory provision stating that decisions under the Act are not reviewable on grounds of procedural unfairness or breach of natural justice. This would be a constitutionally radical step.

### 4. A court could find that the statutory definitions of 'merits' and 'legality' are incompatible with rights under the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR), as incorporated by the Human Rights Act 1998 (HRA), and either 'read down' the definitions to be compatible or issue a declaration of incompatibility.

**Mechanism:** `ALTERNATIVE_SOURCE`

**Rests on:** Sections 3 and 4 of the Human Rights Act 1998. A challenge would likely focus on Article 6 (right to a fair hearing) or Article 13 (right to an effective remedy).

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan's failure to mention the Human Rights Act. The proposal aims to limit common law judicial review but does not address the parallel and distinct statutory jurisdiction under the HRA.

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan is entirely focused on the common law supervisory jurisdiction and does not contain any provision to amend, disapply, or otherwise affect the operation of the Human Rights Act 1998. Therefore, the HRA provides a completely separate, untouched legal framework for challenging the lawfulness of ministerial action.

*What would close it:* Explicit provisions in the primary legislation disapplying sections 3 and 4 of the Human Rights Act 1998 in relation to this specific power, which would be highly controversial and politically difficult.
