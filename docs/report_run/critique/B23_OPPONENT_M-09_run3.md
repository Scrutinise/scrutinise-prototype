### 1. The effect of a Gender Recognition Certificate (GRC) under the Gender Recognition Act 2004 (GRA) survives the new guidance, as statutory guidance cannot override primary legislation.

**Mechanism:** `ENTRENCHED_ELSEWHERE`

**Rests on:** Gender Recognition Act 2004, s.9(1), and the principle of parliamentary sovereignty/statutory hierarchy.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan's core action is to "issue statutory guidance clarifying that 'sex' means biological sex for the purposes of the Act".

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The plan explicitly relies on statutory guidance, a form of secondary instrument, to interpret the Equality Act 2010. This guidance cannot amend or repeal the explicit provision in s.9(1) of the GRA 2004 that a person's gender becomes the acquired gender 'for all purposes'. An opponent will argue that the right conferred by the GRA is a statutory one that remains in full force unless and until it is amended by further primary legislation. The guidance represents the Minister's interpretation, not a change in the law itself.

*What would close it:* Primary legislation that explicitly amends s.9(1) of the GRA 2004, or which states that the definition of 'sex' in the Equality Act 2010 operates notwithstanding the effect of the GRA 2004. This is what the proposal's abandoned 'ACTIONS' section described.

### 2. The guidance is challenged via judicial review as being ultra vires, irrational, or misstating the law established by the interaction of the two statutes.

**Mechanism:** `PROCEDURAL_SURVIVAL`

**Rests on:** Common law principles of judicial review (illegality, irrationality, procedural impropriety) and the Human Rights Act 1998.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan's first step is for the Minister to "issue statutory guidance".

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — The act of issuing statutory guidance is a reviewable public law decision. Opponents will seek judicial review of the guidance itself before it is even applied, arguing that it misrepresents the complex interaction between the GRA 2004 and the Equality Act 2010, even in light of the fictional Supreme Court judgment. They will argue it is an unlawful attempt to amend primary legislation by the back door and is incompatible with Article 8 ECHR rights.

*What would close it:* An ouster clause in primary legislation seeking to prevent judicial review of the guidance, though even this would be subject to challenge.

### 3. In specific discrimination claims, tribunals and courts read down the guidance or disapply it to avoid incompatibility with the Human Rights Act 1998.

**Mechanism:** `DISAPPLICATION`

**Rests on:** Human Rights Act 1998, s.3 (duty to read legislation compatibly with ECHR) and s.6 (duty on public authorities, including courts, to act compatibly). ECHR, Article 8 (right to private life) and Article 14 (non-discrimination).

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The plan relies on the EHRC using its "enforcement powers to investigate and compel compliance".

**⚠ **the plan does NOT close it**** — Enforcement action by the EHRC, or reliance on the guidance by a service provider, will be tested in individual legal cases. In a specific case, a court or tribunal (as a public authority) is obliged under the HRA to interpret the law compatibly with Convention rights. A litigant with a GRC will argue that applying the guidance to them constitutes a disproportionate interference with their Article 8 right to private life. A court could agree and 'read down' the guidance, finding that in this specific instance, it should not be followed. The plan cannot prevent this case-by-case adjudication.

*What would close it:* Derogation from the ECHR or amendment to the Human Rights Act 1998, both of which are far beyond the scope of this proposal.

### 4. The proposal's reliance on the fictional Supreme Court ruling creates a new common law protection for biological sex, which is then used to argue for protections that go beyond the Equality Act's framework, making the situation more complex.

**Mechanism:** `OTHER` — Unintended common law development

**Rests on:** The (fictional) Supreme Court judgment in *For Women Scotland Ltd v The Scottish Ministers* [2025] UKSC 16.

**The opening, in the proposal's own words:**

> The entire diagnosis rests on the idea that "The Supreme Court's ruling... has resolved the legal ambiguity, confirming 'sex' in the Equality Act 2010 means biological sex."

**⚠⚠ **the plan makes it worse**** — By basing the entire strategy on a definitive Supreme Court ruling, the proposal elevates 'biological sex' to a judicially recognised, fundamental concept for statutory interpretation. Opponents can seize on this. If 'biological sex' is now the clear, unambiguous meaning, this may open the door to arguments for sex-based rights and protections in areas *not* covered by the Equality Act's exceptions, creating new and unforeseen legal battlegrounds. The proposal aims for clarity but instead provides a powerful new common law tool for all sides, potentially increasing litigation and making outcomes less predictable.
