# Part 2 §4.6 — Four mechanisms for one problem

**Draft 1, 1 Sep 2026, CCW.** Sources held in full and read directly; nothing here comes from a
summary of a summary.

---

## Why this section is the centre of Part 2

Within about a year, four documents on the same side of the argument have proposed **four different
mechanisms** for the same problem. They agree on the diagnosis to a striking degree and disagree on
the remedy completely.

**Nobody appears to have put that in front of the proposers.** Setting it out — and adjudicating
none of it — is the most useful thing this report does, because the choice between these four is a
decision only they can take, and it changes everything downstream.

| | Who | Mechanism | Sequencing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Starkey** (the thesis) | **Annul** — declare the provisions never to have had effect | One Act, all at once; sequential change is defeated by the remainder |
| **2** | **Prosperity Institute** (Braverman & Dampier, Sept 2025) | **Case Law Review Commission** under a sunset clause, retaining some Strasbourg case law; annual Correction Bills | Two Bills, six-month transition |
| **3** | **Wolfson KC** (advice to the Leader, Oct 2025) | **Structured Article 58 withdrawal** plus enabling legislation; **warns against a Bill of Rights modelled on the Convention** | Six months' notice |
| **4** | **Sumption** (Spectator, 2026) | **Leave the jurisdiction, keep the substance** — replace it with a domestic instrument that "would look very much the same as the convention, but which would not be subject to the jurisdiction of the Strasbourg Court" | Not specified |

---

## ⚠⚠ The finding: two of them recommend opposite things

**Wolfson warns against a Bill of Rights modelled on the Convention. Sumption proposes exactly
that.**

A former Justice of the Supreme Court and the Shadow Attorney General, writing within months of each
other, both persuaded that the present position is untenable, reach **opposite conclusions about
the replacement**. That is not a detail. It is the central unresolved question of the entire
programme, and it is invisible unless the four documents are read side by side.

**What turns on it:** if Sumption is right, withdrawal is a change of *forum* — the substantive
rights survive in domestic form and the argument is about who interprets them. If Wolfson is right,
a Convention-shaped domestic instrument reimports the problem, and withdrawal must therefore also be
a change of *substance*. **The two produce entirely different Bills**, and a programme that has not
chosen between them cannot be drafted at all.

---

## Sumption's diagnosis, and why it matters more than the others

Sumption's objection is precise, and it is **not to the Convention's text**. He says he has no
problem with the text at all. His objection is to the Strasbourg court's "living instrument"
doctrine, operated since the 1970s, which he describes as expanding the text by extrapolation and
analogy to produce a large number of rights, many freestanding. He calls this **"an essentially
legislative process"** involving **"a usurpation of the function of democratic legislatures."**

⚠ **And this sentence corroborates the absorption claim from the highest possible source:**

> "We do not need the Human Rights Convention in order to protect human rights."
> — Lord Sumption, former Justice of the Supreme Court

That is §4.2's proposition, stated by a man who sat on the court the programme proposes to abolish.

**But note what he does with it, because it is the opposite of what the thesis does.** For Sumption
the conclusion is that the rights can safely be re-domesticated — the protection does not depend on
Strasbourg. For the thesis, the conclusion is that the rights have taken root and must be uprooted.
**Same premise, opposite remedy.** The premise is not in dispute; what to do about it entirely is.

---

## The counter-position, stated fairly

The report sets out opposing views rather than only the favourable ones, and the strongest available
counter-position comes from **Dominic Grieve KC**, former Attorney General, in the same broadcast.

He accepts the starting point — that if the UK withdrew but retained the Human Rights Act as a
domestic bill of rights, rights here **"would continue to be pretty well protected."** ⚠ That is a
significant concession and it should be printed, because it means the domestic-protection argument
is common ground across all five figures in this section.

His objection is that domestic protection was never the point: the Convention was joined to raise
standards elsewhere in Europe and to demonstrate willingness to be bound, and that leaving carries
consequences he lists specifically:

- **The UK–EU Trade and Cooperation Agreement** would be jeopardised
- **Horizon participation** would become impossible, because the data-sharing it requires is
  underpinned post-Brexit by shared Convention adherence
- **Northern Ireland**, where he argues Convention adherence is explicit and implicit in the
  Belfast/Good Friday Agreement
- On frequency, he says that since the **Brighton Declaration of 2012** — which he negotiated with
  Ken Clarke — adverse judgments breaking new ground have been very rare, on his recollection two in
  the last year

⚠ **That last point is checkable and should be checked**, because it is an empirical claim doing
real work: if adverse judgments are as rare as he says, the practical case for withdrawal rests on
their severity rather than their number. It is in CCW-B12 as a retrieval task.

**Note also a fifth configuration hiding in his answer:** withdraw from the Convention but keep the
Human Rights Act as a purely domestic instrument. Nobody in the four proposes it, and it is closer
to Sumption's position than to any of the others.

## What this section hands back

**Five questions, and only the proposers can answer them:**

9. Which of the four mechanisms is intended? They are not variants of one plan; they produce
   different Bills, different timetables and different residual rights.
10. Is the replacement Convention-shaped (Sumption) or deliberately not (Wolfson)? This is the single
    most consequential drafting decision in the programme and the two most senior legal voices
    disagree about it.
11. If the rights are re-domesticated, what has been achieved beyond a change of forum — and is a
    change of forum the actual objective?
12. The Prosperity Institute's Case Law Review Commission is modelled on the Retained EU Law Act's
    sunset clause. ⚠ That sunset was abandoned before Royal Assent. Is the model still the right one,
    knowing that?
13. Grieve's consequences — the TCA, Horizon, Northern Ireland — are stated as facts about
    instruments. Which are accepted, and which are contested?

## Two balance points recorded without editorialising

- **Wolfson told the House of Lords in 2023 that he supported UK membership of the Convention.** His
  advice of October 2025 reaches a different conclusion. ⚠ *To be verified against Hansard, not
  against a secondary characterisation, before it prints.*
- **There is an internal Conservative counter-position** from the Conservative European Forum, whose
  president is Sir David Lidington. The CEF reports that Wolfson said his advice should be
  reconsidered if meaningful reform of the Convention system were achieved. ⚠ *That is the CEF's
  characterisation. It must be verified against the advice itself; if it holds, it is a significant
  qualification the other three documents do not carry.*
